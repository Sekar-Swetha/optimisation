\begin{abstract}
This report presents a comprehensive comparative study of optimisation algorithms applied to three benchmark problems of increasing difficulty: a convex quadratic linear regression loss (Benchmark~A, $\kappa \approx 1$), a mildly nonlinear toy neural network loss (Benchmark~B, $\kappa \approx 3$--$10$), and the highly ill-conditioned Rosenbrock function (Benchmark~C, $\kappa \approx 2000$). Six categories of methods are investigated: (1)~adaptive step-size methods --- Polyak step, Adagrad, RMSprop, and Heavy Ball; (2)~momentum and stochastic methods --- Nesterov acceleration, Adam, and mini-batch SGD; (3)~Newton's method with second-order local approximation and quasi-Newton extensions; (4)~derivative-free approaches --- finite differences, Nesterov random search, Nelder--Mead simplex, and grid search; (5)~constrained optimisation via projected gradient descent, penalty methods, and augmented Lagrangian; and (6)~the Frank--Wolfe conditional gradient algorithm for linear-constrained problems.

Key theoretical and empirical findings are reported: the Polyak step diverges catastrophically when $f^{\star}$ is misspecified (step size $\to \infty$ as $\|\nabla f\| \to 0$, bounded only by $\varepsilon = 10^{-4}$), yet outperforms all other methods on Rosenbrock where $f^{\star} = 0$ exactly; Newton's method converges in a single iteration on the quadratic benchmark (provably exact for quadratics), with damped Newton ($\alpha = 0.22$) achieving 5$\times$ better objective value than GD on Rosenbrock in $25\%$ as many iterations; Nesterov acceleration achieves a 7$\times$ objective improvement over GD on Rosenbrock, consistent with its optimal $O(1/k^2)$ rate (vs.\ GD's $O(1/k)$); Nelder--Mead without any gradient information achieves $f \approx 10^{-3}$ on Rosenbrock in 160 iterations ($\sim$480 function evaluations), outperforming gradient-based GD that requires hundreds more iterations; and projected gradient descent enforces hard feasibility from the first iteration, while the Frank--Wolfe method maintains feasibility by construction via convex combinations. Results are connected throughout to Lipschitz smoothness, strong convexity, condition number theory, KKT optimality conditions, and Taylor approximation theory.
\end{abstract}

\section{Benchmark Problems and Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:benchmarks}
% ============================================================

All experiments use a fixed random seed (\texttt{np.random.seed(42)}) for reproducibility. We define three benchmark objective functions of increasing difficulty, characterised by their curvature and conditioning.

\subsection{Benchmark A: Linear Regression Quadratic Loss}

A synthetic dataset with $m = 1000$ samples and two parameters $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^2$ is generated:
\begin{equation}
\theta^{\star} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad x^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_2), \quad y^{(i)} = {\theta^{\star}}^T x^{(i)} + \varepsilon^{(i)}, \quad \varepsilon^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1).
\label{eq:benchmark_A_data}
\end{equation}
The mean-squared-error loss is:
\begin{equation}
J(\theta) = \frac{1}{2m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \bigl(\theta^T x^{(i)} - y^{(i)}\bigr)^2 = \frac{1}{2m}\|X\theta - y\|^2.
\label{eq:loss_A}
\end{equation}
The gradient and Hessian are:
\begin{equation}
\nabla J(\theta) = \frac{1}{m}X^T(X\theta - y), \qquad H_A = \nabla^2 J = \frac{1}{m}X^T X.
\end{equation}
Since $H_A$ is constant and positive semi-definite, $J$ is a \emph{convex quadratic}. By the law of large numbers, $H_A \approx \mathbb{E}[x^{(i)}{x^{(i)}}^T] = I_2$ for large $m$, giving condition number $\kappa(H_A) \approx 1$ and Lipschitz constant $L_A \approx 1$. The observation noise causes the minimum loss to be $J^{\star} = \frac{\sigma^2}{2}(1 - \frac{d}{m}) \approx 0.4826$ (not zero). Starting point: $\theta_0 = [0, 0]^T$.

\subsection{Benchmark B: Toy Neural Network Loss}
\begin{equation}
f(x_1, x_2) = (x_1 - 1)^2 + 5(x_2 - 2)^2 + \sin(x_1).
\label{eq:loss_B}
\end{equation}
The gradient and Hessian are:
\begin{align}
\nabla f(x) &= \bigl[2(x_1 - 1) + \cos(x_1),\; 10(x_2 - 2)\bigr]^T, \\
H_B(x) &= \operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(2 - \sin(x_1),\; 10\bigr).
\end{align}
The Hessian is diagonal, so eigenvalues are $\lambda_1(x_1) = 2 - \sin(x_1) \in [1, 3]$ and $\lambda_2 = 10$. The condition number thus varies: $\kappa(H_B) \in [10/3, 10] \approx [3.3, 10]$, creating moderate anisotropy. The sinusoidal term is non-quadratic but bounded, so the function remains smooth and strictly convex. The unconstrained minimum satisfies $10(x_2-2) = 0 \Rightarrow x_2^{\star} = 2$ and $2(x_1^{\star}-1)+\cos(x_1^{\star})=0$, yielding $x_1^{\star} \approx 0.582$ (by bisection), with $f^{\star} \approx 0.724$. Starting point: $x_0 = [-1, 4]^T$.

\subsection{Benchmark C: Rosenbrock Function}

The Rosenbrock function, introduced by Howard H. Rosenbrock in 1960 as a test for optimisation algorithms, is the canonical benchmark for ill-conditioned nonlinear optimisation. Its characteristic ``banana-shaped'' valley makes it challenging for gradient-based methods: the valley floor is nearly flat (small gradient) while the walls are steep (large gradient), creating a severe curvature anisotropy.
\begin{equation}
f(x_1, x_2) = (1 - x_1)^2 + 100(x_2 - x_1^2)^2.
\label{eq:loss_C}
\end{equation}
The global minimum is at $(1, 1)$ with $f^{\star} = 0$. The Hessian is:
\begin{equation}
H_C(x) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 + 1200x_1^2 - 400x_2 & -400x_1 \\ -400x_1 & 200 \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
At the optimum $(1,1)$: $H_C(1,1) = \begin{bmatrix} 802 & -400 \\ -400 & 200 \end{bmatrix}$, with eigenvalues $\lambda_{\min} \approx 0.5$ and $\lambda_{\max} \approx 1002$, giving $\kappa(H_C) \approx 2000$. This extreme ill-conditioning, combined with the narrow curved valley along $x_2 = x_1^2$, makes Rosenbrock the canonical hard test for first-order methods: the GD step size must satisfy $\alpha < 2/\lambda_{\max}(H_C) \approx 0.002$ everywhere along the path, yet the valley floor gradient in the $x_1$ direction is $O((1-x_1))$, which is tiny near the optimum. This combination forces GD to take thousands of tiny steps along a flat, curved manifold, explaining the poor performance ($f = 3.5$ after 120 iterations). Starting point: $x_0 = [-1, 1]^T$.

\subsection{Theoretical Background}
\label{sec:q1_theory}

For a differentiable function $f : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$, the convergence guarantees of first-order methods depend critically on two structural properties:

\begin{definition}[Convexity]
$f$ is \emph{convex} if $f(\lambda x + (1-\lambda)y) \leq \lambda f(x) + (1-\lambda)f(y)$ for all $x, y$ and $\lambda \in [0,1]$. Equivalently, $\nabla^2 f(x) \succeq 0$ everywhere. All three benchmarks are (locally) convex near their minima: Benchmark~A is globally convex quadratic; Benchmark~B is strictly convex; Benchmark~C is non-convex globally but strictly convex near $(1,1)$.
\end{definition}

Gradient descent with constant step size $\alpha$ guarantees convergence when $f$ is $L$-smooth:
\begin{definition}[$L$-smoothness]
$f$ is $L$-smooth if $\|\nabla f(x) - \nabla f(y)\| \leq L\|x - y\|$ for all $x, y$, or equivalently if $\nabla^2 f(x) \preceq L I$ everywhere.
\end{definition}
Under $L$-smoothness, the update $x_{k+1} = x_k - \alpha \nabla f(x_k)$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}
f(x_{k+1}) \leq f(x_k) - \alpha\!\left(1 - \frac{\alpha L}{2}\right)\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2,
\end{equation}
guaranteeing descent for any $\alpha < 2/L$. The optimal constant step is $\alpha^{\star} = 1/L$. In practice, $L$ is unknown, motivating \emph{adaptive} step-size methods that infer curvature from gradient observations.

\begin{definition}[$\mu$-Strong Convexity]
\label{def:strong_convex}
$f$ is $\mu$-strongly convex (with $\mu > 0$) if for all $x, y \in \mathbb{R}^d$:
\begin{equation}
f(y) \geq f(x) + \nabla f(x)^T(y - x) + \frac{\mu}{2}\|y - x\|^2,
\end{equation}
equivalently $\nabla^2 f(x) \succeq \mu I$ for all $x$. Strong convexity implies a unique global minimum $x^{\star}$ and a quadratic lower bound on the objective: $f(x) - f^{\star} \geq \frac{\mu}{2}\|x - x^{\star}\|^2$, bounding how far from the minimum we can be given a sub-optimality gap. Benchmark~A satisfies $\mu_A = \lambda_{\min}(H_A) \approx 1$ (by the law of large numbers). Benchmark~B satisfies $\mu_B = \min_x \lambda_{\min}(H_B(x)) = 1$ (the minimum of $2 - \sin(x_1)$). Rosenbrock~C is not strongly convex globally but is locally strongly convex near $(1,1)$ with $\mu_C \approx 0.5$.
\end{definition}

If additionally $f$ is $\mu$-strongly convex ($\nabla^2 f \succeq \mu I > 0$), GD achieves linear convergence:
\begin{equation}
f(x_k) - f^{\star} \leq \left(1 - \frac{\mu}{L}\right)^k (f(x_0) - f^{\star}) = \left(1 - \frac{1}{\kappa}\right)^k (f(x_0) - f^{\star}),
\end{equation}
where $\kappa = L/\mu$ is the condition number. Large $\kappa$ (e.g., Benchmark~C, $\kappa \approx 2000$, as characterised in Table~\ref{tab:benchmark_difficulty}) forces very small constant step sizes and extremely slow convergence, motivating preconditioning and adaptive methods. Figure~\ref{fig:convergence_rates} illustrates the theoretical rates side-by-side.

\subsection{Algorithm Formulations}

Four adaptive methods are compared against a constant step-size gradient descent (GD) baseline across all three benchmarks for 120 iterations.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Polyak Step Size}
\label{alg:polyak}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, optimal value estimate $f^{\star}$, tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $\alpha_k \gets \dfrac{f(x_k) - f^{\star}}{\|g_k\|^2 + \varepsilon}$ \Comment{Normalised gap}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha_k\, g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

When $f^{\star}$ is the true minimum and $f$ is convex, the Polyak step minimises the distance to any sub-level set in one step. Specifically, for a $\mu$-strongly convex function: $\|x_{k+1} - x^{\star}\|^2 \leq \|x_k - x^{\star}\|^2 - \frac{(f(x_k)-f^{\star})^2}{\|g_k\|^2}$, which is always non-increasing. This contrasts with the Armijo backtracking line search, which does not require knowledge of $f^{\star}$ but instead selects the largest $\alpha_k$ satisfying $f(x_k - \alpha_k g_k) \leq f(x_k) - c\alpha_k\|g_k\|^2$ for some $c \in (0, 1)$. The Polyak step is optimal for convex functions with known $f^{\star}$, while Armijo line search is robust and adaptive but may require multiple function evaluations per iteration.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Adagrad}
\label{alg:adagrad}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, base learning rate $\alpha_0$, tolerance $\varepsilon$, iterations $N$
\State $G_0 \gets \mathbf{0} \in \mathbb{R}^d$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $G_{k+1} \gets G_k + g_k \odot g_k$ \Comment{Per-coordinate cumulative squared gradient}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \dfrac{\alpha_0}{\sqrt{G_{k+1}} + \varepsilon} \odot g_k$ \Comment{Per-coordinate effective rate decays as $O(1/\sqrt{k})$}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Adagrad pre-conditions the gradient by the square root of cumulative squared gradients, giving coordinates with historically large gradients a smaller effective step. This is particularly beneficial for sparse gradients. However, since $G_k$ is non-decreasing, the effective learning rate monotonically decays to zero, which can prematurely halt convergence on dense, non-stationary problems. Figure~\ref{fig:ada_vs_rms} illustrates this decay compared to RMSprop's recovery.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{RMSprop}
\label{alg:rmsprop}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, learning rate $\alpha_0$, exponential decay $\beta \in (0,1)$, tolerance $\varepsilon$, iterations $N$
\State $v_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $v_{k+1} \gets \beta\, v_k + (1 - \beta)\, g_k \odot g_k$ \Comment{Exponential moving average of $g^2$}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \dfrac{\alpha_0}{\sqrt{v_{k+1}} + \varepsilon} \odot g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

RMSprop addresses Adagrad's monotonic decay by replacing the cumulative sum with an exponential moving average controlled by $\beta = 0.9$. This ``forgets'' old gradient magnitudes with weight $(1-\beta)\beta^j$ for gradient $j$ steps ago, adapting to recent curvature rather than the entire history.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/adagrad_vs_rmsprop_stepsize.pdf}
\caption{Illustration of Adagrad vs.\ RMSprop effective step size evolution in a scenario where gradient magnitudes decrease as $\|\nabla f(x_k)\| \sim 10/\sqrt{k+1}$ (typical near convergence). Left: Adagrad's step decays monotonically to zero (as $O(1/\sqrt{k})$), while RMSprop maintains a non-trivial effective step by forgetting old gradients. Right: the ratio of RMSprop to Adagrad effective steps grows unboundedly as Adagrad's denominator accumulates while RMSprop's denominator reflects only recent gradient magnitudes. This is why RMSprop remains adaptive to current curvature while Adagrad becomes effectively frozen.}
\label{fig:ada_vs_rms}
\end{figure}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Heavy Ball (Polyak Momentum)}
\label{alg:heavyball}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, momentum $\beta \in [0,1)$, iterations $N$
\State $z_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets \beta\, z_k + \alpha\, \nabla f(x_k)$ \Comment{Exponentially weighted gradient accumulator}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - z_{k+1}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The Heavy Ball method adds a momentum term $\beta z_k$ that accumulates past gradients, damping oscillations across the narrow valley of ill-conditioned problems while amplifying progress in consistent directions.

\textbf{Polyak divergence mechanism.} The failure on Benchmarks~A and~B can be formalised as follows. Let $\delta = f^{\star}_{\text{true}} - f^{\star}_{\text{specified}} > 0$ be the misspecification error. As $x_k \to x^{\star}$, one has $f(x_k) \to f^{\star}_{\text{true}} = \delta + f^{\star}_{\text{specified}}$, so $\alpha_k \to \delta / \|g_k\|^2$. Since $\|g_k\| \to 0$ for a smooth convex function at its minimum, the step size $\alpha_k \to \infty$, causing the iterate to overshoot and diverge. The regulariser $\varepsilon$ bounds $\alpha_k \leq \delta/\varepsilon$ but for $\varepsilon = 10^{-4}$ and $\delta \approx 0.48$, this bound is $\sim$4800 --- far larger than any stable step size.

\subsection{Comparative Analysis}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Polyak:} Theoretically optimal when $f^{\star}$ is known; practically fragile to misspecification. Achieves convergence in $O(1/k)$ steps for strongly convex problems with correct $f^{\star}$.

\item \textbf{Adagrad:} Reliable and parameter-robust across all benchmarks. Its per-coordinate scaling effectively handles the anisotropy of Benchmark~B. The monotone step decay is a fundamental limitation: once gradients are large in any coordinate, that coordinate's step size never recovers, causing premature convergence on non-stationary or strongly non-quadratic landscapes.

\item \textbf{RMSprop:} Improves on Adagrad by using exponential averaging, which can adaptively increase the effective step if recent gradients are small. Converges smoothly but achieved slightly worse final accuracy on Benchmark~A ($f = 0.5027$ vs.\ noise floor $0.4826$). This discrepancy arises because the exponential moving average $v_k$ never fully vanishes: even after reaching the minimum, $v_k$ retains a non-negligible memory of past gradients (decaying with rate $\beta^j$ for gradient $j$ steps ago). The resulting effective step $\alpha_0/(\sqrt{v_k}+\varepsilon)$ oscillates rather than converging to zero, causing RMSprop to orbit the minimum rather than settle at it. This is a known limitation of constant-rate RMSprop, and is addressed in practice by using a decaying base rate $\alpha_0(k) \propto 1/\sqrt{k}$.

\item \textbf{Heavy Ball:} Competitive with GD baseline on well-conditioned problems and outperforms all methods (except Polyak) on Rosenbrock. Its momentum accumulation along the valley direction ($x_1$) provides the key advantage. However, Heavy Ball does not have a general $O(1/k^2)$ guarantee on non-quadratic problems and can oscillate on curved surfaces.

\item \textbf{Practical recommendation:} When $f^{\star}$ is unavailable (the typical case), RMSprop and Heavy Ball are the most practical choices from this set. Heavy Ball is preferred on ill-conditioned problems; RMSprop is preferred when per-coordinate scaling is needed.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Connection to Second-Order Methods}

The per-coordinate adaptive methods (Adagrad, RMSprop) can be viewed as performing \emph{diagonal preconditioning}: replacing the gradient step $x \gets x - \alpha g$ with $x \gets x - D^{-1}g$ where $D = \operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{G_k} + \varepsilon)$. This is equivalent to applying a diagonal approximation of the Hessian inverse: $D^{-1} \approx [H^{-1}]_{\text{diag}}$. For Benchmark~B with diagonal Hessian $H_B = \operatorname{diag}(1.45, 10)$, a perfect diagonal preconditioner would give $D = \operatorname{diag}(1.45, 10)$, eliminating the condition number and enabling unit-step convergence.

In practice, Adagrad's diagonal estimate $G_k^{1/2}$ tracks the curvature \emph{direction} (large gradients = high curvature) but not the exact magnitude. This partial curvature information explains why Adagrad/RMSprop substantially outperform plain GD on anisotropic problems but fall short of full quasi-Newton methods. Adam adds momentum to this diagonal preconditioner, combining two orthogonal improvements: curvature correction (from $v_t$) and gradient smoothing (from $m_t$).

\textbf{Justification via the secular equation.} For a diagonal Hessian $H = \operatorname{diag}(h_1, \ldots, h_d)$, the Newton step is $p_k = -H^{-1}g_k = (-g_{k,1}/h_1, \ldots, -g_{k,d}/h_d)^T$. If we identify the Adagrad preconditioner $D_k = \operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{G_{k,i}} + \varepsilon)$ with $H^{1/2}$, then the Adagrad step $-D_k^{-1}g_k$ approximates $-H^{-1/2}g_k$, which is a \emph{half-Newton step} rather than a full Newton step. This explains why Adagrad converges faster than GD (it partially corrects for curvature) but slower than Newton (it only partially inverts the Hessian). For the squared gradient to be a good curvature proxy, we need $g_{k,i}^2 \approx h_i^2 x_{k,i}^2$ (large curvature $\Leftrightarrow$ large gradient), which holds near the optimum for quadratic objectives but degrades for highly nonlinear functions like Rosenbrock where curvature changes rapidly along the trajectory.

\subsection{Predicted vs.\ Observed Convergence Rates}

For the quadratic Benchmark~A with condition number $\kappa \approx 1$, GD converges at rate $(1-1/\kappa)^k \approx 0^k$, meaning one step suffices in theory. In practice, the noise floor $J^{\star} \approx 0.4826$ is reached in $\sim$30 iterations --- the discrepancy arises because the effective condition number $(X^TX/m)$ of a finite sample is not exactly~1. The step size $\alpha = 0.08 < 1/L \approx 1/\lambda_{\max}(H_A)$ ensures stability.

For Benchmark~B with $\kappa \approx 7$ (evaluating $H_B$ at the starting point), GD's linear rate predicts $(1 - 1/7)^k \approx 0.857^k$. At $k = 120$ iterations: $(0.857)^{120} \approx 5 \times 10^{-9}$, predicting convergence to machine precision, which is consistent with the observed $f = 0.7244 \approx f^{\star}$.

For Benchmark~C with $\kappa \approx 2000$ (near the optimum), GD's linear rate gives $(1-1/2000)^k$. To halve the objective gap requires $k \approx 2000\ln 2 \approx 1386$ iterations --- far more than the 120 used here, explaining why GD barely progresses. The adaptive methods in Q1 and Nesterov in Q2 all provide superior handling of this ill-conditioning.

\subsection{Theoretical Background}

Standard gradient descent on a convex $L$-smooth function achieves $O(1/k)$ convergence: $f(x_k) - f^{\star} \leq \frac{L\|x_0-x^{\star}\|^2}{2k}$. Nesterov (1983) showed this rate is optimal for first-order methods on general convex functions. Nesterov acceleration achieves the improved rate $O(1/k^2)$:
\begin{theorem}[Nesterov Acceleration {[1]}]
\label{thm:nesterov}
For a convex $L$-smooth function $f$ and the Nesterov accelerated gradient method with step size $\alpha = 1/L$:
\begin{equation}
f(x_k) - f^{\star} \leq \frac{2L\|x_0 - x^{\star}\|^2}{(k+1)^2}.
\end{equation}
This rate is \emph{optimal} among all first-order methods for smooth convex optimisation.
\end{theorem}
The key mechanism is the \emph{lookahead} point: evaluating the gradient at a point that lies ahead of the current iterate in the momentum direction allows the method to ``correct'' its trajectory before overshooting.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth]{figures/convergence_rate_theory.pdf}
\caption{Theoretical convergence rate comparison. GD on a general convex function achieves $O(1/k)$ (blue dashed). GD on a strongly convex function with $\kappa = 7$ achieves linear convergence $(1-1/\kappa)^k$ (black solid). Nesterov acceleration achieves $O(1/k^2)$ (red), providing a quadratic speedup in $k$ over the basic convex rate. Newton's method with $\rho = 0.5$ achieves quadratic convergence $\rho^{2^k}$ (green), reaching machine precision in $\sim$10 iterations. Note the logarithmic $y$-axis: all non-Newton methods require 120+ iterations to match Newton's 12-iteration accuracy.}
\label{fig:convergence_rates}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Condition number and iteration complexity.} For strongly convex, $L$-smooth $f$, GD with step size $\alpha = 1/L$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}
f(x_k) - f^{\star} \leq \left(1 - \frac{1}{\kappa}\right)^k (f(x_0) - f^{\star}), \quad \kappa = \frac{L}{\mu}.
\label{eq:gd_linear}
\end{equation}
To reach $f(x_k) - f^{\star} \leq \varepsilon$, GD requires $k \geq \kappa \ln(1/\varepsilon)$ iterations: the complexity scales \emph{linearly} in $\kappa$. Nesterov acceleration reduces this to $k \geq \sqrt{\kappa} \ln(1/\varepsilon)$ iterations: the complexity scales as $\sqrt{\kappa}$, giving a quadratic speedup for ill-conditioned problems.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/kappa_convergence.pdf}

Higher condition number $\kappa$ leads to slower convergence: for $\kappa = 2000$ (Rosenbrock), GD requires $\sim$14{,}000 iterations to reach $10^{-6}$ accuracy. Right: iteration complexity (iterations to reach $\varepsilon = 10^{-6}$) as a function of $\kappa$ on a log-log scale. GD complexity grows as $O(\kappa)$ (blue), while Nesterov grows as $O(\sqrt{\kappa})$ (red dashed). \subsection{Algorithm Formulations}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Nesterov Accelerated Gradient Descent}
\label{alg:nesterov}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, max momentum $\beta_{\max}$, iterations $N$
\State $z_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 1, 2, \ldots, N$}
    \State $\beta_k \gets \min\!\left(\dfrac{k-1}{k+2},\ \beta_{\max}\right)$ \Comment{Momentum schedule (approximately $1-3/k$)}
    \State $\tilde{x}_k \gets x_k + \beta_k\, z_k$ \Comment{Lookahead point}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets \beta_k\, z_k - \alpha\, \nabla f(\tilde{x}_k)$ \Comment{Velocity update at lookahead}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k + z_{k+1}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The distinction from Heavy Ball (Algorithm~\ref{alg:heavyball}) is subtle but critical: Heavy Ball evaluates $\nabla f(x_k)$, while Nesterov evaluates $\nabla f(x_k + \beta_k z_k)$. This lookahead allows partial correction of the momentum direction, restoring convergence guarantees that Heavy Ball lacks on non-quadratic functions. The schedule $\beta_k = \min((k-1)/(k+2), \beta_{\max})$ increases monotonically from 0 towards $\min(1, \beta_{\max})$, gradually building up momentum while remaining conservative in early iterations. The cap $\beta_{\max}$ prevents excessive momentum on non-convex problems. Without it, $\beta_k \to 1$ as $k \to \infty$, causing oscillations on curved landscapes like Benchmark~C. The cap activates at iteration $k^{\star}$ where $(k^{\star}-1)/(k^{\star}+2) = \beta_{\max}$, giving $k^{\star} = (3\beta_{\max}+2)/(1-\beta_{\max})$. For $\beta_{\max} = 0.90$: $k^{\star} = 4.7/0.1 = 47$, so after 47 iterations the method runs with constant momentum 0.90. For $\beta_{\max} = 0.92$ (Benchmark~B): $k^{\star} = 4.76/0.08 \approx 60$ iterations.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Adam Optimiser}
\label{alg:adam}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, learning rate $\alpha$, moments $\beta_1, \beta_2 \in (0,1)$, $\varepsilon > 0$, iterations $N$
\State $m_0 \gets \mathbf{0},\quad v_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$t = 1, 2, \ldots, N$}
    \State $g_t \gets \nabla f(x_t)$
    \State $m_t \gets \beta_1\, m_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_1)\, g_t$ \Comment{First moment: $\hat{\mu} \approx \mathbb{E}[g]$}
    \State $v_t \gets \beta_2\, v_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_2)\, g_t \odot g_t$ \Comment{Second moment: $\hat{\sigma}^2 \approx \mathbb{E}[g^2]$}
    \State $\hat{m}_t \gets m_t / (1 - \beta_1^t)$ \Comment{Bias correction: accounts for zero initialisation}
    \State $\hat{v}_t \gets v_t / (1 - \beta_2^t)$
    \State $x_{t+1} \gets x_t - \alpha\, \hat{m}_t / (\sqrt{\hat{v}_t} + \varepsilon)$ \Comment{Signal-to-noise normalised update}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Adam combines momentum (via $m_t$, analogous to RMSprop's gradient averaging) with per-coordinate adaptive scaling (via $v_t$, analogous to Adagrad). The bias correction is essential in early iterations: at $t=1$, $m_1 = (1-\beta_1)g_1$ is biased towards zero; dividing by $(1-\beta_1)$ recovers an unbiased estimate of the true gradient. Without correction, early steps would be artificially small. Adam can be viewed as a generalisation of RMSprop with momentum: setting $\beta_1 = 0$ reduces Adam exactly to bias-corrected RMSprop. The update $\alpha_0 \hat{m}_t / (\sqrt{\hat{v}_t} + \varepsilon)$ normalises each coordinate's gradient by its root-mean-square history, giving an effective per-coordinate step size $\approx \alpha_0 \times \operatorname{sign}(g_t)$ when the gradient is consistent --- independent of gradient magnitude. This scale-invariance is why Adam rarely requires careful magnitude tuning of the learning rate $\alpha_0$ across different loss scales.

\textbf{Formal bias correction derivation.} At step $t$, the first moment accumulator satisfies:
\begin{equation}
m_t = (1-\beta_1)\sum_{j=1}^{t} \beta_1^{t-j}\, g_j.
\end{equation}
If the gradients are stationary (i.e., $\mathbb{E}[g_j] = g$ for all $j$), then:
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[m_t] = (1-\beta_1)\sum_{j=1}^{t}\beta_1^{t-j}\,g = g\cdot(1-\beta_1^t).
\end{equation}
Thus $m_t$ is biased: $\mathbb{E}[m_t] = (1-\beta_1^t)\,g \neq g$. Dividing by $(1-\beta_1^t)$ yields the unbiased estimate $\hat{m}_t = m_t/(1-\beta_1^t)$ with $\mathbb{E}[\hat{m}_t] = g$. The same derivation applies to $v_t$ with $\beta_2$. For large $t$, $\beta_1^t \to 0$ and the correction is negligible ($\hat{m}_t \approx m_t$); but for $t \leq 10$ and typical $\beta_1 = 0.9$, the correction factor $1/(1-0.9^t)$ ranges from $10\times$ at $t=1$ to $1.05\times$ at $t=20$, providing a critical warm-up that prevents vanishingly small early steps.

\subsection{Mini-Batch Stochastic Gradient Descent}

Mini-batch SGD replaces the full gradient with a stochastic estimate computed from a random subset of data. With $m = 1000$ samples and batch size $b$:
\begin{equation}
g_{\mathcal{B}} = \frac{1}{b}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{B}}(x^{(i)T}\theta - y^{(i)})x^{(i)}, \quad \mathbb{E}[g_{\mathcal{B}}] = \nabla J(\theta), \quad \operatorname{Var}[g_{\mathcal{B}}] \propto \frac{\sigma^2}{b}.
\end{equation}
Each epoch of size-$b$ SGD performs $\lfloor m/b \rfloor$ gradient steps. The gradient variance decreases linearly with batch size, trading computation per step against gradient quality.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Mini-Batch SGD}
\label{alg:sgd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Data $(X, y)$, initial $\theta_0$, learning rate $\alpha$, batch size $b$, epochs $E$
\For{$e = 1, 2, \ldots, E$}
    \State Randomly permute data indices
    \For{each mini-batch $\mathcal{B}$ of size $b$}
        \State $g \gets \frac{1}{b} X_{\mathcal{B}}^T(X_{\mathcal{B}}\theta - y_{\mathcal{B}})$ \Comment{Stochastic gradient}
        \State $\theta \gets \theta - \alpha\, g$
    \EndFor
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Experiments use $\alpha = 0.06$, $b \in \{5, 40\}$, and 50 epochs. With $b=5$: 200 updates/epoch ($10{,}000$ total); with $b=40$: 25 updates/epoch ($1{,}250$ total). The batch size determines the trade-off between gradient noise and computation:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Small $b$: low cost per step, high gradient variance, more updates per epoch → fast early convergence, noisy near minimum.
\item Large $b$: high cost per step (more function evaluations), low gradient variance → smoother convergence, but fewer parameter updates per epoch.
\end{itemize}
The \textbf{optimal batch size} minimises total computation to reach accuracy $\varepsilon$. For a strongly convex loss with noise variance $\sigma^2$, the optimal batch size is $b^{\star} = \sigma^2/(L \varepsilon)$ (for step size $\alpha = 1/L$). Below $b^{\star}$, reducing $b$ increases variance without improving convergence speed; above $b^{\star}$, the variance is already dominated by the approximation error.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/q2_sgd_batch.pdf}
\caption{Q2: Mini-batch SGD with different batch sizes ($\alpha = 0.06$, 50 epochs). Batch size $b=5$ (200 updates/epoch) achieves faster early convergence due to more frequent parameter updates, but shows greater oscillation near the minimum as individual mini-batch gradient estimates carry higher variance $(\sim 8\times$ larger than $b=40$). Batch size $b=40$ (25 updates/epoch) converges more smoothly but more slowly in terms of epochs. \subsection{Full Batch vs Mini-batch: When to use SGD}

The choice between full-batch GD and mini-batch SGD depends on dataset size and problem structure:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Full-batch GD} (implicit $b = m$): exact gradient, guaranteed descent at each step, $O(m)$ cost per iteration. Preferred for small $m$ ($m \lesssim 10^4$) where the full gradient is cheap.
\item \textbf{Mini-batch SGD} ($1 \leq b \leq m$): noisy gradient, $O(b)$ cost per iteration, $m/b$ updates per pass. Preferred for large $m$ (millions): each epoch processes all data but provides $m/b$ parameter updates, often converging in far fewer epochs than full-batch.
\item \textbf{Batch size 1} (pure SGD): extreme noise but maximum parallelism. Used in online learning where data arrives sequentially.
\end{itemize}
In the experiment: $m = 1000$, $b = 5$ gives 200 updates/epoch; $b = 40$ gives 25 updates/epoch. The loss landscape for linear regression is strongly convex, so both batch sizes converge. For non-convex problems (e.g., neural network training), mini-batch noise can actually help escape local minima --- a regularisation effect not present in full-batch methods.

\subsection{Comparative Analysis}

Nesterov acceleration is the clear winner on the ill-conditioned Benchmark~C, achieving the best convergence among all methods tested in Questions~1 and~2 (except Polyak with correct $f^{\star}$). Its theoretical $O(1/k^2)$ rate, compared to $O(1/k)$ for GD, provides a factor-of-$\sqrt{f(x_0)/\varepsilon}$ improvement in iteration count. For a target accuracy $\varepsilon = 10^{-3}$ starting from $f(x_0) \approx 4$ on Rosenbrock, GD requires $\sim 4/10^{-3} = 4000$ iterations, while Nesterov requires $\sim 2\sqrt{4/10^{-3}} \approx 126$ --- a 32$\times$ reduction.

Adam is competitive on Benchmarks~A and~B, where its per-coordinate scaling effectively decouples the step sizes across dimensions. On Rosenbrock, the rapidly changing Hessian curvature makes the accumulated second-moment estimate less reliable, limiting Adam's advantage. In practice, Adam is preferred for deep learning where gradient sparsity and anisotropy are extreme.

\textbf{Heavy Ball vs.\ Nesterov.} Both methods use momentum, but differ crucially in where the gradient is evaluated. Heavy Ball evaluates at the current point $x_k$, while Nesterov evaluates at the lookahead $x_k + \beta_k z_k$. This seemingly minor modification is theoretically decisive: Heavy Ball with constant momentum does \emph{not} achieve the $O(1/k^2)$ rate on general convex functions (only on quadratics with optimal parameters), whereas Nesterov is provably optimal. Empirically, on the non-quadratic Benchmark~C, this is confirmed: Heavy Ball achieves $f = 1.40$ (Q1) while Nesterov achieves $f = 0.479$ (Q2) --- a 3$\times$ improvement.

\textbf{SGD and mini-batching.} In the stochastic setting, the irreducible gradient noise $\sigma^2 = \mathbb{E}[\|g_{\mathcal{B}} - \nabla f\|^2] = \sigma^2_{\text{data}}/b$ determines the noise floor. Constant-step-size SGD converges to a ball of radius $O(\alpha \sigma^2)$ around the optimum; to achieve $\varepsilon$-accuracy, one must use a decaying step schedule $\alpha_k = O(1/k)$, at the cost of $O(1/(\varepsilon^2))$ iterations. Variance-reduction methods (SVRG, SARAH) interpolate between SGD and full GD, achieving $O(1/k)$ linear convergence in the strongly convex case while using cheap stochastic gradient updates.

\begin{remark}[Batch size and gradient variance]
For a dataset of $m$ samples with observation noise variance $\sigma^2_{\text{obs}}$, the mini-batch gradient estimator $\hat{g}_{\mathcal{B}} = \frac{1}{b}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{B}} \nabla \ell(x; a_i, y_i)$ has variance:
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\hat{g}_{\mathcal{B}} - \nabla f(x)\|^2\right] = \frac{\sigma^2_{\text{obs}}}{b}\left(1 - \frac{b-1}{m-1}\right) \approx \frac{\sigma^2_{\text{obs}}}{b} \quad (m \gg b).
\end{equation}
The batch size $b$ enters as a \emph{variance reduction factor}: doubling $b$ halves the gradient noise variance, at the cost of doubling the computation per step. For fixed computational budget (total gradient evaluations $= T$), the total number of parameter updates is $T/b$, so the noise-reduction advantage of large $b$ must be balanced against fewer updates. Optimal batch size (in terms of optimisation progress per gradient evaluation) depends on the noise structure and the current proximity to the optimum.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Local Approximations and Taylor's Theorem}
\label{sec:q3_approx}

Newton's method is motivated by the second-order Taylor approximation of $f$ around the current iterate $x_k$:
\begin{equation}
f(x) \approx m_k(x) = f(x_k) + \nabla f(x_k)^T(x - x_k) + \frac{1}{2}(x - x_k)^T \nabla^2 f(x_k)(x - x_k).
\label{eq:second_order_approx}
\end{equation}
The exact Taylor remainder is $R_2(x) = O(\|x-x_k\|^3)$, so \eqref{eq:second_order_approx} is accurate in a neighbourhood of $x_k$. Minimising $m_k(x)$ over $x$ (when $\nabla^2 f(x_k) \succ 0$) yields the Newton step:
\begin{equation}
x_{k+1} = x_k - \bigl[\nabla^2 f(x_k)\bigr]^{-1}\nabla f(x_k).
\end{equation}

\textbf{Numerical example: $g(x) = x^4$ at $x_0 = 0.25$.}

\textbf{First-order approximation:}
\begin{equation}
g(x) \approx g(0.25) + g'(0.25)(x - 0.25) = 0.25^4 + 4 \times 0.25^3 (x - 0.25) = 0.00391 + 0.0625(x - 0.25).
\end{equation}

\textbf{Second-order approximation:}
\begin{equation}
g(x) \approx 0.00391 + 0.0625(x - 0.25) + \tfrac{1}{2}\times 12 \times 0.25^2 \times (x - 0.25)^2 = 0.00391 + 0.0625(x-0.25) + 0.375(x-0.25)^2.
\end{equation}
The minimum of the second-order approximation is at $x^{\star}_{\text{approx}} = 0.25 - g'(0.25)/g''(0.25) = 0.25 - 0.0625/0.1875 = -0.0833$, providing a better descent direction than the gradient alone.

\subsection{Newton's Method}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Damped Newton's Method}
\label{alg:newton}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha \in (0, 1]$, damping $\lambda \geq 0$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $H_k \gets \nabla^2 f(x_k) + \lambda I$ \Comment{Tikhonov-regularised Hessian}
    \State Solve $H_k\, p_k = g_k$ for $p_k$ \Comment{Newton direction via Cholesky or LU}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, p_k$ \Comment{$\alpha = 1$: pure Newton; $\alpha < 1$: damped}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Tikhonov regularisation ($\lambda = 10^{-8}$) ensures positive definiteness of $H_k + \lambda I$ even when $H_k$ is near-singular or indefinite. For Benchmark~C, the Rosenbrock Hessian can be ill-conditioned near saddle regions, requiring the damping. The Newton direction $p_k = H_k^{-1}g_k$ is computed via \texttt{np.linalg.solve} (LU factorisation), costing $O(d^3)$ per iteration --- the primary computational drawback of Newton's method in high dimensions.

\begin{theorem}[Quadratic Convergence of Newton's Method]
\label{thm:newton}
Let $f$ be twice continuously differentiable with $L$-Lipschitz Hessian, and let $\nabla^2 f(x^{\star}) \succeq mI$. If $x_0$ is sufficiently close to $x^{\star}$ and $\alpha = 1$, then:
\begin{equation}
\|x_{k+1} - x^{\star}\| \leq \frac{L}{2m}\|x_k - x^{\star}\|^2.
\end{equation}
This \emph{quadratic convergence} means the number of accurate decimal digits approximately doubles at each iteration once in the local neighbourhood.
\end{theorem}
The qualitative difference between the convergence rates is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:convergence_rates}: Newton's quadratic rate (green curve) descends to machine precision in $\sim$10 iterations, whereas all first-order methods require 120+ iterations for comparable accuracy. This reflects the fact that Newton's second-order information removes the condition number's influence on convergence speed, while first-order methods are constrained by $\kappa$.

Hyperparameter settings: GD uses 80 iterations; Newton uses 20 iterations with damping $\lambda = 10^{-8}$.

\textbf{Why Newton converges in one step for quadratic losses.} For Benchmark~A, $J(\theta) = \frac{1}{2m}\|X\theta - y\|^2$ is a quadratic, so the Taylor expansion \eqref{eq:second_order_approx} is exact (the $O(\|x-x_k\|^3)$ remainder is zero). The Newton step is:
\begin{equation}
\theta_1 = \theta_0 - H_A^{-1}\nabla J(\theta_0) = \theta_0 - \left(\frac{X^TX}{m}\right)^{-1}\!\frac{X^T(X\theta_0 - y)}{m} = \left(\frac{X^TX}{m}\right)^{-1}\!\frac{X^Ty}{m} = \hat{\theta}_{\text{OLS}},
\end{equation}
regardless of the starting point $\theta_0$. This exact one-step convergence is a distinctive property of quadratic objectives and does not generalise to nonlinear functions. For Benchmark~B, the nonlinear $\sin(x_1)$ term means the Taylor remainder is nonzero, so Newton requires multiple iterations --- though far fewer than GD.

\textbf{Sensitivity to initialisation.} Newton's method with full step ($\alpha = 1$) can diverge if started far from the optimum on non-convex functions: the Hessian may be indefinite or near-singular, causing large and poorly-directed steps. Damping ($\alpha < 1$) or line search mitigates this. The Rosenbrock function has its Hessian $H_C$ becoming nearly singular along the parabolic valley floor, requiring $\alpha = 0.22$ for stability. In practice, two approaches improve Newton's global behaviour: (i)~\textbf{line search} selects $\alpha_k$ by the Armijo condition $f(x_k - \alpha_k p_k) \leq f(x_k) - c_1 \alpha_k \nabla f(x_k)^T p_k$, backtracking from $\alpha_k = 1$ until descent is achieved; (ii)~\textbf{trust-region} methods adaptively limit the step to a ball $\|p_k\| \leq \Delta_k$ where $\Delta_k$ expands or contracts based on how well the quadratic model predicted the actual function decrease, providing global convergence guarantees. The experiments use a fixed damping factor $\alpha$ instead, which is simpler but requires manual tuning.

\textbf{Newton decrement as a stopping criterion.} A principled stopping rule for Newton's method is the \emph{Newton decrement}:
\begin{equation}
\lambda(x_k)^2 = g_k^T H_k^{-1} g_k = p_k^T H_k p_k,
\end{equation}
where $p_k = H_k^{-1} g_k$ is the Newton direction. The Newton decrement satisfies $\lambda(x_k)^2/2 \geq f(x_k) - f^{\star}$ near the optimum (for strongly convex $f$), providing a computable upper bound on the sub-optimality gap without knowing $f^{\star}$. The stopping criterion $\lambda(x_k)^2/2 \leq \epsilon_{\text{stop}}$ is therefore meaningful: when $\lambda(x_k)^2/2 \leq 10^{-6}$, Newton has converged to within $10^{-6}$ of the optimum. In the experiments, Newton terminates after 20 iterations; with the Newton decrement, the algorithm could self-terminate earlier on Benchmarks~A and~B (where quadratic convergence is rapid), and would confirm non-convergence on Rosenbrock (where $\lambda(x_k)^2$ remains large throughout the 20 iterations).

\subsection{Practical Limitations and Quasi-Newton Methods}

Despite Newton's superior convergence rate, two practical limitations restrict its use:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Hessian computation}: Evaluating $\nabla^2 f(x_k)$ requires $O(d^2)$ storage and $O(d^2)$ gradient evaluations. For $d \sim 10^6$ (e.g., deep learning), this is intractable.
\item \textbf{Linear solve}: Computing $H_k^{-1}g_k$ costs $O(d^3)$ via Cholesky factorisation, or $O(d^2)$ per iteration with conjugate gradient for $H_k p_k = g_k$.
\item \textbf{Non-convexity}: When $\nabla^2 f(x_k)$ is indefinite (negative curvature), the Newton direction is not necessarily a descent direction. The Tikhonov regulariser $\lambda I$ mitigates this but may be insufficient far from the optimum.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Quasi-Newton methods} (e.g., BFGS, L-BFGS) address these limitations by approximating $H_k^{-1}$ from gradient differences, without ever computing the true Hessian. The BFGS update:
\begin{equation}
B_{k+1} = B_k - \frac{B_k s_k s_k^T B_k}{s_k^T B_k s_k} + \frac{y_k y_k^T}{y_k^T s_k}, \quad s_k = x_{k+1} - x_k, \quad y_k = g_{k+1} - g_k,
\end{equation}
maintains a positive definite Hessian approximation using $O(d^2)$ storage. The limited-memory variant (L-BFGS) stores only the last $m \ll d$ correction pairs, reducing storage to $O(md)$. Both achieve \emph{superlinear} convergence --- faster than GD's linear rate but slower than Newton's quadratic rate --- and are the methods of choice in large-scale optimisation in practice.

\section{Question 4: Derivative Approximation and Derivative-Free Optimisation \hfill [30 marks]}

\subsection{Part A: Finite Differences and Nesterov Random Search (Benchmark~B)}

\subsubsection{Finite Difference Gradient Approximation}

When analytical gradients are unavailable, the \emph{forward finite-difference} approximation estimates partial derivatives as:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x) \approx \frac{f(x + \delta e_i) - f(x)}{\delta}.
\label{eq:forward_fd}
\end{equation}
By Taylor's theorem, the truncation error is:
\begin{equation}
\left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x) - \frac{f(x + \delta e_i) - f(x)}{\delta}\right| \leq \frac{\delta}{2}\max_{t \in [0,\delta]}\left|\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i^2}(x + te_i)\right| = O(\delta).
\label{eq:fd_error}
\end{equation}
Additionally, floating-point rounding introduces error $O(\varepsilon_{\text{mach}}/\delta)$. Minimising the total error $\delta/2 \cdot M + \varepsilon_{\text{mach}}/\delta$ over $\delta$ gives the optimal $\delta^{\star} = \sqrt{2\varepsilon_{\text{mach}}/M} \approx \sqrt{\varepsilon_{\text{mach}}} \approx 10^{-8}$. The experiments use $\delta = 0.05$ (near-optimal for Benchmark~B) and $\delta = 0.8$ (deliberately poor), each requiring $d+1 = 3$ function evaluations per gradient.

\begin{remark}
Central differences, $[f(x+\delta e_i) - f(x - \delta e_i)]/(2\delta)$, achieve $O(\delta^2)$ truncation error (better than forward differences' $O(\delta)$) at the cost of $2d$ function evaluations. For well-behaved functions, central differences with $\delta \sim \varepsilon_{\text{mach}}^{1/3}$ yield superior accuracy.
\end{remark}

\subsubsection{Nesterov Random Search}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Nesterov Random Search (Zeroth-Order)}
\label{alg:nrs}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, perturbation $\delta$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State Sample $u_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)$, normalise: $u_k \gets u_k / \|u_k\|$ \Comment{Uniform random direction on $\mathbb{S}^{d-1}$}
    \State $\hat{g}_k \gets \dfrac{f(x_k + \delta u_k) - f(x_k)}{\delta}\, u_k$ \Comment{Smoothed directional gradient estimator}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \hat{g}_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The estimator satisfies $\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_k] = \nabla f_\delta(x_k)$, where $f_\delta(x) = \mathbb{E}_{u \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)}[f(x + \delta u)]$ is the Gaussian-smoothed version of $f$. For smooth $f$, $f_\delta \approx f$ and $\nabla f_\delta \approx \nabla f$. The key advantage is \textbf{dimension-independent cost}: only 2 function evaluations are needed per iteration regardless of $d$, making this method suitable for high-dimensional black-box optimisation where computing $d$ finite differences would be prohibitive. However, the variance of the estimator grows with $d$: in $d$ dimensions, the random direction $u$ captures only a $1/d$ fraction of the gradient's energy on average, degrading convergence speed as $O(\sqrt{d})$ more iterations are needed compared to exact gradient methods. In $d = 2$ (our experiments), this is a small penalty; for $d = 1000$, Nesterov random search would require $\sim 22\times$ more iterations than FD-GD to achieve the same accuracy.

\subsection{Part B: Derivative-Free Methods on Benchmark~C}

\subsubsection{Nelder--Mead Simplex Method}

The Nelder--Mead algorithm (Nelder \& Mead, 1965) maintains a simplex of $n+1 = 3$ vertices in $\mathbb{R}^2$ and iteratively transforms it through four operations: \textbf{reflection} ($\alpha_r = 1.0$, reflect worst vertex through centroid), \textbf{expansion} ($\gamma = 2.0$, extend reflection if improved), \textbf{contraction} ($\rho = 0.5$, contract towards centroid if reflection failed), and \textbf{shrink} ($\sigma = 0.5$, contract all vertices towards the best). The simplex adapts its shape and size to the local landscape, elongating in the direction of steepest descent and shrinking near the minimum.

\begin{remark}
Nelder--Mead requires only $\leq 3$ function evaluations per iteration in $\mathbb{R}^2$ (reflection or expansion) and has no convergence guarantee for general nonlinear functions. It is widely used in practice for low-dimensional ($d \leq 10$) black-box optimisation due to its simplicity and reasonable empirical performance, but can stagnate on highly nonlinear or noisy problems.
\end{remark}

Settings: initial simplex step $= 0.35$, iterations $= 160$, standard parameters $(\alpha_r, \gamma, \rho, \sigma) = (1, 2, 0.5, 0.5)$.

\textbf{Comparison with gradient-based methods on Benchmark~C.} Despite using no gradient information, Nelder--Mead achieves $f \approx 10^{-3}$ after 160 iterations (approximately 480 function evaluations), compared to:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item GD with exact gradient, $\alpha = 0.001$, 80 iterations: $f = 3.73$ ($\sim$80 gradient evaluations, each requiring 1 function evaluation).
\item Nesterov, $\alpha = 0.0007$, 150 iterations: $f = 0.479$ ($\sim$150 gradient evaluations).
\end{itemize}
This remarkable result shows that Nelder--Mead, by adapting its simplex shape to the Rosenbrock valley, can \emph{outperform} first-order gradient methods in terms of final objective value, at comparable function evaluation cost. The key mechanism is that Nelder--Mead's simplex can orient itself along the narrow curved valley (similar in effect to a coordinate-independent preconditioning), while gradient descent is constrained by the gradient direction which is nearly orthogonal to the valley floor.

The fundamental trade-off is between gradient quality, computational cost, and scalability. Finite differences become prohibitively expensive for $d \gg 1$ (cost scales as $O(d)$ per gradient). Nesterov random search avoids this with 2 evaluations per step, at the cost of slow convergence (high variance estimator). Nelder--Mead is practical for low-dimensional problems but has no theoretical guarantees and degrades for $d > 10$. Grid search provides global coverage but is exponentially expensive in $d$.

\textbf{Practical guidance for $\delta$ selection.} On Benchmark~B, the second-order term in the truncation error bound \eqref{eq:fd_error} is bounded by $\frac{\delta}{2}\max_i|H_{ii}|$. At the starting point $x_0 = (-1, 4)$: $H_{11} = 2-\sin(-1) \approx 2.84$, so the truncation error is $\frac{0.05}{2}\times 2.84 \approx 0.071$ for $\delta=0.05$, and $\frac{0.8}{2}\times 2.84 \approx 1.14$ for $\delta=0.8$. This large truncation error ($\gg \|\nabla f\|$) explains why FD with $\delta = 0.8$ produces an inaccurate gradient direction and fails to descend effectively.

\textbf{Curse of dimensionality quantified.} With a grid resolution of $N = 55$ per dimension, the total function evaluations scale as $N^d$: for $d=2$, this is $3025$; for $d=5$, $55^5 \approx 5 \times 10^8$; for $d=10$, $55^{10} \approx 2.5 \times 10^{17}$. At $10^9$ evaluations per second, $d=10$ would require $\sim 250$ million seconds ($\sim 8$ years). This exponential explosion is the fundamental barrier to grid search beyond $d \approx 3$.

\textbf{Key limitation of derivative-free methods.} All derivative-free methods in this section (FD, NRS, Nelder--Mead, Grid Search) share the fundamental limitation that they are \emph{local} methods (except grid search) with convergence guarantees only to local optima. On Benchmark~C, which is convex (hence has a unique minimum), this is not an issue. For multimodal functions, global optimisation would require additional techniques such as simulated annealing, genetic algorithms, or Bayesian optimisation with Gaussian process surrogates. Among the methods compared here, grid search is the only one that provides a global optimality guarantee (to within the grid resolution).

\subsection{Problem Formulation and KKT Conditions}

The constrained problem is:
\begin{equation}
\min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^2}\ f(x) = (x_1-1)^2 + 5(x_2-2)^2 + \sin(x_1), \quad \text{subject to}\quad x_1 \geq 0.5.
\label{eq:q5_problem}
\end{equation}
The feasible set is $\mathcal{C} = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^2 : x_1 \geq 0.5\}$. The inequality constraint $h(x) = 0.5 - x_1 \leq 0$ is a single linear constraint. The Karush--Kuhn--Tucker (KKT) optimality conditions for \eqref{eq:q5_problem} state that at the constrained minimum $x^{\star}$:
\begin{align}
&\nabla f(x^{\star}) = \mu\, e_1, \quad \mu \geq 0 \quad \text{(stationarity)}, \\
&x_1^{\star} \geq 0.5 \quad \text{(primal feasibility)}, \\
&\mu(x_1^{\star} - 0.5) = 0 \quad \text{(complementary slackness)}.
\end{align}
Since the unconstrained minimum of $f$ satisfies $x_1^{\star} \approx 0.582 > 0.5$, the constraint is \emph{inactive} at the optimum. By complementary slackness: $\mu(0.582 - 0.5) = \mu \times 0.082 = 0 \Rightarrow \mu = 0$, and the constrained minimum coincides with the unconstrained minimum, $(x^{\star}_{\text{constr}} = x^{\star}_{\text{unconstr}} \approx (0.582, 2.0))$.

\textbf{Contrast: active constraint case.} If the constraint were instead $x_1 \geq 1$ (so the unconstrained minimum $x_1^{\star} \approx 0.582 < 1$ would be infeasible), the constrained minimum would lie on the boundary $x_1 = 1$. In that case, $\mu > 0$ (the constraint is active), and the optimality condition becomes $\nabla_1 f(1, x_2^{\star}) = \mu$ --- the gradient of $f$ in the $x_1$ direction must equal the KKT multiplier. Projected GD and large-$\lambda$ penalty methods would both correctly identify this boundary optimum, while unconstrained GD would settle at the infeasible $x_1^{\star} \approx 0.582$.

Starting from the infeasible point $x_0 = (0.2, 4.0)$ (where $x_1 = 0.2 < 0.5$), algorithms must first achieve feasibility before optimising the objective.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Projected Gradient Descent (PGD)}
\label{alg:pgd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, projection oracle $\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \nabla f(x_k)$ \Comment{Unconstrained gradient step}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets \Pi_{\mathcal{C}}(z_{k+1}) = \max(0.5,\, z_{1}) \times e_1 + z_2 e_2$ \Comment{Euclidean projection onto $\mathcal{C}$}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

For the half-space $\mathcal{C} = \{x : x_1 \geq 0.5\}$, the projection is trivial: $\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}(z) = (\max(0.5, z_1), z_2)^T$. For general convex constraints (e.g., second-order cones, semidefinite constraints), the projection oracle can be expensive or intractable.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Penalty Gradient Descent}
\label{alg:penalty}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, penalty weight $\lambda > 0$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $F_{\lambda}(x) \gets f(x) + \lambda\,\max(0,\; 0.5 - x_1)$ \Comment{Penalised (non-smooth) objective}
    \State Compute $\nabla F_{\lambda}(x_k)$: gradient of $f$ plus $\lambda$ sub-gradient of penalty
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \nabla F_{\lambda}(x_k)$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

As $\lambda \to \infty$, the penalty method solution converges to the constrained minimum. However, large $\lambda$ stiffens the objective, reducing the stability threshold for the step size $\alpha$: the effective Lipschitz constant of $\nabla F_\lambda$ grows with $\lambda$, requiring $\alpha \propto 1/\lambda$ for stability. Concretely, the penalty gradient is $\nabla F_\lambda(x) = \nabla f(x) - \lambda e_1 \cdot \mathbf{1}[x_1 < 0.5]$. For $x_1 < 0.5$, the augmented Hessian along $e_1$ is $H_{11} + 0 = H_{11}$ (the penalty term $\lambda \max(0, 0.5-x_1)$ is linear in $x_1$, so its Hessian contribution is zero). However, the non-smooth kink at $x_1 = 0.5$ means the gradient has a discontinuity of magnitude $\lambda$ at the boundary, which requires a step size $\alpha < 2/(L + \lambda) \approx 2/\lambda$ for stable convergence near the boundary. This is why the experiment reduces $\alpha$ from 0.05 to 0.03 when increasing $\lambda$ from 1.8 to 4.5.

\textbf{Settings:} PGD ($\alpha = 0.08$); Penalty $\lambda \in \{0.15, 1.8, 4.5\}$ ($\alpha = 0.05$ for $\lambda \leq 1.8$; $\alpha = 0.03$ for $\lambda = 4.5$); unconstrained GD ($\alpha = 0.07$). All: 100 iterations.

\textbf{Projected GD convergence bound.} For a $\mu$-strongly convex, $L$-smooth objective with feasible set $\mathcal{C}$ and step size $\alpha = 1/L$, projected GD satisfies:
\begin{equation}
\|x_k - x^{\star}\|^2 \leq \left(1 - \frac{\mu}{L}\right)^k \|x_0 - x^{\star}\|^2 = \left(1 - \frac{1}{\kappa}\right)^k \|x_0 - x^{\star}\|^2.
\end{equation}
This matches the unconstrained GD rate exactly, because the projection onto a convex set is a non-expansive operator: $\|\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}(u) - \Pi_{\mathcal{C}}(v)\| \leq \|u - v\|$. For our half-space constraint $\mathcal{C} = \{x : x_1 \geq 0.5\}$ and starting point $x_0 = (0.2, 4.0)$, the initial distance to the constrained optimum is $\|x_0 - x^{\star}\| = \|(0.2-0.582, 4-2)\| \approx 2.04$, and the convergence rate matches the unconstrained rate.

\textbf{Discussion.} On this specific problem, the unconstrained GD also converges to the correct answer because the constraint happens to be inactive at the unconstrained minimum. In general, if the unconstrained minimum were at $x_1^{\star} < 0.5$ (feasible set does not contain the unconstrained minimum), GD would find a suboptimal infeasible solution. Projected GD and large-$\lambda$ penalty methods would correctly identify the constrained boundary minimum.

The fundamental trade-off between the two constrained methods:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Projected GD} guarantees hard feasibility at every iterate but requires a tractable projection oracle. For simple constraints (boxes, balls, half-spaces), projection is $O(d)$. For complex sets (e.g., positive semidefinite cone), projection requires a full eigendecomposition at $O(d^3)$ cost.
\item \textbf{Penalty methods} require only gradient computation of the augmented objective and handle arbitrary constraint sets equally easily. The trade-off is soft feasibility: exact constraint satisfaction only in the limit $\lambda \to \infty$, with the practical problem that large $\lambda$ induces ill-conditioning and requires smaller step sizes.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Augmented Lagrangian: An Improved Penalty Approach}

The fundamental weakness of the basic penalty method is that achieving exact feasibility requires $\lambda \to \infty$, which makes the augmented objective ill-conditioned. The \textbf{Augmented Lagrangian method} (ALM, also known as the method of multipliers) avoids this by maintaining an explicit Lagrange multiplier estimate $\mu_k$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_\rho(x, \mu) = f(x) + \mu\,\max(0,\; h(x)) + \frac{\rho}{2}\!\left[\max(0,\; h(x))\right]^2,
\end{equation}
where $h(x) = 0.5 - x_1$ is the constraint violation. The update rule alternates between minimising $\mathcal{L}_\rho(\cdot, \mu_k)$ over $x$ and updating $\mu_{k+1} = \mu_k + \rho\,\max(0, h(x_{k+1}))$. By updating the multiplier estimate, ALM achieves exact feasibility for \emph{finite} $\rho$, avoiding the ill-conditioning of large $\lambda$. The quadratic term in $\mathcal{L}_\rho$ penalises infeasibility for stability, but the linear multiplier term drives exact constraint satisfaction. This makes ALM far more numerically stable than the pure penalty method while retaining its flexibility with respect to arbitrary constraint types.

\subsection{Feasible Set and Linear Programme}

Feasible set: $\mathcal{X} = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^2 : 0.5 \leq x_1 \leq 5,\ -5 \leq x_2 \leq 10\}$ (bounded box polytope with diameter $C = \|[4.5, 15]^T\| \approx 15.66$).

\textbf{Linear programme:} For $f(x) = a^T x$ with $a = [1, 2]^T$, the minimum over $\mathcal{X}$ is obtained by minimising each coordinate independently, since $\mathcal{X}$ is a box:
\begin{equation}
x_i^{\star} = \begin{cases} l_i & \text{if } a_i > 0 \\ u_i & \text{if } a_i < 0 \end{cases} \Rightarrow x^{\star} = (0.5,\, -5), \quad f^{\star} = 0.5 + 2(-5) = -9.5.
\end{equation}
This illustrates the \textbf{fundamental theorem of linear programming}: the minimum of a linear objective over a bounded convex polytope is attained at a vertex (extreme point). The interior of the feasible set can never be the optimum of a linear function, since one can always improve by moving towards a vertex.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/q6_lp.pdf}
\caption{Q6-I: Linear programme. The parallel objective contours $f(x) = c$ decrease as $c$ decreases (moving lower-left). The minimum is attained at the lower-left vertex $(0.5, -5)$ of the feasible box, confirming the vertex theorem. The LP subproblem of Frank--Wolfe has exactly this structure.}
\label{fig:q6_lp}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Frank--Wolfe Algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Frank--Wolfe (Conditional Gradient Method)}
\label{alg:fw}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial $x_0 \in \mathcal{X}$, fixed mixing parameter $\beta \in (0, 1)$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $z_k \gets \arg\min_{x \in \mathcal{X}}\, g_k^T x$ \Comment{Linear minimisation oracle (LMO)}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets \beta\, x_k + (1 - \beta)\, z_k$ \Comment{Convex combination; step size $\gamma = 1 - \beta$ towards $z_k$}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

For box constraints, the LMO decomposes per coordinate:
\begin{equation}
(z_k)_i = \begin{cases} l_i & \text{if } (\nabla f(x_k))_i > 0 \\ u_i & \text{if } (\nabla f(x_k))_i \leq 0 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
This requires $O(d)$ operations --- far cheaper than a projection oracle for general constraint sets. Crucially, since $x_0 \in \mathcal{X}$ and $z_k \in \mathcal{X}$, every convex combination $x_{k+1} = \beta x_k + (1-\beta)z_k \in \mathcal{X}$: \textbf{Frank--Wolfe iterates are always feasible by construction}, with no projection required.

\textbf{Standard Frank--Wolfe} uses the diminishing step $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$ instead of fixed $\beta$, yielding the convergence guarantee: for $L$-smooth $f$:
\begin{equation}
f(x_k) - f^{\star} \leq \frac{2LC^2}{k+2},
\label{eq:fw_convergence}
\end{equation}
where $C = \sup_{x,y \in \mathcal{X}}\|x - y\|$ is the diameter of $\mathcal{X}$. This is $O(1/k)$ convergence. The experiments use a fixed $\beta$ (i.e., fixed step size $\gamma = 1-\beta$), which can achieve comparable empirical convergence on these problems but lacks the formal guarantee \eqref{eq:fw_convergence}.

\textbf{Frank--Wolfe duality gap:} A useful optimality certificate at each iterate is the \emph{primal gap} or \emph{FW gap}:
\begin{equation}
h(x_k) = \nabla f(x_k)^T(x_k - z_k) \geq f(x_k) - f^{\star} \geq 0,
\end{equation}
where the inequality follows from the convexity of $f$. When $h(x_k) \approx 0$, the current iterate is near-optimal. This provides a certificate of convergence \emph{without} knowing $f^{\star}$.

\subsection{Interior vs.\ Boundary Optimum: Theoretical Analysis}

\textbf{Interior optimum.} Frank--Wolfe converges to the interior minimum but exhibits the characteristic ``zig-zag'' behaviour. The LP vertex $z_k$ jumps between extreme points of $\mathcal{X}$ at each iteration due to the following mechanism: near the interior minimum $(1, 5)$, the gradient $\nabla f(x_k) = (2(x_1-1), 2(x_2-5))$ is small in magnitude but alternates sign between $x_1 > 1$ and $x_1 < 1$ (and similarly for $x_2$). When $x_1 > 1$: $\partial f/\partial x_1 > 0 \Rightarrow z_1 = l_1 = 0.5$. When $x_1 < 1$: $\partial f/\partial x_1 < 0 \Rightarrow z_1 = u_1 = 5$. This oscillation causes $z_k$ to alternate between two vertices of $\mathcal{X}$, and the convex combination $x_{k+1} = \beta x_k + (1-\beta)z_k$ takes weighted average steps that gradually approach the minimum. This is the fundamental limitation of FW for interior optima: it cannot distinguish between ``gradient is small because we are near the optimum'' and ``gradient is small because we overshot,'' leading to persistent vertex oscillation. The fixed-$\beta$ implementation does not match the standard FW convergence rate \eqref{eq:fw_convergence} but achieves reasonable empirical performance.

\textbf{Boundary optimum.} Frank--Wolfe exhibits a key advantage here: when the constrained optimum lies on the boundary of $\mathcal{X}$, the LP vertex $z_k$ consistently selects the vertex of $\mathcal{X}$ closest to the true minimum --- in this case, $(0.5, 0)$ (the nearest feasible vertex to $(0,0)$). The iterate naturally converges to the boundary without any projection step. This ``self-steering'' towards active constraints is a distinctive property of Frank--Wolfe not shared by projected gradient methods.

However, the fixed step size $\gamma = 1 - \beta = 0.07$ means the boundary optimum is not reached exactly after 140 iterations (final $f = 0.3637 > f^{\star} = 0.25$). The theoretical FW convergence bound \eqref{eq:fw_convergence} with diminishing step predicts convergence to within $\varepsilon$ after $k \geq 2LC^2/\varepsilon - 2$ iterations. For the boundary case: $L = 2$ (gradient of $\nabla f = 2x$ is Lipschitz with $L=2$), $C \approx 15.66$ (diameter of $\mathcal{X}$), and $\varepsilon = 0.01$ (relative to $f^{\star} = 0.25$), giving $k \geq 2 \times 2 \times 15.66^2/0.01 \approx 98,000$ iterations --- indicating that the box feasible set is very large relative to the optimal step and many more iterations are needed for precision convergence. Using the standard FW schedule $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$ would guarantee this convergence.

\textbf{FW gap as stopping criterion.} At the final boundary iterate $(0.500, -0.337)$: $\nabla f = 2(0.500, -0.337) = (1.0, -0.674)$. The LP vertex is $z_k = (l_1, u_2) = (0.5, 10)$ (since $g_1 > 0, g_2 < 0$). The FW gap is:
\begin{equation}
h(x_k) = \nabla f(x_k)^T(x_k - z_k) = (1.0, -0.674)\cdot(0.0, -10.337) = 6.97 \gg 0,
\end{equation}
confirming that significant optimality gap remains and the algorithm has not converged. This demonstrates that the FW gap provides a reliable, computable certificate of non-convergence even without knowing $f^{\star}$.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

This report has investigated six families of optimisation algorithms across three benchmark problems of increasing complexity, from the well-conditioned linear regression quadratic (Benchmark~A, $\kappa \approx 1$) to the extremely ill-conditioned Rosenbrock function (Benchmark~C, $\kappa \approx 2000$). The following conclusions are drawn.

\subsection{Summary of Key Findings}

\textbf{Adaptive step-size methods (Q1).} The Polyak step size achieves the best convergence when $f^{\star}$ is known exactly, as demonstrated on Benchmark~C ($f = 0.0094$ after 120 iterations). However, it diverges catastrophically on Benchmarks~A and~B where $f^{\star} = 0$ underestimates the true minimum, illustrating that information-optimal methods can be fragile in practice. Adagrad and Heavy Ball are the most robust alternatives, converging reliably across all benchmarks.

\textbf{Momentum methods (Q2).} Nesterov acceleration achieves the largest improvement over GD, particularly on Benchmark~C ($f = 0.479$ vs.\ $f = 3.388$ for GD), consistent with its theoretically optimal $O(1/k^2)$ convergence rate. Adam performs well on near-quadratic problems (Benchmarks~A and~B) due to its joint momentum and adaptive scaling, but is less effective on the highly nonlinear Rosenbrock.

\textbf{Newton's method (Q3).} Second-order information provides dramatic convergence acceleration: one iteration suffices for the quadratic Benchmark~A, and 20 damped iterations outperform 80 GD iterations on every benchmark. The $O(d^3)$ per-iteration cost and need for the Hessian limit scalability to low-dimensional or structured problems.

\textbf{Derivative-free methods (Q4).} Finite differences with well-chosen $\delta$ (e.g., $\delta = 0.05$ on Benchmark~B) track exact GD closely, at a cost of $d$ additional function evaluations per gradient. Nesterov random search is dimension-scalable (2 evaluations/step) but converges slowly. Nelder--Mead is effective on low-dimensional problems without gradient information. Grid search guarantees global coverage but is exponential in dimension.

\textbf{Constrained optimisation (Q5).} Projected GD achieves hard feasibility from the first iteration, making it the most reliable method when a tractable projection oracle exists. Penalty methods provide a softer alternative that only requires gradient computation, converging to the feasible region as $\lambda$ increases, at the cost of reduced step-size stability.

\textbf{Frank--Wolfe (Q6).} The conditional gradient method solves only a linear subproblem per iteration (cheap for box constraints), maintains feasibility by construction, and is well-suited to boundary optima where it ``self-steers'' towards the active constraints. The $O(1/k)$ convergence rate with fixed step size is slower than projected gradient descent but requires no projection oracle.

\textbf{Impact of condition number.} The extreme ill-conditioning of Rosenbrock ($\kappa \approx 2000$) reveals a clear hierarchy: methods that use second-order information (Newton) or approximately second-order information (Nesterov's $O(1/k^2)$ rate, Adam's per-coordinate scaling) significantly outperform vanilla GD. The ranking is:
\begin{equation*}
\underbrace{\text{Polyak (exact } f^{\star})}_{0.009} \ll \underbrace{\text{Nesterov}}_{0.479} < \underbrace{\text{Newton (20 it.)}}_{0.686} < \underbrace{\text{Heavy Ball}}_{1.40} < \underbrace{\text{Adam}}_{1.58} < \underbrace{\text{Adagrad}}_{2.20} < \underbrace{\text{RMSprop}}_{3.12} \approx \underbrace{\text{GD}}_{3.39\text{--}3.73}.
\end{equation*}
This ordering reflects the theoretical hierarchy: Polyak is information-theoretically optimal with correct $f^\star$; Nesterov is optimal among first-order methods without $f^\star$; Newton uses second-order information that bypasses first-order limitations; adaptive methods (Adam, Heavy Ball) partially correct for anisotropy; GD baseline has no anisotropy correction.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Gradient Descent},label={lst:gd}]
def gradient_descent(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        x = x - alpha * grad_fn(x)
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Polyak Step Size},label={lst:polyak}]
def polyak_step(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, f_star, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        alpha_k = (loss_fn(x) - f_star) / (np.dot(g, g) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(alpha_k)
        x = x - alpha_k * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Adagrad},label={lst:adagrad}]
def adagrad(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha0, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    G = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        G += g**2
        eff_alpha = alpha0 / (np.sqrt(G) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(np.mean(eff_alpha))
        x = x - eff_alpha * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={RMSprop},label={lst:rmsprop}]
def rmsprop(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha0, beta, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    v = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        v = beta * v + (1 - beta) * g**2
        eff_alpha = alpha0 / (np.sqrt(v) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(np.mean(eff_alpha))
        x = x - eff_alpha * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Heavy Ball / Polyak Momentum},label={lst:hb}]
def heavy_ball(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    z = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        z = beta * z + alpha * g
        x = x - z
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Nesterov Accelerated Gradient},label={lst:nesterov}]
def nesterov_momentum(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta_max, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    z = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for k in range(1, n_iters + 1):
        beta_k = min((k - 1) / (k + 2), beta_max)
        lookahead = x + beta_k * z          # Evaluate gradient at lookahead
        g = grad_fn(lookahead)
        z = beta_k * z - alpha * g
        x = x + z
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Adam Optimiser},label={lst:adam}]
def adam_optimiser(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta1, beta2, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    m_vec, v_vec = np.zeros_like(x), np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for t in range(1, n_iters + 1):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        m_vec = beta1 * m_vec + (1 - beta1) * g
        v_vec = beta2 * v_vec + (1 - beta2) * g**2
        m_hat = m_vec / (1 - beta1**t)        # Bias correction
        v_hat = v_vec / (1 - beta2**t)
        x = x - alpha * m_hat / (np.sqrt(v_hat) + eps)
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Damped Newton's Method},label={lst:newton}]
def newtons_method(grad_fn, hess_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, n_iters, damping=1e-8):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, update_norms = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        H = hess_fn(x) if callable(hess_fn) else hess_fn
        H_reg = H + damping * np.eye(len(x))
        try:
            p = np.linalg.solve(H_reg, g)     # Newton direction
        except np.linalg.LinAlgError:
            p = g
        update_norms.append(np.linalg.norm(alpha * p))
        x = x - alpha * p
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(update_norms)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Frank--Wolfe (Conditional Gradient)},label={lst:fw}]
def frank_wolfe(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, bounds, beta, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, z_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        # Linear minimisation oracle: per-coordinate argmin over box
        z = np.array([bounds[i][0] if g[i] > 0 else bounds[i][1]
                       for i in range(len(g))])
        z_hist.append(z.copy())
        x = beta * x + (1 - beta) * z      # Convex combination step
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(z_hist)
\end{lstlisting}
