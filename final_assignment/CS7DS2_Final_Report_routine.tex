\begin{abstract}
This report presents a systematic comparative study of sixteen optimisation algorithms applied to three benchmark objective functions of increasing difficulty: a quadratic linear-regression loss (Benchmark A), a nearly-quadratic function with sinusoidal perturbation (Benchmark B), and the highly ill-conditioned Rosenbrock function (Benchmark C). Six algorithmic families are investigated: (i) adaptive step-size methods---Polyak, Adagrad, RMSprop, and Polyak momentum (Heavy Ball)---demonstrating that accurate knowledge of $f^{\star}$ is critical for Polyak and that Heavy Ball achieves the best momentum-free performance on Rosenbrock ($f = 1.40$); (ii) momentum and stochastic methods---Nesterov acceleration and Adam---where Nesterov attains $f = 0.479$ on Rosenbrock in 150 iterations, achieving the theoretical $O(1/k^2)$ rate; (iii) Newton's method, which solves the quadratic loss exactly in one step and achieves $f = 0.686$ on Rosenbrock in only 20 iterations; (iv) derivative-free methods including finite differences, Nesterov random search, Nelder--Mead, and grid search, revealing the fundamental bias--variance trade-off in gradient approximation; (v) constrained optimisation via projected gradient descent and quadratic penalty methods, where projected GD enforces hard feasibility from iteration one; and (vi) the Frank--Wolfe (conditional gradient) algorithm for box-constrained problems, demonstrating the $O(1/k)$ convergence rate and the effect of mixing parameters on final accuracy. All experiments use a fixed random seed (\texttt{np.random.seed(42)}) for full reproducibility. The central finding is that second-order curvature information---whether exact (Newton) or approximated (Adam, RMSprop)---is decisive for ill-conditioned problems such as Rosenbrock, while first-order methods with momentum are sufficient and far cheaper for well-conditioned problems.
\end{abstract}

\section{Benchmark Problems and Experimental Setup}

All experiments use \texttt{np.random.seed(42)} for reproducibility. Gradients and Hessians are computed analytically unless stated otherwise.

\subsection{Benchmark A: Linear Regression Quadratic Loss}

A synthetic dataset with $m = 1000$ samples and $d = 2$ parameters is generated as follows:
\begin{equation}
\theta^{\star} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix},\quad x^{(i)} \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0,I_2),\quad y^{(i)} = \theta^{\star T} x^{(i)} + \varepsilon^{(i)},\quad \varepsilon^{(i)} \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0,1).
\end{equation}
The mean-squared-error loss is:
\begin{equation}
J(\theta) = \frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\!\left(\theta^T x^{(i)} - y^{(i)}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{2m}\|X\theta - y\|^2,
\end{equation}
with gradient $\nabla J(\theta) = \frac{1}{m}X^T(X\theta - y)$ and constant Hessian $H = \frac{1}{m}X^TX$.  Because $X$ has i.i.d.\ Gaussian rows, by the law of large numbers $H \approx I_2$ for large $m$, so the condition number $\kappa(H) \approx 1$---making this a well-conditioned quadratic.  The irreducible minimum due to observation noise is $J^{\star} \approx 0.4826$; the starting point is $\theta_0 = [0,0]^T$.

\subsection{Benchmark B: Toy Neural Network Loss}
\begin{equation}
f(x_1,x_2) = (x_1-1)^2 + 5(x_2-2)^2 + \sin(x_1).
\label{eq:bench_B}
\end{equation}
Gradient: $\nabla f = \bigl[2(x_1-1)+\cos(x_1),\;10(x_2-2)\bigr]^T$.
Hessian: $H(x) = \operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(2-\sin(x_1),\;10\bigr)$.
Setting $\nabla f = 0$ yields $x_2^{\star} = 2$ and $2(x_1^{\star}-1)+\cos(x_1^{\star}) = 0$, solved numerically as $x_1^{\star} \approx 0.582$, giving $f^{\star} \approx 0.7244$.  The eigenvalue ratio of $H$ at the optimum lies in the range $[1.8, 3.0]$ vs.\ $10$, producing a \emph{moderate} condition number $\kappa \approx 5$. Starting point: $x_0 = [-1,4]^T$.

\subsection{Benchmark C: Rosenbrock Function}
\begin{equation}
f(x_1,x_2) = (1-x_1)^2 + 100(x_2-x_1^2)^2.
\label{eq:bench_C}
\end{equation}
Global minimum: $x^{\star} = (1,1)$, $f^{\star} = 0$.  At the optimum:
\begin{equation}
H(1,1) = \begin{bmatrix} 802 & -400 \\ -400 & 200 \end{bmatrix},
\quad \lambda_{\min} \approx 0.4,\quad \lambda_{\max} \approx 1001.6,\quad \kappa \approx 2004.
\end{equation}
This extreme ill-conditioning, combined with the narrow curved valley $x_2 \approx x_1^2$, makes Rosenbrock the definitive stress test: any algorithm that performs well here is genuinely robust to curvature anisotropy. Starting point: $x_0 = [-1,1]^T$.

Crucially, the Hessian eigenvalues are \emph{not constant} across the Rosenbrock domain: $\lambda_{\max}(H(x)) = 2 + 1200x_1^2 - 400x_2 + \sqrt{(\cdots)^2 + (400x_1)^2}$ varies from $\sim 1265$ at the starting point $(-1,1)$ to $\sim 1002$ at the optimum $(1,1)$.  This means the global Lipschitz constant $L$ (which governs GD step size) is determined by the worst-case curvature, forcing a conservative step for the entire trajectory---even once the iterate reaches regions of lower curvature.  Figure~\ref{fig:rosenbrock_hessian} visualises this variation.

\subsection{Theoretical Foundations}
\label{sec:theory}

\begin{definition}[$L$-smoothness]
A differentiable function $f$ is \emph{$L$-smooth} if $\|\nabla f(x) - \nabla f(y)\| \leq L\|x-y\|$ for all $x,y$.  Equivalently, $f(y) \leq f(x) + \nabla f(x)^T(y-x) + \tfrac{L}{2}\|y-x\|^2$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[$\mu$-strong convexity]
$f$ is \emph{$\mu$-strongly convex} if $f(y) \geq f(x) + \nabla f(x)^T(y-x) + \tfrac{\mu}{2}\|y-x\|^2$ for all $x,y$.
\end{definition}

\label{tab:benchmarks_summary}
\begin{tabular}{lccccl}
\toprule
\textbf{Benchmark} & \textbf{$L$ (global)} & \textbf{$\mu$} & \textbf{$\kappa$} & \textbf{Convexity} & \textbf{Character} \\
\midrule

For Benchmark A, $L = \lambda_{\max}(H) \approx 1$ and $\mu = \lambda_{\min}(H) \approx 1$, so $\kappa = L/\mu \approx 1$.  Gradient descent with step $\alpha \leq 1/L$ achieves geometric convergence at rate $\rho = 1 - \mu\alpha \leq 1 - \mu/L = 1 - 1/\kappa$.  For Benchmark C, the Rosenbrock function is non-convex (the Hessian has a negative eigenvalue at some points away from the optimum), so strong convexity theory does not directly apply; convergence results are local.

\subsection{Algorithm Formulations}

Four adaptive step-size methods are compared against a constant step-size gradient descent (GD) baseline across all three benchmarks for 120 iterations.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Gradient Descent (Baseline)}
\label{alg:gd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, constant step size $\alpha$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0,1,\ldots,N-1$}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\,\nabla f(x_k)$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Polyak Step Size}
\label{alg:polyak}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, known optimum $f^{\star}$, tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0,1,\ldots,N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $\alpha_k \gets \dfrac{f(x_k) - f^{\star}}{\|g_k\|^2 + \varepsilon}$ \Comment{Self-tuning step eliminates line search}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha_k\,g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Adagrad}
\label{alg:adagrad}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, base rate $\alpha_0$, tolerance $\varepsilon$, iterations $N$
\State $G_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 0,1,\ldots,N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $G_{k+1} \gets G_k + g_k \odot g_k$ \Comment{Monotonically accumulates squared gradients}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \dfrac{\alpha_0}{\sqrt{G_{k+1}} + \varepsilon} \odot g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{RMSprop}
\label{alg:rmsprop}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, learning rate $\alpha_0$, decay $\beta \in (0,1)$, tolerance $\varepsilon$, iterations $N$
\State $v_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 0,1,\ldots,N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $v_{k+1} \gets \beta\,v_k + (1-\beta)\,g_k \odot g_k$ \Comment{Exponential moving average}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \dfrac{\alpha_0}{\sqrt{v_{k+1}} + \varepsilon} \odot g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Polyak Momentum / Heavy Ball}
\label{alg:heavyball}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, momentum $\beta \in (0,1)$, iterations $N$
\State $z_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 0,1,\ldots,N-1$}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets \beta\,z_k + \alpha\,\nabla f(x_k)$ \Comment{Accumulate gradient momentum}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - z_{k+1}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Theoretical Convergence Properties}
\label{sec:q1_theory}

\begin{theorem}[GD convergence on strongly convex problems]
For an $L$-smooth, $\mu$-strongly convex function, gradient descent with step $\alpha = 1/L$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}
f(x_k) - f^{\star} \leq \left(1 - \frac{\mu}{L}\right)^k\!\bigl(f(x_0) - f^{\star}\bigr) = \left(1 - \frac{1}{\kappa}\right)^k\!\bigl(f(x_0) - f^{\star}\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $\kappa = L/\mu$ is the condition number.
\end{theorem}

The implication is that Benchmark A ($\kappa \approx 1$) converges almost immediately, while Benchmark C ($\kappa \approx 2004$) requires $O(\kappa \log(1/\varepsilon))$ iterations, explaining why GD barely moves on Rosenbrock with a step size constrained by $\alpha \leq 1/L$.

\textbf{Polyak step convergence} \cite{polyak1987}.  When $f^{\star}$ is known exactly and $f$ is convex, the Polyak step guarantees $\|x_{k+1} - x^{\star}\|^2 \leq \|x_k - x^{\star}\|^2 - \alpha_k\,\|g_k\|^2$.  For a quadratic, the step $\alpha_k = (f(x_k)-f^{\star})/\|g_k\|^2$ produces the exact minimiser along the gradient direction, achieving fast (in some cases one-step) convergence.  However, if $f^{\star}$ is \emph{underestimated} (set to 0 when the true minimum is $\approx 0.48$), the step size $\alpha_k \to \infty$ as $\|g_k\| \to 0$, causing divergence.

\textbf{Heavy Ball physical interpretation and optimal parameters.}  The Heavy Ball update $z_{k+1} = \beta z_k + \alpha \nabla f(x_k)$, $x_{k+1} = x_k - z_{k+1}$ is directly analogous to the equations of motion for a heavy ball rolling on the loss surface with friction coefficient $(1-\beta)$: the ball accumulates velocity $z_k$ in the direction of the gradient, and friction prevents it from accelerating forever.  For a $\mu$-strongly convex, $L$-smooth quadratic, Heavy Ball with optimal $\alpha^* = \bigl(\frac{2}{\sqrt{L}+\sqrt{\mu}}\bigr)^2$ and $\beta^* = \bigl(\frac{\sqrt{L}-\sqrt{\mu}}{\sqrt{L}+\sqrt{\mu}}\bigr)^2$ converges at rate $(1 - 2/(\sqrt{\kappa}+1))^k$, which is $O(\sqrt{\kappa})$ times faster than GD.  In our experiments, the parameters $\alpha = 0.035$, $\beta = 0.90$ for Benchmark B approach this optimal setting for $\kappa \approx 5$ ($\alpha^* \approx 0.033$, $\beta^* \approx 0.39$); larger $\beta = 0.90$ was used for smoother convergence at the cost of some oscillation.

\textbf{Adagrad cumulative regret} \cite{duchi2011}.  Adagrad's diagonal scaling $\alpha_0 / \sqrt{G_k + \varepsilon}$ adapts to the geometry of the problem: coordinates with historically large gradients receive smaller effective steps.  This is beneficial for sparse problems but can cause premature slowdown on dense, smooth objectives.

Note that the GD step sizes are chosen below $1/L$ for stability: $\alpha = 0.0012$ for Benchmark C is required because $L \approx 1265$ near the starting point $(-1,1)$.  In practice, instead of a fixed $\alpha$, one would use \emph{backtracking line search}: starting from a trial step $\alpha_0$, reduce by a factor $\rho < 1$ until the Armijo condition $f(x_k - \alpha \nabla f(x_k)) \leq f(x_k) - c_1 \alpha \|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2$ is satisfied for some $c_1 \in (0,1)$.  This automatically adapts to the local Lipschitz constant without requiring $L$ to be known globally.

\textbf{Key finding:} The Polyak step size is critically sensitive to $f^{\star}$ accuracy. On Benchmarks A and B where the true minimum is non-zero but $f^{\star}=0$ is specified, the method diverges because the step $\alpha_k = (f(x_k)-f^{\star})/\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2$ grows unboundedly as the gradient vanishes. On Benchmark C where $f^{\star}=0$ is exact, it achieves superior convergence. This demonstrates the practical limitation of Polyak's step: its optimality relies on exact knowledge of $f^{\star}$, which is rarely available.

Adaptive methods like Polyak and RMSprop automatically reduce the step near high-curvature regions, partially alleviating this constraint.

\subsection{Per-Coordinate Adaptive Step-Size Behaviour}

A key feature of Adagrad and RMSprop is their \emph{per-coordinate} adaptivity.  Figure~\ref{fig:q1_percoord} demonstrates how each method independently adapts the step size for the $x_1$ direction (Hessian eigenvalue $\approx 2$) and the $x_2$ direction (Hessian eigenvalue $= 10$).

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/q1_adaptive_percoord_B.pdf}
\caption{Q1: Per-coordinate effective step sizes for Adagrad and RMSprop on Benchmark B (left) and their convergence (right). Adagrad assigns a larger effective step to coordinate $x_1$ (low curvature $\approx 2$) and a smaller step to coordinate $x_2$ (high curvature $= 10$). This automatic scaling prefers directions with historically small gradients, effectively acting as a diagonal preconditioner. RMSprop exhibits the same qualitative behaviour but with a smoother decay due to exponential forgetting ($\beta = 0.9$). The convergence plot confirms both methods reach $f^{\star} = 0.7244$ efficiently, with RMSprop maintaining a larger step near the minimum (avoiding the over-shrinkage that can stall Adagrad).}
\label{fig:q1_percoord}
\end{figure}

The ratio of effective step sizes between the two coordinates reflects the ratio of their historical gradient magnitudes.  For Adagrad, after $k$ iterations:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\alpha_k^{(1)}}{\alpha_k^{(2)}} = \sqrt{\frac{G_k^{(2)}}{G_k^{(1)}}} \approx \sqrt{\frac{(g^{(2)})^2}{(g^{(1)})^2}} = \frac{|g^{(2)}|}{|g^{(1)}|} \approx \frac{10}{2} = 5,
\end{equation}
so Adagrad implicitly gives a 5:1 step-size ratio matching the inverse curvature ratio---a form of diagonal preconditioning.

\subsection{Comparative Analysis}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Convergence speed:} On well-conditioned problems (A, B), all correctly-specified methods reach machine precision quickly; the differences are negligible. On ill-conditioned Rosenbrock (C), Polyak (correct $f^{\star}$) $\gg$ Heavy Ball $>$ Adagrad $>$ RMSprop $\approx$ GD.
\item \textbf{Robustness:} Adagrad and Heavy Ball are most robust---they never diverge and perform at or above GD on all benchmarks. Polyak is brittle: optimal when $f^{\star}$ is known, catastrophic otherwise.
\item \textbf{Stability mechanism:} GD has the simplest stability analysis ($\alpha \leq 1/L$). Adagrad is unconditionally stable for convex problems regardless of $\alpha_0$ because the effective step $\alpha_0/\sqrt{G_k}$ decays to zero. RMSprop is conditionally stable, requiring tuning of $\alpha_0$ and $\beta$.
\item \textbf{Diagonal preconditioning:} Both Adagrad and RMSprop can be interpreted as gradient descent with a diagonal preconditioner $D_k^{-1/2}$ where $D_k$ accumulates squared gradient information.  This is an approximation to Newton's method (which uses the full Hessian as preconditioner) restricted to diagonal matrices.  The approximation quality depends on how well the Hessian is diagonally dominant---on Benchmark B with diagonal Hessian, the approximation is exact.
\item \textbf{Practical implication:} In practice, exact $f^{\star}$ is rarely known, limiting Polyak's applicability. RMSprop and Adagrad are widely used in deep learning where the loss landscape is non-convex and exact curvature information is unavailable.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Algorithm Formulations}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Nesterov Momentum / Accelerated Gradient}
\label{alg:nesterov}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, max momentum $\beta_{\max}$, iterations $N$
\State $z_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 1,2,\ldots,N$}
    \State $\beta_k \gets \min\!\left(\dfrac{k-1}{k+2},\,\beta_{\max}\right)$ \Comment{Increasing momentum schedule}
    \State $\tilde{x}_k \gets x_k + \beta_k\,z_k$ \Comment{Lookahead point}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets \beta_k\,z_k - \alpha\,\nabla f(\tilde{x}_k)$ \Comment{Update velocity at lookahead}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k + z_{k+1}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The critical distinction from Heavy Ball (Algorithm~\ref{alg:heavyball}) is the \emph{lookahead}: the gradient is evaluated at $\tilde{x}_k = x_k + \beta_k z_k$ rather than $x_k$.  This corrective gradient evaluation at the anticipated next position allows Nesterov's method to counteract overshoot before it occurs, thereby achieving a faster convergence rate.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Adam Optimiser}
\label{alg:adam}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, learning rate $\alpha$, moments $\beta_1,\beta_2 \in (0,1)$, tolerance $\varepsilon$, iterations $N$
\State $m_0 \gets \mathbf{0},\quad v_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$t = 1,2,\ldots,N$}
    \State $g_t \gets \nabla f(x_t)$
    \State $m_t \gets \beta_1\,m_{t-1} + (1-\beta_1)\,g_t$ \Comment{First moment (momentum)}
    \State $v_t \gets \beta_2\,v_{t-1} + (1-\beta_2)\,g_t \odot g_t$ \Comment{Second moment (adaptive scaling)}
    \State $\hat{m}_t \gets m_t/(1-\beta_1^t)$ \Comment{Bias-corrected first moment}
    \State $\hat{v}_t \gets v_t/(1-\beta_2^t)$ \Comment{Bias-corrected second moment}
    \State $x_{t+1} \gets x_t - \alpha\,\hat{m}_t/(\sqrt{\hat{v}_t}+\varepsilon)$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Adam \cite{kingma2014} combines the gradient momentum of Nesterov/Heavy Ball with the per-coordinate adaptive scaling of RMSprop.  The bias correction terms $(1-\beta_1^t)^{-1}$ and $(1-\beta_2^t)^{-1}$ counteract the initialisation at zero: without them, $m_1 = (1-\beta_1)g_1 \ll g_1$ for $\beta_1 \approx 0.9$, which would cause under-stepping in early iterations.

\subsection{Theoretical Analysis}
\label{sec:q2_theory}

\begin{theorem}[Nesterov acceleration \cite{nesterov1983}]
\label{thm:nesterov}
For an $L$-smooth convex function $f$, the Nesterov accelerated gradient with step $\alpha = 1/L$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}
f(x_k) - f^{\star} \leq \frac{2L\|x_0 - x^{\star}\|^2}{(k+1)^2} = O\!\left(\frac{1}{k^2}\right).
\end{equation}
This is \emph{optimal} among all first-order methods; gradient descent achieves only $O(1/k)$.
\end{theorem}

The $O(1/k^2)$ rate is achieved by the momentum schedule $\beta_k = (k-1)/(k+2) \to 1$, which increases the effective step progressively.  For the capped version $\beta_k = \min((k-1)/(k+2), \beta_{\max})$ used in our experiments, the asymptotic rate may be slightly slower but typically maintains the practical advantage shown in Figure~\ref{fig:q2_convergence_rates}.

\textbf{Lower-bound optimality.}  The $O(1/k^2)$ rate is not just fast---it is \emph{optimal} for first-order methods on smooth convex functions.  Nesterov's 1983 lower bound construction shows that for any first-order algorithm (that may use all previous gradients), there exists an $L$-smooth convex function such that:
\begin{equation}
f(x_k) - f^\star \geq \frac{3L\|x_0 - x^\star\|^2}{32(k+1)^2},
\end{equation}
matching (up to a constant) the upper bound of Theorem~\ref{thm:nesterov}.  The proof uses a specially constructed ``worst-case'' tridiagonal quadratic function where information about new gradient components is revealed one at a time.  Any first-order method that achieves $o(1/k^2)$ convergence on this function would contradict the lower bound, so Nesterov's method is \emph{information-theoretically optimal} in the first-order oracle model.

This optimality is what makes Nesterov acceleration theoretically significant: unlike Heavy Ball (which is not provably optimal) or gradient descent (which has a $1/\kappa$ gap from optimality for strongly convex problems), Nesterov closes the gap between the algorithmic complexity and the information-theoretic lower bound.

\textbf{Adam's non-convex convergence.}  Adam does not generally achieve a clean $O(1/k)$ or $O(1/k^2)$ rate for non-convex problems.  For non-convex $L$-smooth objectives, Adam with step size $\alpha_t = \alpha/\sqrt{t}$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T \mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\nabla f(x_t)\|^2\right] \leq O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\right),
\end{equation}
establishing convergence to a first-order stationary point (not necessarily a local minimum).  The per-coordinate scaling of Adam can \emph{slow convergence} relative to SGD in certain non-convex settings (the ``Adam diverges'' phenomenon noted by Reddi et al., 2018 for fixed $\beta_2$), which explains why Adam underperforms Nesterov on Rosenbrock despite its adaptive scaling.  For convex problems, Adam with decreasing learning rates achieves $O(\log T / \sqrt{T})$ regret, competitive with Adagrad \cite{kingma2014}.

\textbf{Mini-batch SGD noise floor.}  For the linear regression problem with noise $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$, the expected loss at the optimum is:
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[J(\theta^{\star})] = \frac{\sigma^2}{2},
\end{equation}
so scaling $\sigma \to 6\sigma$ raises the noise floor by a factor of $36$: from $J^{\star} \approx 0.5/2 = 0.25$ (approximately, $0.4826$ observed) to $\approx 0.4826 \times 36 \approx 17.4$ (observed $\approx 18$, consistent with $\sigma^2 = 36$).  Mini-batch SGD's gradient variance per update is $\text{Var}[g_{\text{batch}}] = \sigma^2_{\text{grad}} / b$, so larger batches reduce this noise but do not eliminate the irreducible noise floor.

\subsection{Mini-Batch Stochastic Gradient Descent}
\label{sec:q2_sgd}

Mini-batch SGD on Benchmark A uses learning rate $\alpha = 0.06$, batch sizes $b \in \{5, 40\}$, 50 epochs, with data shuffled at each epoch.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Mini-Batch SGD}
\label{alg:sgd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Data $(X, y)$, initial $\theta_0$, learning rate $\alpha$, batch size $b$, epochs $E$
\For{$e = 1,2,\ldots,E$}
    \State Randomly permute indices $\{1,\ldots,m\}$
    \For{each mini-batch $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \{1,\ldots,m\}$ of size $b$}
        \State $g_{\mathcal{B}} \gets \frac{1}{b} X_{\mathcal{B}}^T(X_{\mathcal{B}}\theta - y_{\mathcal{B}})$ \Comment{Mini-batch gradient}
        \State $\theta \gets \theta - \alpha\,g_{\mathcal{B}}$
    \EndFor
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\textbf{Variance reduction: SVRG.}  A key limitation of mini-batch SGD is the \emph{variance floor}: even with constant step size, gradient noise prevents convergence below $O(\alpha \sigma^2 / b)$.  SVRG (Stochastic Variance Reduced Gradient) \cite{nocedal2006} eliminates this by periodically computing a full gradient snapshot $\tilde{\mu}$ and using it to de-bias each stochastic gradient:

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{SVRG (Stochastic Variance Reduced Gradient)}
\label{alg:svrg}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Data $(X,y)$, initial $\theta_0$, step size $\eta$, inner-loop length $m$, outer epochs $S$
\For{$s = 0,1,\ldots,S-1$}
    \State $\tilde{\theta} \gets \theta_s$ \Comment{Snapshot of current iterate}
    \State $\tilde{\mu} \gets \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla f_i(\tilde{\theta})$ \Comment{Full gradient at snapshot: one pass, $O(nd)$}
    \State $w_0 \gets \theta_s$
    \For{$t = 0,1,\ldots,m-1$}
        \State Sample $i_t$ uniformly from $\{1,\ldots,n\}$
        \State $v_t \gets \nabla f_{i_t}(w_t) - \nabla f_{i_t}(\tilde{\theta}) + \tilde{\mu}$ \Comment{Variance-reduced estimator}
        \State $w_{t+1} \gets w_t - \eta\,v_t$
    \EndFor
    \State $\theta_{s+1} \gets \frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=0}^{m-1} w_t$ \Comment{Option A: average; Option B: $w_m$}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The estimator $v_t = \nabla f_{i_t}(w_t) - \nabla f_{i_t}(\tilde{\theta}) + \tilde{\mu}$ is unbiased ($\mathbb{E}[v_t] = \nabla f(w_t)$) and has variance $\mathbb{E}[\|v_t - \nabla f(w_t)\|^2] \leq 4L(f(w_t) - f^\star + f(\tilde{\theta}) - f^\star)$, which shrinks as the iterates approach $\theta^\star$.  This contracting variance enables SVRG to achieve \emph{geometric} convergence $\mathbb{E}[f(\theta_s) - f^\star] \leq \rho^s (f(\theta_0) - f^\star)$ for strongly convex $f$---the same rate as full gradient descent---while using only $O(n + m)$ gradient evaluations per outer epoch rather than $O(nm)$.

\textbf{Step size and convergence.}  With constant learning rate $\alpha$, mini-batch SGD converges to within a neighbourhood of $\theta^{\star}$ of radius $O(\alpha \sigma^2 / b)$, where $\sigma^2$ is the gradient variance. For exact convergence, a \emph{diminishing step sequence} $\alpha_t = c/t$ (or $c/\sqrt{t}$) is required; the \emph{Polyak--Ruppert averaging} scheme maintains a running average $\bar{\theta}_t = \frac{1}{t}\sum_{s=1}^t \theta_s$, which converges at the optimal $O(1/\sqrt{t})$ rate for convex objectives even with constant step size. In our experiments, constant $\alpha = 0.06$ is used, which converges to the noise floor without explicit averaging.

\subsection{Local Taylor Approximations of $g(x) = x^4$}

The first- and second-order Taylor approximations around $x_0 = 0.25$ are:
\begin{align}
g(x) &\approx g(x_0) + g'(x_0)(x-x_0) = 0.0039 + 0.0625\,(x-0.25), \label{eq:taylor1}\\
g(x) &\approx g(x_0) + g'(x_0)(x-x_0) + \tfrac{1}{2}g''(x_0)(x-x_0)^2 = 0.0039 + 0.0625\,(x-0.25) + 0.375\,(x-0.25)^2. \label{eq:taylor2}
\end{align}
using $g(0.25) = 0.25^4 = 0.0039$, $g'(0.25) = 4 \times 0.25^3 = 0.0625$, $g''(0.25) = 12 \times 0.25^2 = 0.75$.  The Newton step minimises~(\ref{eq:taylor2}) by setting its derivative to zero: $g'(x_0) + g''(x_0)(x-x_0) = 0$, giving:
\begin{equation}
x_1 = x_0 - \frac{g'(x_0)}{g''(x_0)} = 0.25 - \frac{0.0625}{0.75} = 0.25 - 0.0833 = 0.1667.
\end{equation}
This is far closer to the true minimum $x^{\star} = 0$ than the gradient-descent step $x_0 - \alpha g'(x_0) = 0.25 - 0.0625\alpha$ for any $\alpha \leq 1$.

\caption{Q3: Local approximations of $g(x) = x^4$ at $x_0 = 0.25$. The first-order tangent line deviates rapidly from the true function, providing only directional information. The second-order parabola captures the local curvature and remains a faithful surrogate in a neighbourhood of $x_0$. The minimum of the parabola $x = x_0 - g'/g'' = 0.1667$ is a significantly better estimate of the true minimiser ($x^{\star} = 0$) than any gradient step, motivating Newton's method as a sequence of local quadratic minimisations.}

\subsection{Newton's Method with Hessian Regularisation}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Newton's Method with Regularised Hessian}
\label{alg:newton}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha \in (0,1]$, damping $\lambda \geq 0$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0,1,\ldots,N-1$}

    \State $H_k \gets \nabla^2 f(x_k) + \lambda I$ \Comment{Regularise to ensure positive definiteness}
    \State $p_k \gets H_k^{-1}\,g_k$ \Comment{Newton direction (solve linear system)}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\,p_k$ \Comment{Damped Newton step}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Convergence Theory}
\label{sec:q3_theory}

\begin{theorem}[Quadratic convergence of Newton's method, \cite{nocedal2006}]
Let $f$ be twice continuously differentiable with $L$-Lipschitz Hessian.  If $x_0$ is sufficiently close to a non-degenerate local minimum $x^{\star}$ (where $\nabla^2 f(x^{\star}) \succ 0$), then Newton's method with $\alpha = 1$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}
\|x_{k+1} - x^{\star}\| \leq C\,\|x_k - x^{\star}\|^2,
\end{equation}
for some constant $C > 0$, implying the number of correct decimal digits approximately doubles at each iteration.
\end{theorem}

\textbf{Exact one-step convergence on quadratics.}  For $J(\theta) = \frac{1}{2}\theta^T H \theta - b^T\theta + c$ (Benchmark A with $H = \frac{1}{m}X^TX$, constant Hessian), Newton's step is:
\begin{equation}
\theta_1 = \theta_0 - H^{-1}\nabla J(\theta_0) = \theta_0 - H^{-1}(H\theta_0 - b) = H^{-1}b = \theta^{\star}.
\end{equation}
The Newton direction $p_k = H^{-1}g_k$ exactly cancels the quadratic error in one step, regardless of the starting point or condition number.  This is why Figure~\ref{fig:q3_fval_A} shows Newton reaching the minimum at iteration 1.

In high-dimensional problems (e.g.\ deep neural networks with $d \sim 10^6$ to $10^9$ parameters), the $O(d^2)$ Hessian storage and $O(d^3)$ solve are entirely prohibitive, motivating \emph{quasi-Newton methods}.

\textbf{L-BFGS two-loop recursion.}  L-BFGS approximates the inverse Hessian $H_k^{-1}$ implicitly using the last $r$ \emph{curvature pairs} $\{(s_j, y_j)\}_{j=k-r}^{k-1}$, where $s_j = x_{j+1} - x_j$ (step) and $y_j = \nabla f(x_{j+1}) - \nabla f(x_j)$ (gradient change).  The BFGS secant condition $H_k s_{k-1} = y_{k-1}$ ensures the model exactly captures the most recent curvature.  The search direction $p_k = -H_k^{-1}\nabla f(x_k)$ is computed without ever forming $H_k^{-1}$ via the following recursion:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,label=\arabic*.]
  \item Initialise $q \gets \nabla f(x_k)$.
  \item \textbf{Back-sweep} (for $j = k-1, k-2, \ldots, k-r$): set $\rho_j = 1/(y_j^T s_j)$; $\alpha_j = \rho_j\,s_j^T q$; $q \gets q - \alpha_j\,y_j$.
  \item \textbf{Scale}: $q \gets \bigl(s_{k-1}^T y_{k-1}/\|y_{k-1}\|^2\bigr)\,q$ \quad (Hessian initialisation: $H_0 \approx \gamma I$, $\gamma = s^Ty/\|y\|^2$).
  \item \textbf{Forward-sweep} (for $j = k-r, k-r+1, \ldots, k-1$): $\beta = \rho_j\,y_j^T q$; $q \gets q + (\alpha_j - \beta)\,s_j$.
  \item Return $p_k = -q$.
\end{enumerate}
The cost is $O(rd)$ per direction computation---versus $O(d^3)$ for exact Newton---with no $d \times d$ matrix stored.  L-BFGS with $r \approx 5$--$20$ achieves superlinear convergence (faster than linear, sub-quadratic) and is the standard method for large-scale unconstrained smooth optimisation \cite{nocedal2006}.

\section{Question 4: Derivative Approximation and Derivative-Free Optimisation \hfill [30 marks]}
\label{sec:q4}

\subsection{Part A: Finite Differences and Random Search (Benchmark B)}

\subsubsection{Finite Difference Gradient Approximation}

The forward finite-difference approximation of the $i$-th partial derivative is:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x) \approx \frac{f(x + \delta e_i) - f(x)}{\delta}.
\label{eq:fd}
\end{equation}
By Taylor expansion, the truncation error is:
\begin{equation}
\frac{f(x+\delta e_i) - f(x)}{\delta} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i} + \frac{\delta}{2}\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i^2} + O(\delta^2),
\end{equation}
so the truncation error is $O(\delta)$.  Numerical precision introduces an error of order $\varepsilon_{\text{mach}}/\delta$ (round-off noise divided by the step, because $f(x+\delta e_i)$ and $f(x)$ are nearly equal for small $\delta$).  The total error is therefore:
\begin{equation}
\text{Error}(\delta) = \underbrace{\frac{\delta}{2}|f''|}_{\text{truncation}} + \underbrace{\frac{\varepsilon_{\text{mach}}}{\delta}}_{\text{round-off}},\quad \text{optimal at } \delta^* = \sqrt{2\varepsilon_{\text{mach}}/|f''|} \approx \sqrt{\varepsilon_{\text{mach}}} \approx 10^{-8}.
\end{equation}
The experiment uses $\delta = 0.05$ (near the truncation-dominant regime, low error) and $\delta = 0.8$ (high truncation error, poor approximation).

\textbf{Centred finite differences.}  A higher-order approximation is the centred difference:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x) \approx \frac{f(x + \delta e_i) - f(x - \delta e_i)}{2\delta},
\end{equation}
which cancels the $O(\delta)$ truncation error (the odd-order Taylor terms), achieving $O(\delta^2)$ accuracy at the cost of $2d$ function evaluations per step (instead of $d+1$ for the forward difference).  The optimal $\delta$ for centred differences is $\delta^* \approx \varepsilon_{\text{mach}}^{1/3} \approx 10^{-5.3}$.  For our experiments, both $\delta = 0.05$ and $\delta = 0.8$ lie in the truncation-dominated regime where centred differences would yield significantly better gradient estimates.

\subsubsection{Nesterov Random Search}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Nesterov Random Search (Zeroth-Order)}
\label{alg:nrs}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, perturbation radius $\delta$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0,1,\ldots,N-1$}
    \State Sample $u_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)$, normalise: $u_k \gets u_k/\|u_k\|$ (uniform on $\mathbb{S}^{d-1}$)
    \State $\hat{g}_k \gets \dfrac{f(x_k+\delta u_k) - f(x_k)}{\delta}\,u_k$ \Comment{Stochastic directional derivative}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\,\hat{g}_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

This method uses only 2 function evaluations per iteration regardless of dimension $d$.  For the experiments: $\alpha = 0.025$, $\delta = 0.08$, 220 iterations.

\textbf{Gradient estimator properties.}  The estimator $\hat{g}_k = \frac{f(x+\delta u)-f(x)}{\delta} u$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}_{u}[\hat{g}_k] = \frac{1}{d}\nabla f_\delta(x) \approx \frac{1}{d}\nabla f(x) + O(\delta^2),
\end{equation}
where $f_\delta(x) = \mathbb{E}_{u \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)}[f(x + \delta u)]$ is the Gaussian-smoothed surrogate.  The variance of the estimator is $\text{Var}[\hat{g}_k] = O(d \cdot \|\nabla f\|^2)$, which means the signal-to-noise ratio \emph{decreases with dimension}: a random direction carries $(1/d)$ of the gradient information in a $d$-dimensional space.  For $d = 2$, this is mild, but for $d = 1000$ the random search would require $\sim 1000\times$ more iterations than gradient descent.

\textbf{Oracle complexity.}  For a convex $L$-smooth function in $\mathbb{R}^d$, the Nesterov random search requires $O(dL/\varepsilon)$ function evaluations to achieve $\varepsilon$-accuracy, compared to $O(L/\varepsilon)$ for gradient descent \cite{nesterov2011}.  The factor $d$ arises precisely from the variance argument above: $d$ times as many steps are needed to average out the directional noise.

\subsection{Part B: Derivative-Free Methods on Benchmark C}

\subsubsection{Nelder--Mead Simplex Method}

The Nelder--Mead algorithm \cite{nelder1965} maintains a simplex of $n+1 = 3$ vertices in $\mathbb{R}^2$ and iteratively transforms it through four operations:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Reflection} ($\alpha_r = 1.0$): Move the worst vertex $x_{n+1}$ through the centroid $\bar{x}$ of the remaining vertices: $x_r = \bar{x} + \alpha_r(\bar{x} - x_{n+1})$.
\item \textbf{Expansion} ($\gamma = 2.0$): If reflection improved, try $x_e = \bar{x} + \gamma(\bar{x} - x_{n+1})$ and keep the better of $x_r$ and $x_e$.
\item \textbf{Contraction} ($\rho = 0.5$): If reflection worsened, contract towards the centroid.
\item \textbf{Shrink} ($\sigma = 0.5$): If contraction fails, contract all vertices towards the best.
\end{enumerate}

No gradient information is used; all decisions are based on function values at the simplex vertices.  The four operations---reflection, expansion, contraction, shrink---collectively allow the simplex to: (i) move away from regions of high function value; (ii) expand when improvement is found (aggressive search); (iii) contract when reflection fails (cautious refinement); (iv) shrink towards the best vertex when local search fails (convergence).  On well-conditioned problems, the simplex naturally elongates along the descent direction; on ill-conditioned problems like Rosenbrock, the simplex aligns itself with the narrow valley.

\textbf{Theoretical properties.}  Nelder--Mead has no general convergence guarantee for non-convex functions in $\mathbb{R}^d$ with $d > 1$.  However, on the Rosenbrock function it performs well empirically because the simplex geometry can track the curved valley.  The method requires at most $n+1 = 3$ function evaluations per iteration (one for each simplex operation attempt), and converges practically at rates comparable to gradient descent on well-posed problems.

Settings: initial simplex step $= 0.35$, 160 iterations.

\section{Question 5: Constrained Optimisation \hfill [30 marks]}
\label{sec:q5}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Problem Setup and KKT Analysis}
\label{sec:q5_kkt}

Minimise Benchmark B (Equation~\ref{eq:bench_B}) subject to the constraint $x_1 \geq 0.5$, i.e.:
\begin{equation}
\min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^2}\; f(x) \quad \text{s.t.}\quad g(x) = 0.5 - x_1 \leq 0.
\label{eq:q5_problem}
\end{equation}
The starting point $x_0 = (0.2, 4.0)$ is infeasible ($x_1 = 0.2 < 0.5$).

\textbf{KKT conditions.}  The Lagrangian is $\mathcal{L}(x,\lambda) = f(x) + \lambda\,g(x) = f(x) + \lambda(0.5-x_1)$.  At the constrained optimum $x^{\star}$:
\begin{align}
\nabla_x \mathcal{L} &= \nabla f(x^{\star}) + \lambda^{\star}\nabla g(x^{\star}) = 0, \label{eq:kkt_stat}\\
\lambda^{\star} &\geq 0, \quad g(x^{\star}) \leq 0, \quad \lambda^{\star}\,g(x^{\star}) = 0. \label{eq:kkt_comp}
\end{align}
Since $\nabla g(x) = [-1, 0]^T$, Equation~(\ref{eq:kkt_stat}) gives $[2(x_1^{\star}-1)+\cos(x_1^{\star})-\lambda^{\star},\;10(x_2^{\star}-2)]^T = 0$.  Testing the two cases:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Constraint inactive} ($x_1^{\star} > 0.5$, $\lambda^{\star} = 0$): The KKT conditions reduce to $\nabla f(x^{\star}) = 0$, yielding $x_1^{\star} \approx 0.582$, $x_2^{\star} = 2.0$.  Since $0.582 > 0.5$, feasibility holds. $\checkmark$
\item \textbf{Constraint active} ($x_1^{\star} = 0.5$, $\lambda^{\star} = 2(0.5-1)+\cos(0.5) = -1 + 0.878 = -0.122 < 0$): Violates $\lambda^{\star} \geq 0$. $\times$
\end{itemize}
Therefore the constrained optimum \emph{coincides} with the unconstrained optimum: $x^{\star} = (0.582, 2.0)$, $f^{\star} = 0.7244$.  The constraint is \emph{inactive} at the optimum.  This makes the problem a controlled test of how quickly methods recover feasibility from an infeasible starting point, since the final answer is the same as the unconstrained case.

\subsection{Algorithm Formulations}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Projected Gradient Descent}
\label{alg:pgd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, projection $\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0,1,\ldots,N-1$}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\,\nabla f(x_k)$ \Comment{Unconstrained gradient step}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets \Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(z_{k+1})$ \Comment{Project back to feasible set}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

For the constraint $x_1 \geq 0.5$, the projection is the closed-form operation $\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(z) = [\max(0.5, z_1), z_2]^T$---a single $\max$ operation, $O(1)$ per coordinate.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Quadratic Penalty Method}
\label{alg:penalty}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, penalty $\lambda > 0$, iterations $N$
\State Define $F_\lambda(x) = f(x) + \lambda\,\max(0,\;0.5-x_1)$ \Comment{Exact penalty function}
\For{$k = 0,1,\ldots,N-1$}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\,\nabla_x F_\lambda(x_k)$ \Comment{Gradient of penalised objective}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The gradient of $F_\lambda$ is:
\begin{equation}
\nabla F_\lambda(x) = \nabla f(x) + \lambda\,\nabla_x\max(0,\;0.5-x_1) = \nabla f(x) - \begin{bmatrix}\lambda\,\mathbb{1}[x_1 < 0.5] \\ 0\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Settings: Projected GD ($\alpha = 0.08$), Penalty with $\lambda \in \{0.15, 1.8, 4.5\}$ ($\alpha = 0.05$ for $\lambda \leq 1.8$; $\alpha = 0.03$ for $\lambda = 4.5$---smaller step required to handle the larger effective Lipschitz constant), unconstrained GD ($\alpha = 0.07$). All run for 100 iterations.

\subsection{Effect of Penalty Parameter $\lambda$}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/q5_penalty_lambda_sweep.pdf}
\caption{Q5: Effect of the penalty weight $\lambda$ on objective value (left) and constraint violation (right) for four values $\lambda \in \{0.15, 1.8, 4.5, 15.0\}$ with correspondingly smaller step sizes $\alpha \in \{0.05, 0.05, 0.03, 0.01\}$. Larger $\lambda$ enforces feasibility faster but requires smaller $\alpha$ to counteract the increased Lipschitz constant of $\nabla F_\lambda$: the effective smoothness constant of the penalised objective grows as $O(L_f + \lambda)$, requiring $\alpha \leq 1/(L_f + \lambda)$ for stability. The fundamental trade-off is thus: $\lambda \uparrow$ $\Rightarrow$ faster feasibility, smaller permissible step size, slower per-iteration progress. At $\lambda = 15$, feasibility is achieved in $\sim 3$ iterations, but the very small step $\alpha = 0.01$ limits optimality progress.}
\label{fig:q5_lambda_sweep}
\end{figure}

This trade-off motivates the \emph{augmented Lagrangian} (multiplier method) approach, which replaces the pure penalty $F_\lambda(x) = f(x) + \lambda g^+(x)$ with:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_\rho(x,\mu) = f(x) + \mu\,g(x) + \frac{\rho}{2}\max(0,\,g(x))^2,
\end{equation}
where $g(x) = 0.5 - x_1 \leq 0$ is our constraint. The full multiplier method alternates between minimising $\mathcal{L}_\rho$ over $x$ and updating the dual variable $\mu$:

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Augmented Lagrangian Method (Multiplier Method)}
\label{alg:alm}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, initial multiplier $\mu_0 \geq 0$, penalty parameter $\rho > 0$, outer iterations $K$
\For{$k = 0,1,\ldots,K-1$}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets \arg\min_{x}\;\mathcal{L}_\rho(x,\,\mu_k)$ \Comment{Inner minimisation (e.g.\ gradient descent on $\mathcal{L}_\rho$)}
    \State $\mu_{k+1} \gets \max\!\bigl(0,\;\mu_k + \rho\,g(x_{k+1})\bigr)$ \Comment{Dual ascent: increase multiplier if constraint violated}
    \State Optionally increase $\rho$ (e.g.\ $\rho \gets \min(10\rho,\,\rho_{\max})$) \Comment{Penalty escalation for faster feasibility}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The dual update $\mu_{k+1} = \max(0,\,\mu_k + \rho g(x_{k+1}))$ is a \emph{projected gradient ascent} step on the dual problem $\max_{\mu \geq 0} d(\mu)$.  Crucially, the dual variable $\mu_k$ captures the correct Lagrange multiplier: when $\mu_k \to \lambda^\star = 0$ (inactive constraint), the augmented Lagrangian reduces to $f(x)$ itself, and the inner minimisation recovers the exact unconstrained minimum without needing $\rho \to \infty$.  This is why the augmented Lagrangian avoids the severe ill-conditioning of the pure penalty approach while still enforcing exact feasibility.

\begin{theorem}[Projected GD convergence \cite{nocedal2006}]
\label{thm:pgd}
Let $f$ be $L$-smooth and convex, and $\mathcal{X}$ a non-empty closed convex set.  Projected gradient descent with step $\alpha = 1/L$ satisfies, for all $k \geq 1$:
\begin{equation}
f(x_k) - f^\star \leq \frac{L\|x_0 - x^\star\|^2}{2k} = O\!\left(\frac{1}{k}\right).
\end{equation}
Moreover, the distance-to-feasibility satisfies $\Pi_\mathcal{X}(x_{k+1}) = x_{k+1}$ for all $k$ (exact feasibility from iteration 1).
\end{theorem}

\textbf{Proof sketch.}  The projection $\Pi_\mathcal{X}$ is non-expansive: $\|\Pi_\mathcal{X}(z) - x^\star\| \leq \|z - x^\star\|$ for all $z$.  Applying this to $z = x_k - \alpha\nabla f(x_k)$ and using $L$-smoothness yields $\|x_{k+1} - x^\star\|^2 \leq \|x_k - x^\star\|^2 - \frac{2\alpha}{L}(f(x_k) - f^\star)$ (for $\alpha = 1/L$, the squared-distance decreases at each step).  Telescoping over $k$ gives the $O(1/k)$ rate.

The projection step $\Pi_\mathcal{X}(x_k - \alpha \nabla f(x_k))$ is guaranteed to reduce both the distance to the optimum and the constraint violation simultaneously, since $\Pi_\mathcal{X}$ is a non-expansive operator.

\textbf{Projected GD} enforces feasibility \emph{exactly and instantaneously}. The projection $\Pi_\mathcal{X}$ is both cheap ($O(d)$) and precise for box constraints, making this the method of choice when a projection oracle is available.

\textbf{Penalty methods} trade exact feasibility for algorithmic simplicity: only an unconstrained gradient step is required. The penalty weight $\lambda$ controls the feasibility--optimality trade-off: $\lambda \to \infty$ enforces feasibility exactly but makes the penalised problem increasingly ill-conditioned (the gradient of $F_\lambda$ becomes discontinuous at $x_1 = 0.5$, requiring smaller step sizes). In practice, \emph{augmented Lagrangian} methods iterate on $\lambda$ and a dual variable simultaneously, achieving exact feasibility at finite $\lambda$ while maintaining better conditioning.

\subsection{Feasible Set and Linear Programme}

Feasible set: $\mathcal{X} = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^2 : 0.5 \leq x_1 \leq 5,\; -5 \leq x_2 \leq 10\}$ (diameter $D \approx 15.66$).

\textbf{Linear Programme.}  Minimise $f(x) = a^T x = x_1 + 2x_2$ over $\mathcal{X}$ ($a = [1,2]^T$).  Since both components of $a$ are positive, the minimum is attained by setting each coordinate to its lower bound:
\begin{equation}
x^{\star} = (0.5,\,-5),\qquad f^{\star} = 0.5 + 2(-5) = -9.5.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[LP vertex optimality]
The minimum of any linear function over a bounded polytope is attained at a vertex of the polytope.
\end{theorem}

For $\mathcal{X}$ (a box), the four vertices are $(0.5,-5)$, $(5,-5)$, $(0.5,10)$, $(5,10)$ with objective values $-9.5$, $-5$, $20.5$, $25$.  The minimum $-9.5$ is achieved at $(0.5,-5)$.  $\checkmark$

\subsection{Frank--Wolfe Algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Frank--Wolfe Conditional Gradient \cite{frank1956}}
\label{alg:fw}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0 \in \mathcal{X}$, mixing parameter $\beta \in (0,1)$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0,1,\ldots,N-1$}
    \State $z_k \gets \arg\min_{z \in \mathcal{X}}\;\nabla f(x_k)^T z$ \Comment{Linear minimisation oracle (LMO)}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets \beta\,x_k + (1-\beta)\,z_k$ \Comment{Convex combination; step $\gamma = 1-\beta$}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

For box constraints $\mathcal{X} = \prod_i [l_i, u_i]$, the LMO decomposes per coordinate:
\begin{equation}
(z_k)_i = \begin{cases} l_i & \text{if } (\nabla f(x_k))_i > 0 \\ u_i & \text{if } (\nabla f(x_k))_i \leq 0 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
requiring only $O(d)$ operations per iteration---no projection oracle needed.

\textbf{Convergence guarantee.}  For convex $L$-smooth $f$ over a compact feasible set with diameter $D$, the standard Frank--Wolfe with diminishing steps $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}
f(x_k) - f^{\star} \leq \frac{2LD^2}{k+2} = O\!\left(\frac{1}{k}\right).
\end{equation}
The experiments use a \emph{fixed} $\beta$ (equivalently, $\gamma = 1-\beta$), which converges to within a residual ball of radius $O(\gamma L D^2)$ around $f^{\star}$.

\textbf{Frank--Wolfe gap.}  The duality gap $g_k = \max_{z \in \mathcal{X}} \nabla f(x_k)^T(x_k - z)$ satisfies $g_k \geq f(x_k) - f^{\star}$ for convex $f$, providing a computable certificate of optimality.

\subsection{Frank--Wolfe Duality Gap Analysis}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/q6_fw_gap.pdf}
\caption{Q6: The Frank--Wolfe duality gap $g_k = \nabla f(x_k)^T(x_k - z_k)$ (solid lines) vs.\ primal suboptimality $f(x_k) - f^{\star}$ (dashed lines). Left: interior optimum with both $\beta$ values; right: boundary optimum. The gap $g_k \geq f(x_k) - f^{\star}$ for convex $f$ (this inequality holds exactly), so the gap provides a computable \emph{upper bound} on the unknown suboptimality. In practice, terminating when $g_k < \varepsilon$ guarantees $\varepsilon$-optimality. The gap and primal suboptimality converge together, confirming the theoretical bound. For $\beta = 0.985$, both quantities are smaller at every iterate because the smaller step reduces oscillation.}
\label{fig:q6_fw_gap}
\end{figure}

The Frank--Wolfe gap is the fundamental progress measure of the algorithm. At each iteration, the gain in objective value is:
\begin{equation}
f(x_k) - f(x_{k+1}) \geq (1-\beta)\,g_k - \frac{L(1-\beta)^2 D^2}{2} = (1-\beta)\!\left[g_k - \frac{L(1-\beta)D^2}{2}\right],
\end{equation}
where $D = \|x_k - z_k\|$ is the step length.  The method makes positive progress when $g_k > L(1-\beta)D^2/2$, i.e.\ when the gap exceeds the curvature correction term.  With diminishing steps $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$ the standard analysis gives $g_k = O(1/k)$, which directly implies $f(x_k) - f^{\star} = O(1/k)$ via the gap bound.

\subsection{Convergence Theory Discussion}

\textbf{Interior vs.\ boundary behaviour.}  When the unconstrained minimum lies in the interior of $\mathcal{X}$, the LMO selects vertices of $\mathcal{X}$ that are far from the optimum, creating a ``zig-zag'' pattern as $z_k$ alternates between opposite vertices.  The convex combination damps these oscillations, but a fixed step $\gamma$ leaves a residual error proportional to $\gamma$.

When the optimum is on the boundary, the LMO consistently selects the vertex on the constrained side, providing more useful gradient information.  The algorithm converges towards the boundary naturally, without requiring a projection.  This is the key structural advantage of Frank--Wolfe over projected gradient methods for sparsity-inducing constraints (e.g., $\ell_1$ ball), where the LMO selects a sparse extreme point.

\textbf{Diminishing vs.\ fixed step size: quantitative comparison.}  The standard Frank--Wolfe uses $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$, which achieves the $O(1/k)$ rate \emph{with no asymptotic residual}: $f(x_k) - f^\star \leq 2LD^2/(k+2) \to 0$.  With fixed $\gamma = 1-\beta$, the method converges to within $O(\gamma L D^2/2)$ of $f^\star$ but not to $f^\star$ itself.

For the interior case ($f(x) = (x_1-1)^2+(x_2-5)^2$, $L = 2$, $D^2 \leq (4.5^2 + 15^2) \approx 245$):
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item Fixed $\gamma = 0.015$ ($\beta = 0.985$): residual ball $\approx 0.015 \times 2 \times 245 / 2 = 3.7$.  Observed gap $= 0.0046$ (much smaller because the effective local diameter $\|x_k - z_k\|$ contracts with the iterate).
  \item Diminishing $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$ at $k = 180$: bound $= 2 \times 2 \times 245/182 \approx 5.4$.  Asymptotically, however, the diminishing schedule guarantees exact convergence to $f^\star$ with no residual.
\end{itemize}
The practical takeaway: fixed $\gamma$ converges faster early (larger step) but stalls at a residual floor determined by the step size; diminishing $\gamma_k$ converges slower early but reaches $f^\star$ exactly.  For the experiments, fixed $\gamma$ was sufficient to confirm the $O(1/k)$ rate visually; a production implementation would use exact line search or the diminishing schedule.

For the boundary case ($\gamma = 0.07$, $L = 2$, $D^2 \approx 245$): theoretical residual ball $= 0.07 \times 2 \times 245/2 = 17.2$.  The observed gap of $\approx 0.11$ is far smaller because near the boundary optimum the effective step length $\|x_k - z_k\|$ is only $\approx \|x_k - (0.5, 0)\|$, which contracts to near zero as convergence proceeds.

\subsection{Findings Per Benchmark}

\textbf{Benchmark A (quadratic, $\kappa \approx 1$):}  All methods converge near-instantly to the noise floor $J^{\star} \approx 0.4826$.  The condition number is so favourable that the choice of algorithm is irrelevant at this scale.  Newton solves it exactly in one step.

\textbf{Benchmark B (nearly quadratic, $\kappa \approx 5$):}  The moderate anisotropy is handled well by any of the adaptive or momentum methods.  The sinusoidal perturbation introduces mild nonlinearity that GD handles easily with $\alpha = 0.06$.  Newton converges in $\sim 5$ steps.

\textbf{Benchmark C (Rosenbrock, $\kappa \approx 2004$):}  The extreme ill-conditioning and nonlinear valley structure reveal sharp algorithm differences.  Performance ranking: Newton (20 it.) $>$ Polyak (correct $f^{\star}$, 120 it.) $>$ Nesterov (150 it.) $>$ Adam (150 it.) $>$ Heavy Ball (120 it.) $>$ Adagrad (120 it.) $>$ GD $\approx$ RMSprop.

\subsection{Algorithm Design Principles}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Curvature information is decisive for ill-conditioned problems.}  Newton's method solves Benchmark C in 20 iterations where GD needs 80 and still fails; any approximation to $H^{-1}$ (Adam's diagonal, RMSprop's coordinate scaling) helps but cannot match the full second-order step.

\item \textbf{Momentum is the best first-order improvement.}  Nesterov's $O(1/k^2)$ rate outperforms all other first-order methods on all benchmarks.  For high-dimensional problems where Newton's method is prohibitive, Nesterov or L-BFGS is the practical choice.

\item \textbf{Adaptive step-size methods trade stability for accuracy.}  Adagrad never diverges but may stall on ill-conditioned problems.  RMSprop is more aggressive but requires careful tuning.  Polyak is optimal when $f^{\star}$ is known but catastrophic otherwise.

\item \textbf{Derivative-free methods are viable when gradients are unavailable.}  Nelder--Mead is practical for low-dimensional problems; Nesterov random search scales to higher dimensions with known complexity $O(d/\varepsilon)$.  Grid search provides global guarantees at exponential cost.

\item \textbf{Constrained methods require matching the constraint structure.}  Projected GD enforces hard feasibility at each step when a cheap projection exists.  Penalty methods are simpler to implement but require $\lambda \to \infty$ for exact feasibility, causing ill-conditioning.  Frank--Wolfe is ideal for linear constraints where the LMO is cheap.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Connection to Optimisation Theory}

The six questions span the core topics of the CS7DS2 course: the progression from first-order gradient methods (Q1--Q2) to second-order methods (Q3), then derivative-free methods (Q4), constrained optimisation (Q5), and finally LP-based methods (Q6) mirrors the theoretical development from basic gradient descent theory to more advanced topics.

The central tension throughout is the \textbf{cost-per-iteration vs.\ convergence-rate trade-off}, summarised in a three-level oracle hierarchy:

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Oracle complexity hierarchy: minimum iterations to reach $\varepsilon$-accuracy for smooth convex functions. The lower bounds are tight (matched by the listed method).}
\label{tab:oracle_hierarchy}
\begin{tabular}{lccl}
\toprule
\textbf{Oracle} & \textbf{Best rate} & \textbf{Achieved by} & \textbf{Per-iter cost} \\
\midrule
Zeroth-order (function values only) & $O(d/\varepsilon)$ & Nesterov random search & $O(d)$ FE \\
First-order (gradient oracle) & $O(1/\sqrt{\varepsilon})$ & Nesterov GD \cite{nesterov1983} & $O(d)$ \\
Second-order (Hessian oracle) & $O(\log\log(1/\varepsilon))$ & Newton's method & $O(d^3)$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item GD: cheapest per iteration ($O(d)$), rate $O(1/k)$ for smooth convex---a factor $O(\sqrt{\varepsilon})$ sub-optimal vs.\ the first-order lower bound.
\item Nesterov: same cost as GD, optimal rate $O(1/k^2)$---the best possible for first-order methods \cite{nesterov1983}.
\item Newton: expensive per iteration ($O(d^3)$), quadratic convergence---the best possible for second-order methods, with a qualitatively different (super-polynomial) speed advantage near the optimum.
\item Derivative-free methods: require no gradient but need $O(d)$ more iterations than gradient methods; practical only when function evaluations are cheap and gradients are unavailable.
\end{itemize}

The practical choice among these depends on: (a) problem dimension $d$; (b) availability of gradients/Hessians; (c) smoothness and convexity of the objective; (d) accuracy required; (e) computational budget.  These experiments, while in two dimensions, provide clear empirical evidence supporting the theoretical hierarchy; Figure~\ref{fig:all_methods_C} visually confirms the ordering on the most discriminating Benchmark C.

def gradient_descent(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        x = x - alpha * grad_fn(x)
        x_hist.append(x.copy()); f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)

def polyak_step(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, f_star, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        alpha_k = (loss_fn(x) - f_star) / (np.dot(g, g) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(alpha_k)
        x = x - alpha_k * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy()); f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Adagrad and RMSprop}]
def adagrad(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha0, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float); G = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x); G += g**2
        eff_alpha = alpha0 / (np.sqrt(G) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(np.mean(eff_alpha))
        x = x - eff_alpha * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy()); f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)

def rmsprop(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha0, beta, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float); v = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        v = beta * v + (1 - beta) * g**2
        eff_alpha = alpha0 / (np.sqrt(v) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(np.mean(eff_alpha))
        x = x - eff_alpha * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy()); f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Heavy Ball and Nesterov Momentum}]
def heavy_ball(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float); z = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x); z = beta * z + alpha * g
        x = x - z
        x_hist.append(x.copy()); f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)

def nesterov_momentum(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta_max, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float); z = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for k in range(1, n_iters + 1):
        beta_k = min((k - 1) / (k + 2), beta_max)
        lookahead = x + beta_k * z
        g = grad_fn(lookahead)
        z = beta_k * z - alpha * g; x = x + z
        x_hist.append(x.copy()); f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Adam Optimiser}]
def adam_optimiser(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta1, beta2, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    m_vec, v_vec = np.zeros_like(x), np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for t in range(1, n_iters + 1):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        m_vec = beta1 * m_vec + (1 - beta1) * g
        v_vec = beta2 * v_vec + (1 - beta2) * g**2
        m_hat = m_vec / (1 - beta1**t)
        v_hat = v_vec / (1 - beta2**t)
        x = x - alpha * m_hat / (np.sqrt(v_hat) + eps)
        x_hist.append(x.copy()); f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Newton's Method with Regularised Hessian}]
def newtons_method(grad_fn, hess_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, n_iters, damping=1e-8):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, update_norms = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        H = hess_fn(x) if callable(hess_fn) else hess_fn
        H_reg = H + damping * np.eye(len(x))
        try:
            p = np.linalg.solve(H_reg, g)  # More stable than direct inversion
        except np.linalg.LinAlgError:
            p = g  # Fall back to gradient direction
        update_norms.append(np.linalg.norm(alpha * p))
        x = x - alpha * p
        x_hist.append(x.copy()); f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(update_norms)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Frank--Wolfe Algorithm}]
def frank_wolfe(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, bounds, beta, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, z_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        # Linear minimisation oracle: select vertex minimising g^T z
        z = np.array([bounds[i][0] if g[i] > 0 else bounds[i][1]
                      for i in range(len(g))])
        z_hist.append(z.copy())
        x = beta * x + (1 - beta) * z  # Convex combination
        x_hist.append(x.copy()); f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(z_hist)
\end{lstlisting}
