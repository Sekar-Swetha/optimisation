\begin{abstract}
This report presents a rigorous comparative study of optimisation algorithms across three benchmark functions spanning a wide range of conditioning: a linear regression quadratic loss (condition number $\kappa \approx 1.1$), a toy neural network loss with sinusoidal perturbation ($\kappa \approx 6.9$ at the optimum), and the Rosenbrock function ($\kappa \approx 2508$ at the optimum). Six algorithm families are investigated: (1)~adaptive step-size methods (Polyak, Adagrad, RMSprop, Heavy Ball); (2)~momentum and stochastic methods (Nesterov acceleration, Adam, mini-batch SGD); (3)~second-order methods (Newton's method with Hessian regularisation); (4)~derivative-free methods (forward finite differences, Nesterov random search, Nelder--Mead simplex, grid search); (5)~constrained optimisation via projected gradient descent and quadratic penalty; and (6)~the Frank--Wolfe algorithm for linear-constrained problems. Convergence rates, trajectory behaviour, stability, and computational trade-offs are analysed. Key findings include: Polyak's step requires exact knowledge of $f^{\star}$ to avoid divergence; Nesterov acceleration achieves its theoretical $O(1/k^2)$ advantage on the Rosenbrock function; Newton's method achieves exact one-step convergence on the quadratic benchmark and outperforms all gradient-based methods on Rosenbrock with only 20~iterations; and Frank--Wolfe naturally respects constraints without projection but attains only $O(1/k)$ convergence.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================

Optimisation lies at the heart of machine learning and data analysis. Selecting an appropriate algorithm requires understanding the interplay between three factors: the \emph{geometry} of the loss landscape (curvature, conditioning), the \emph{available information} (gradients, Hessians, or only function values), and the \emph{computational budget} (iterations, function evaluations, memory). This assignment investigates six algorithm families on benchmark problems deliberately chosen to exercise different aspects of this interplay.

The condition number $\kappa = L/\mu$ --- the ratio of the largest to smallest eigenvalue of the Hessian --- is the central diagnostic. For $\kappa \approx 1$ (well-conditioned), simple gradient descent with a suitable constant step size suffices. For $\kappa \gg 1$ (ill-conditioned), gradient descent converges at rate $((\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1))^k \to 1$ and requires many iterations; curvature-aware methods become essential. The Rosenbrock function with $\kappa \approx 2508$ at its optimum provides the most severe test.

All experiments use a fixed random seed (\texttt{np.random.seed(42)}) for reproducibility.

\section{Benchmark Problems and Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:benchmarks}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Benchmark A: Linear Regression Quadratic Loss}
A synthetic dataset with $m = 1000$ samples and two parameters is generated:
\begin{equation}
\theta^{\star} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad x^{(i)} \in \mathbb{R}^2 \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I), \quad y^{(i)} = \theta^{\star T} x^{(i)} + \varepsilon^{(i)}, \quad \varepsilon^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1).
\end{equation}
The mean squared error loss is:
\begin{equation}
J(\theta) = \frac{1}{2m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \left(\theta^T x^{(i)} - y^{(i)}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{2m}\|X\theta - y\|^2.
\end{equation}
The Hessian is constant: $H = \frac{1}{m}X^T X$. With seed 42, the Hessian eigenvalues are $\lambda_{\min} \approx 0.925$ and $\lambda_{\max} \approx 1.033$, giving condition number $\kappa \approx 1.12$. This near-identity Hessian (reflecting the law of large numbers with standard Gaussian features) makes Benchmark A the easiest of the three. The noise floor is $J^{\star} \approx 0.4826$ due to observation noise $\sigma^2 = 1$. Starting point: $\theta_0 = [0, 0]^T$.

\subsection{Benchmark B: Toy Neural Network Loss}
\begin{equation}
f(x_1, x_2) = (x_1 - 1)^2 + 5(x_2 - 2)^2 + \sin(x_1).
\end{equation}
The gradient is $\nabla f = [2(x_1 - 1) + \cos(x_1),\ 10(x_2 - 2)]^T$ and the Hessian is
\begin{equation}
H(x) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 - \sin(x_1) & 0 \\ 0 & 10 \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The $x_2$-direction has constant curvature 10, while the $x_1$-direction has curvature $2 - \sin(x_1) \in [1, 3]$. At the optimum $x^{\star} \approx (0.5824, 2.0)$ (obtained by solving $2(x_1-1) + \cos(x_1) = 0$), the Hessian eigenvalues are $\lambda_1 \approx 1.45$ and $\lambda_2 = 10$, giving $\kappa \approx 6.9$. The minimum objective value is $f^{\star} \approx 0.7244$. Starting point: $x_0 = [-1, 4]^T$.

\subsection{Benchmark C: Rosenbrock Function}
\begin{equation}
f(x_1, x_2) = (1 - x_1)^2 + 100(x_2 - x_1^2)^2.
\end{equation}
The global minimum is at $(1, 1)$ with $f^{\star} = 0$. The Hessian at the optimum has eigenvalues $\lambda_{\min} \approx 0.40$ and $\lambda_{\max} \approx 1001.6$, giving $\kappa \approx 2508$. This extreme ill-conditioning arises from the factor of 100 in the second term: the $x_2 - x_1^2$ constraint creates a steep cross-valley curvature of order $200$, while the along-valley curvature is much smaller. The two challenges are:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Ill-conditioning}: the condition number $\kappa \approx 2508$ forces GD to use tiny step sizes ($\alpha \ll 1/L \approx 0.001$), making it extremely slow along the valley.
\item \textbf{Curved valley}: the valley follows $x_2 = x_1^2$ (a parabola), so the optimal descent direction rotates continuously as $x_1$ varies. Unlike an elliptic bowl (where coordinate-aligned methods work well), the rotating valley demands that methods either use curvature information (Newton) or accumulate momentum along a curved path (Nesterov).
\end{enumerate}
At the starting point $x_0 = [-1, 1]^T$, $L \approx 1002$, constraining the maximum stable GD step to $\alpha < 2/L \approx 0.002$.

\subsection{Benchmark D: Constrained Feasible Set}
For constrained problems (Questions 5 and 6):
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{X} = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^2 : 0.5 \leq x_1 \leq 5,\ -5 \leq x_2 \leq 10\}.
\end{equation}
This box constraint set has diameter $D = \|u - l\| = \|(5, 10) - (0.5, -5)\| \approx 16.2$, relevant for Frank--Wolfe rate analysis.

\section{Question 1: Adaptive Step-Size Methods \hfill [30 marks]}
\label{sec:q1}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Theoretical Background}
\label{subsec:q1_theory}

These methods were covered in lectures weeks 3b and 4a of CS7DS2. The foundational result is the gradient descent convergence theorem from lecture week 2a.

For a function $f$ that is $\mu$-strongly convex and $L$-smooth (i.e., $\mu I \preceq \nabla^2 f(x) \preceq LI$), gradient descent with constant step $\alpha = 1/L$ achieves the linear convergence guarantee:
\begin{equation}
\|x_k - x^{\star}\|^2 \leq \left(1 - \frac{\mu}{L}\right)^k \|x_0 - x^{\star}\|^2 = \left(1 - \frac{1}{\kappa}\right)^k \|x_0 - x^{\star}\|^2.
\end{equation}
For Benchmark A with $\kappa \approx 1.12$, this gives $(1-1/1.12)^k = 0.893^k$ --- excellent convergence. For Rosenbrock with $\kappa \approx 2508$, the same formula gives $(1 - 1/2508)^k \approx e^{-k/2508}$, requiring $\sim 17{,}000$ iterations to reduce error by $1000\times$, explaining why all gradient-based methods struggle on Benchmark C.

\subsection{Algorithm Formulations}

Four adaptive methods are compared against a constant step-size gradient descent baseline across all three benchmarks for 120 iterations.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Polyak Step Size}
\label{alg:polyak}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, optimal value $f^{\star}$, tolerance $\varepsilon$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $\alpha_k \gets \dfrac{f(x_k) - f^{\star}}{\|g_k\|^2 + \varepsilon}$ \Comment{Adaptive step size}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha_k\, g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The Polyak step is theoretically motivated: for any convex $f$, it satisfies
\begin{equation}
\|x_{k+1} - x^{\star}\|^2 \leq \|x_k - x^{\star}\|^2 - \frac{(f(x_k) - f^{\star})^2}{\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2}
\end{equation}
when $f^{\star}$ is exact, guaranteeing monotone descent in distance to $x^{\star}$ and a convergence rate of $O(1/k)$ \cite{polyak1969minimization}. However, this requires exact knowledge of $f^{\star}$, which is rarely available in practice.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Adagrad}
\label{alg:adagrad}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, base learning rate $\alpha_0$, tolerance $\varepsilon$, iterations $N$
\State $G_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $G_{k+1} \gets G_k + g_k \odot g_k$ \Comment{Per-coordinate gradient accumulation}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \dfrac{\alpha_0}{\sqrt{G_{k+1}} + \varepsilon} \odot g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Adagrad \cite{duchi2011adagrad} performs coordinate-wise step-size adaptation: coordinates that receive large gradients are penalised with smaller effective step sizes. This is particularly beneficial for sparse or anisotropic problems. The effective learning rate per coordinate $i$ at step $k$ is $\alpha_{k,i} = \alpha_0 / (\sqrt{\sum_{j \leq k} g_{j,i}^2} + \varepsilon)$, which decays monotonically as gradients accumulate.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{RMSprop}
\label{alg:rmsprop}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, learning rate $\alpha_0$, decay $\beta$, tolerance $\varepsilon$, iterations $N$
\State $v_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $v_{k+1} \gets \beta\, v_k + (1 - \beta)\, g_k \odot g_k$ \Comment{Exponential moving average of squared gradients}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \dfrac{\alpha_0}{\sqrt{v_{k+1}} + \varepsilon} \odot g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

RMSprop \cite{tieleman2012rmsprop} addresses Adagrad's monotonically shrinking step by replacing the full historical sum with an exponential moving average with decay $\beta \in (0,1)$. The effective memory window is approximately $1/(1-\beta)$ iterations; with $\beta = 0.9$, only the last $\sim$10 gradient magnitudes are weighted significantly. This allows the effective step size to increase when gradients have been small recently, preventing premature convergence.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Polyak Momentum / Heavy Ball}
\label{alg:heavyball}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, momentum $\beta$, iterations $N$
\State $z_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets \beta\, z_k + \alpha\, \nabla f(x_k)$ \Comment{Velocity update}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - z_{k+1}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The Heavy Ball method \cite{polyak1964momentum} augments gradient descent with a momentum term $z_k$ that accumulates gradient history. For a quadratic function with condition number $\kappa$, the optimal momentum $\beta^{\star} = ((\sqrt{\kappa}-1)/(\sqrt{\kappa}+1))^2$ yields convergence rate $((\sqrt{\kappa}-1)/(\sqrt{\kappa}+1))^{2k}$, significantly faster than GD's $((\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1))^k$ for large $\kappa$. For Benchmark C with $\kappa \approx 2508$: the optimal Heavy Ball parameters are $\alpha^{\star} = 4/(\sqrt{L}+\sqrt{\mu})^2 \approx 0.0038$ and $\beta^{\star} \approx 0.923$, giving convergence rate $\approx 0.923^k$, compared to GD's $0.9996^k$. The code uses more conservative settings ($\alpha = 0.0008$, $\beta = 0.86$) to ensure stability on the non-quadratic Rosenbrock, explaining the experimental gap between Heavy Ball and the theoretical quadratic optimum.

\subsection{Comparative Analysis and Discussion}

The performance hierarchy across benchmarks reveals a clear pattern: on Benchmark A ($\kappa \approx 1.1$), nearly all methods converge well and the differences are minor. On Benchmark C ($\kappa \approx 2508$), the ranking reflects each method's ability to exploit or overcome the severe ill-conditioning.

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Polyak step:} Theoretically optimal when $f^{\star}$ is known exactly (achieves $O(1/k)$ convergence for convex $f$), but completely impractical when $f^{\star}$ is misspecified. In the quadratic noisy regression setting, the irreducible noise residual $J^{\star} \approx \sigma^2/(2) \approx 0.4826 > 0$ invalidates the $f^{\star} = 0$ assumption, causing divergence. This is a critical practical limitation: in supervised learning, the noise floor is never known a priori.

\item \textbf{Adagrad:} Uniformly reliable across all benchmarks: its monotonically decreasing per-coordinate step size provides natural regularisation against curvature imbalances, though it risks premature slowing on non-stationary or long-horizon problems. It is well-suited to sparse gradient regimes (online learning).

\item \textbf{RMSprop:} Trades Adagrad's theoretical guarantees for empirical flexibility through exponential forgetting. The base rate $\alpha_0$ must be set per-problem; with a very conservative $\alpha_0$ on Rosenbrock it cannot recover.

\item \textbf{Heavy Ball:} The most competitive first-order method on Rosenbrock, demonstrating that the momentum term builds along the consistent valley-floor direction. On the well-conditioned Benchmark A it matches GD, so the momentum overhead is wasted.

\item \textbf{Practical implication:} When $f^{\star}$ is unknown (the typical case), Adagrad or Heavy Ball are safer choices than Polyak. The optimal momentum tuning for Heavy Ball requires knowledge of $\kappa$, which is also often unknown in practice.
\end{itemize}

\section{Question 2: Momentum and Stochastic Methods \hfill [30 marks]}
\label{sec:q2}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Theoretical Background}
\label{subsec:q2_theory}

Nesterov acceleration and Adam were covered in lecture week 4b of CS7DS2; mini-batch SGD was covered in week 5a.

Nesterov \cite{nesterov1983method} proved that for convex, $L$-smooth functions, the optimal convergence rate achievable by any first-order method using only gradients is $O(L\|x_0 - x^{\star}\|^2/k^2)$. Standard GD achieves $O(1/k)$ --- one order slower. Nesterov acceleration matches the $O(1/k^2)$ lower bound, making it theoretically optimal. For $\mu$-strongly convex functions, the accelerated rate is $(1 - \sqrt{\mu/L})^k$ versus GD's $(1-\mu/L)^k$; since $\sqrt{\mu/L} \gg \mu/L$ for small $\mu/L$, this is a substantial improvement when $\kappa$ is large.

\subsection{Algorithm Formulations}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Nesterov Momentum / Acceleration}
\label{alg:nesterov}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, max momentum $\beta_{\max}$, iterations $N$
\State $z_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 1, 2, \ldots, N$}
    \State $\beta_k \gets \min\!\left(\frac{k-1}{k+2},\ \beta_{\max}\right)$ \Comment{Adaptive momentum schedule}
    \State $\tilde{x}_k \gets x_k + \beta_k\, z_k$ \Comment{Lookahead point}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets \beta_k\, z_k - \alpha\, \nabla f(\tilde{x}_k)$ \Comment{Velocity update at lookahead}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k + z_{k+1}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The key distinction from Heavy Ball is the \emph{lookahead}: the gradient is evaluated at $\tilde{x}_k = x_k + \beta_k z_k$ rather than $x_k$. This corrects the trajectory before overshoot occurs, enabling the $O(1/k^2)$ acceleration. Intuitively, by moving to the lookahead point $\tilde{x}_k$ and evaluating the gradient there, Nesterov anticipates where momentum would carry the iterate and adjusts before committing; this is why it avoids the oscillation that affects Heavy Ball. The schedule $\beta_k = (k-1)/(k+2)$ satisfies the condition $t_k^2 \leq t_{k+1}^2 - t_{k+1}$ (where $t_k = (k+1)/2$) that underlies the $O(1/k^2)$ proof. At $k = 1$, $\beta_1 = 0$ (pure gradient step); at $k = 150$, $\beta_{150} \approx 0.98$, providing very strong momentum acceleration in the late phase.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Adam Optimiser}
\label{alg:adam}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, learning rate $\alpha$, moments $\beta_1, \beta_2$, tolerance $\varepsilon$, iterations $N$
\State $m_0 \gets \mathbf{0},\ v_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$t = 1, 2, \ldots, N$}
    \State $g_t \gets \nabla f(x_t)$
    \State $m_t \gets \beta_1\, m_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_1)\, g_t$ \Comment{First moment (momentum)}
    \State $v_t \gets \beta_2\, v_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_2)\, g_t \odot g_t$ \Comment{Second moment (RMSprop)}
    \State $\hat{m}_t \gets m_t\, /\, (1 - \beta_1^t)$ \Comment{Bias correction}
    \State $\hat{v}_t \gets v_t\, /\, (1 - \beta_2^t)$
    \State $x_{t+1} \gets x_t - \alpha\, \hat{m}_t\, /\, (\sqrt{\hat{v}_t} + \varepsilon)$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Adam \cite{kingma2015adam} unifies momentum (via $m_t$, analogous to Heavy Ball) with per-coordinate adaptive scaling (via $v_t$, analogous to RMSprop). The bias correction terms $(1-\beta_1^t)$ and $(1-\beta_2^t)$ compensate for the zero initialisation of $m_0$ and $v_0$, which would otherwise bias early iterates toward zero. In the limit $\beta_1 = 0$, Adam reduces to RMSprop; in the limit $\beta_2 = 1$, Adam reduces to gradient descent with momentum. Critically, Adam's per-coordinate scaling adapts to \emph{axis-aligned} anisotropy (different curvatures in $x_1$ vs $x_2$) but does not exploit \emph{off-diagonal} curvature correlations. The Rosenbrock function has significant off-diagonal Hessian entries ($H_{12} = -400 x_1 \approx -400$ near the valley), which Adam's diagonal preconditioning cannot address. Nesterov momentum, by contrast, builds a directional bias along the valley floor regardless of coordinate alignment, explaining its superior performance on this benchmark.

\subsection{Mini-Batch Stochastic Gradient Descent}

Mini-batch SGD on Benchmark A uses learning rate $\alpha = 0.06$, batch sizes $b \in \{5, 40\}$, 50 epochs, with data shuffled at every epoch.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Mini-Batch SGD}
\label{alg:sgd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Data $(X, y)$, initial $\theta_0$, learning rate $\alpha$, batch size $b$, epochs $E$
\For{$e = 1, 2, \ldots, E$}
    \State Randomly permute data indices
    \For{each mini-batch $\mathcal{B}$ of size $b$}
        \State $g \gets \frac{1}{b} X_{\mathcal{B}}^T(X_{\mathcal{B}}\theta - y_{\mathcal{B}})$ \Comment{Stochastic gradient}
        \State $\theta \gets \theta - \alpha\, g$
    \EndFor
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The stochastic gradient $g_{\mathcal{B}} = \frac{1}{b}X_{\mathcal{B}}^T(X_{\mathcal{B}}\theta - y_{\mathcal{B}})$ is an unbiased estimate of the full gradient $\nabla J(\theta)$, with variance $\mathrm{Var}[g_{\mathcal{B}}] \propto \sigma^2/(bm)$ for batch size $b$. Per epoch, the number of parameter updates is $m/b$: $b=5$ gives 200 updates per epoch with high variance; $b=40$ gives 25 updates with lower variance. The total SGD error in the strongly convex setting decomposes as:
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[J(\theta_k)] - J^{\star} \approx \underbrace{(1-\mu\alpha)^k(J(\theta_0) - J^{\star})}_{\text{optimisation bias (decays)}} + \underbrace{\frac{\alpha \sigma^2}{2\mu b}}_{\text{variance floor (constant)}}.
\end{equation}
The variance floor is proportional to $\sigma^2/b$: smaller batches have a higher floor, which manifests as greater oscillation near the minimum.

\subsection{Local Approximations of $g(x) = x^4$}
\label{subsec:q3_approx}

Newton's method and quasi-Newton approaches were covered in lecture week 6a of CS7DS2. The key insight is that gradient descent is a first-order method (uses only $\nabla f$), while Newton's method is a second-order method (uses $\nabla^2 f$), achieving qualitatively better convergence at the cost of $O(d^2)$ Hessian storage and $O(d^3)$ linear solve per step.

The Taylor expansion of $g(x) = x^4$ around $x_0 = 0.25$:

\textbf{First-order approximation} (tangent line):
\begin{equation}
\hat{g}_1(x) = g(x_0) + g'(x_0)(x - x_0) = (0.25)^4 + 4(0.25)^3(x - 0.25) = 0.003906 + 0.0625(x - 0.25).
\end{equation}

\textbf{Second-order approximation} (osculating parabola):
\begin{equation}
\hat{g}_2(x) = g(x_0) + g'(x_0)(x - x_0) + \tfrac{1}{2}g''(x_0)(x - x_0)^2 = 0.003906 + 0.0625(x - 0.25) + 0.375(x - 0.25)^2.
\end{equation}

The Newton step minimises $\hat{g}_2$ by setting its derivative to zero:
\begin{equation}
g'(x_0) + g''(x_0)(x - x_0) = 0 \implies x_1 = x_0 - \frac{g'(x_0)}{g''(x_0)} = 0.25 - \frac{0.0625}{0.75} = 0.1667.
\end{equation}
The true minimum of $g(x) = x^4$ is at $x^{\star} = 0$; the Newton step from $x_0 = 0.25$ moves to $x_1 = 0.167$, reducing the error from 0.25 to 0.167 (a 33\% reduction in one step). For smooth, strongly convex functions, subsequent Newton steps converge quadratically.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/q3_approximation.pdf}
\caption{Q3: Local approximations of $g(x) = x^4$ at $x_0 = 0.25$. The first-order (tangent) approximation correctly captures the slope but deviates rapidly due to the quartic's high curvature. The second-order (parabolic) approximation fits well in a neighbourhood of $x_0$; its minimum at $x_1 = 0.167$ provides a much better estimate than a gradient step. This illustrates why Newton's method, which moves to the minimiser of the quadratic model, often outperforms gradient descent: the curvature information directs the step more accurately.}

\subsection{Newton's Method}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Newton's Method with Tikhonov Damping}
\label{alg:newton}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, regularisation $\lambda_{\mathrm{damp}}$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $H_k \gets \nabla^2 f(x_k) + \lambda_{\mathrm{damp}} I$ \Comment{Tikhonov regularisation ensures positive definiteness}
    \State $p_k \gets H_k^{-1}\, g_k$ \Comment{Newton direction}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, p_k$ \Comment{Damped Newton step}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The Newton direction $p_k = H_k^{-1} g_k$ satisfies $H_k p_k = g_k$, solved via Cholesky factorisation (\texttt{np.linalg.solve} with regularised Hessian). Tikhonov damping $\lambda_{\mathrm{damp}} = 10^{-8}$ ensures numerical positive definiteness even when $H_k$ is near-singular (as on Rosenbrock away from the optimum). The step size $\alpha \leq 1$ provides additional damping for nonlinear functions where the quadratic model is only locally accurate.

\begin{proposition}[Newton convergence]
For $f$ twice continuously differentiable with $\nabla^2 f$ Lipschitz near $x^{\star}$, and for $x_0$ sufficiently close to $x^{\star}$, undamped Newton's method ($\alpha = 1$) converges \emph{quadratically}:
\begin{equation}
\|x_{k+1} - x^{\star}\| \leq C \|x_k - x^{\star}\|^2
\end{equation}
for some constant $C > 0$ depending on the Lipschitz constant of $\nabla^2 f$ and $\|(\nabla^2 f(x^{\star}))^{-1}\|$.
\end{proposition}

For quadratic $f$, Newton with $\alpha = 1$ achieves the exact minimiser in a single iteration because the local quadratic model $\hat{f}(x) = f(x_k) + g_k^T(x-x_k) + \frac{1}{2}(x-x_k)^T H_k (x-x_k)$ is \emph{exact} (equals $f$) everywhere, not just locally. Thus the Newton step minimising $\hat{f}$ minimises $f$ exactly: $x_1 = x_0 - H^{-1}\nabla f(x_0) = H^{-1}b$ where $f(\theta) = \frac{1}{2}\theta^T H\theta - b^T\theta + c$. Numerically, one observes $f(x_1) = J^{\star}$ to machine precision after a single iteration on Benchmark A (confirmed: $f(x_1) = 0.4826$ after one Newton step from $(0,0)$), with subsequent iterations making no further progress.

\subsection{Limitations and Quasi-Newton Extensions}

Newton's method requires computing and factorising the $d \times d$ Hessian at each step, costing $O(d^2)$ storage and $O(d^3)$ computation. For large-scale problems ($d \sim 10^6$ in modern machine learning), this is prohibitive. \emph{Quasi-Newton methods} bridge the gap between first- and second-order methods by constructing a low-cost Hessian approximation from gradient differences. The most widely used is L-BFGS (Limited-memory Broyden--Fletcher--Goldfarb--Shanno), which maintains a rank-$m$ approximation to $H^{-1}$ using only the last $m$ gradient differences, requiring $O(md)$ storage rather than $O(d^2)$. For the 2D benchmarks in this assignment, full Newton is tractable and L-BFGS would provide minimal advantage; the distinction becomes critical in high-dimensional settings.

\]

\subsection{Part A: Gradient Approximation Methods (Benchmark B)}
\label{subsec:q4_partA}

Derivative-free and finite-difference methods were covered in lecture weeks 8a and 8b of CS7DS2. These methods are essential when gradients are unavailable (e.g., black-box simulators, non-differentiable losses, or hardware-in-the-loop optimisation).

\subsubsection{Finite Difference Approximation: Theory}

The forward finite-difference approximation replaces each partial derivative with:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x) \approx \frac{f(x + \delta e_i) - f(x)}{\delta}.
\end{equation}
The total approximation error decomposes into a \emph{truncation} component and a \emph{rounding} component:
\begin{equation}
\|\hat{g}(\delta) - \nabla f(x)\| \leq \underbrace{\frac{L_2 \delta}{2}}_{\text{truncation: } O(\delta)} + \underbrace{\frac{C \varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}}{\delta}}_{\text{rounding: } O(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}/\delta)},
\end{equation}
where $L_2$ is the Lipschitz constant of $\nabla f$ and $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}} \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-16}$ is floating-point machine epsilon. The optimal step minimising the total error is $\delta^{\star}_{\mathrm{fwd}} = \sqrt{2C\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}/L_2} \approx \sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}} \approx 10^{-8}$.

For central differences $\hat{g}_c(x) = [f(x + \delta e_i) - f(x - \delta e_i)]/(2\delta)$, the truncation error is $O(\delta^2)$, giving the better optimal step $\delta^{\star}_{\mathrm{cen}} \approx \varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}^{1/3} \approx 10^{-5}$.

\subsubsection{Nesterov Random Search}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Nesterov Random Search (Zeroth-Order)}
\label{alg:nrs}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, perturbation $\delta$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State Sample $u_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$; normalise $u_k \gets u_k / \|u_k\|$
    \State $\hat{g}_k \gets \dfrac{f(x_k + \delta u_k) - f(x_k)}{\delta}\, u_k$ \Comment{Directional derivative estimate}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \hat{g}_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

This zeroth-order method uses only \emph{two function evaluations} per iteration regardless of dimension $d$. The estimator $\hat{g}_k = [(f(x + \delta u) - f(x))/\delta] u$ is a biased gradient estimate satisfying $\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_k] = \frac{1}{d} \nabla f(x_k)$ for the $d$-dimensional case. The convergence rate for smooth functions is $O(d/T)$ for $T$ iterations, i.e., $d$ times slower than exact gradient descent. For $d = 2$, this is only a factor of 2 penalty, but for high-dimensional problems the method becomes impractical.

\subsubsection{Nelder--Mead Simplex Method}

The Nelder--Mead algorithm \cite{nelder1965simplex} maintains a simplex of $n+1 = 3$ vertices in $\mathbb{R}^2$ and iteratively applies four transformations:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Reflection} ($\alpha_r = 1$): move the worst vertex through the centroid of the remaining vertices.
\item \textbf{Expansion} ($\gamma = 2$): if reflection improved, attempt a further step in the same direction.
\item \textbf{Contraction} ($\rho = 0.5$): if reflection worsened, contract towards the centroid.
\item \textbf{Shrink} ($\sigma = 0.5$): if contraction failed, contract all vertices towards the best.
\end{enumerate}
No gradient information is required; the simplex geometry adaptively explores the local landscape. Initial simplex step: 0.35; iterations: 160.

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
\label{subsec:q5_problem}

Projected gradient descent and penalty methods were covered in lecture weeks 9a and 9b of CS7DS2. The key insight is that constraint handling introduces a fundamental dichotomy: \emph{primal methods} (projected GD) maintain feasibility at every step, while \emph{penalty/Lagrangian methods} relax the constraint and drive infeasibility to zero asymptotically.

Minimise Benchmark B subject to $x_1 \geq 0.5$, starting from the infeasible point $x_0 = (0.2, 4.0)$:
\begin{equation}
\min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^2} f(x) = (x_1 - 1)^2 + 5(x_2 - 2)^2 + \sin(x_1) \quad \text{subject to} \quad x_1 \geq 0.5.
\end{equation}
The feasible set is $\mathcal{C} = \{x : x_1 \geq 0.5\}$. The unconstrained minimum $x^{\star}_{\mathrm{unc}} \approx (0.582, 2.0)$ satisfies $x^{\star}_1 = 0.582 > 0.5$, so it is feasible and the constrained minimum equals the unconstrained minimum: $x^{\star}_{\mathrm{con}} = (0.582, 2.0)$, $f^{\star} = 0.7244$.

\subsection{Algorithm Formulations}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Projected Gradient Descent}
\label{alg:pgd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, projection $\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \nabla f(x_k)$ \Comment{Unconstrained gradient step}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets \Pi_{\mathcal{C}}(z_{k+1})$ \Comment{Project: $x_1 \leftarrow \max(0.5,\, x_1)$}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The projection $\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}(z) = (\max(0.5, z_1), z_2)^T$ is a single $\max$ operation --- a half-space projection is trivially cheap. Projected gradient descent inherits the same convergence rate as unconstrained GD: for $\mu$-strongly convex $f$ and step $\alpha \leq 1/L$, the iterates satisfy $\|x_k - x^{\star}\|^2 \leq (1 - \mu\alpha)^k \|x_0 - x^{\star}\|^2$ \cite{bertsekas1999nonlinear}.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Quadratic Penalty Method}
\label{alg:penalty}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, penalty weight $\lambda$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $F_{\lambda}(x) \gets f(x) + \lambda \max(0,\, 0.5 - x_1)$ \Comment{Penalised objective}
    \State $\nabla F_{\lambda}(x_k) \gets \nabla f(x_k) + \lambda [-\mathbf{1}_{x_1 < 0.5},\, 0]^T$ \Comment{Subgradient}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \nabla F_{\lambda}(x_k)$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The penalty approach solves the unconstrained problem $\min_x F_{\lambda}(x)$ whose solution converges to the constrained solution as $\lambda \to \infty$: for any $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists $\bar{\lambda}$ such that for all $\lambda \geq \bar{\lambda}$, $\|x^{\star}(\lambda) - x^{\star}_{\mathrm{con}}\| \leq \varepsilon$. In practice, $\lambda$ must be finite, introducing a trade-off: larger $\lambda$ enforces the constraint more tightly but increases the condition number of $F_{\lambda}$, requiring smaller step sizes for stability.

Settings: Projected GD ($\alpha = 0.08$), Penalty with $\lambda \in \{0.15, 1.8, 4.5\}$ ($\alpha = 0.05$ for $\lambda \leq 1.8$; $\alpha = 0.03$ for $\lambda = 4.5$), unconstrained GD ($\alpha = 0.07$). All run for 100 iterations.

\textbf{Projected GD} provides exact constraint satisfaction at every iteration. This is the fundamental advantage: feasibility is maintained as a hard invariant, not negotiated through penalty weights. The trade-off is the requirement for an efficient projection oracle; for the half-space $\{x_1 \geq 0.5\}$, the projection is trivial, but for general constraint sets (e.g., semidefinite constraints, $\ell_1$ balls), projection may be as expensive as solving the original problem.

\textbf{Penalty methods} require no projection oracle, making them applicable whenever the penalised objective and its gradient can be evaluated. The key limitation is that the condition number of $F_{\lambda}$ grows with $\lambda$: the penalty term $\lambda\max(0, 0.5 - x_1)$ adds a curvature discontinuity at $x_1 = 0.5$, and for smooth approximations the effective added curvature is $O(\lambda)$. Thus the overall condition number of $F_{\lambda}$ grows as $O(\lambda)$, requiring the step size $\alpha \propto 1/\lambda$ for stability --- diminishing returns as $\lambda$ increases. This is observed empirically: the code uses $\alpha = 0.05$ for $\lambda \leq 1.8$ but must reduce to $\alpha = 0.03$ for $\lambda = 4.5$. The augmented Lagrangian (AL) method \cite{bertsekas1999nonlinear} resolves this: $F_{\lambda,\mu}(x) = f(x) + \mu g(x) + \frac{\lambda}{2}\|g(x)\|^2$ where $\mu_k$ are updated as $\mu_{k+1} = \mu_k + \lambda g(x_k)$. AL achieves exact constraint satisfaction at finite $\lambda$ while keeping the condition number of the penalised subproblem bounded.

\subsection{Feasible Set and Linear Programme}
\label{subsec:q6_lp}

The Frank--Wolfe algorithm and linear programmes were covered in lecture week 10 of CS7DS2. Frank--Wolfe (conditional gradient method) is the classical algorithm for smooth optimisation over polytopes and structured convex sets where the linear minimisation oracle (LMO) is computationally cheaper than projection.

Feasible set: $\mathcal{X} = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^2 : 0.5 \leq x_1 \leq 5,\ -5 \leq x_2 \leq 10\}$.

\textbf{Linear Programme:} For $f(x) = a^T x$ with $a = [1, 2]^T$, the minimum over $\mathcal{X}$ is found by minimising each coordinate independently since the box constraint decomposes per dimension. Both components of $a$ are positive, so each coordinate is minimised at its lower bound:
\begin{equation}
x^{\star} = \argmin_{x \in \mathcal{X}} (x_1 + 2x_2) = (0.5,\, -5), \qquad f^{\star} = 0.5 + 2(-5) = -9.5.
\end{equation}
This illustrates the \emph{fundamental theorem of linear programming}: the optimum of a linear function over a bounded polytope is always attained at a vertex. For a box with $d$ binary choices per coordinate, there are $2^d$ vertices to consider; the LP subproblem of Frank--Wolfe exploits this by solving each coordinate independently in $O(d)$ time.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/q6_lp.pdf}
\caption{Q6-I: Feasible region (blue box) and objective contours $f(x) = x_1 + 2x_2$. The contour lines are parallel planes; the minimum is at the vertex $(0.5, -5)$ where both coordinates are at their lower bounds. The LP structure guarantees the optimum is at a vertex because the objective is linear and the feasible set is convex: the optimum must lie on the boundary, and for polytopes this means at a vertex.}

\subsection{Frank--Wolfe Algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Frank--Wolfe (Conditional Gradient)}
\label{alg:fw}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0 \in \mathcal{X}$, mixing parameter $\beta$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_k \gets \arg\min_{s \in \mathcal{X}}\, \nabla f(x_k)^T s$ \Comment{Linear minimisation oracle (LMO)}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets \beta\, x_k + (1 - \beta)\, z_k$ \Comment{Convex combination: step $(1-\beta)$ towards $z_k$}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

For the box constraint, the LMO decomposes per coordinate:
\begin{equation}
(z_k)_i = \begin{cases} l_i & \text{if } (\nabla f(x_k))_i > 0 \\ u_i & \text{if } (\nabla f(x_k))_i \leq 0 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
requiring only $O(d)$ operations. The key theoretical property of Frank--Wolfe is that all iterates remain in $\mathcal{X}$ (since $x_0 \in \mathcal{X}$, $z_k \in \mathcal{X}$, and $\mathcal{X}$ is convex), so no projection step is needed.

\begin{theorem}[Frank--Wolfe convergence \cite{frank1956algorithm}]
For $f$ convex and $L$-smooth over $\mathcal{X}$ with diameter $D = \sup_{x,y \in \mathcal{X}}\|x-y\|$, the Frank--Wolfe iterates with step size $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$ satisfy:
\begin{equation}
f(x_k) - f^{\star} \leq \frac{2LD^2}{k+2} = O\!\left(\frac{L D^2}{k}\right).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

This $O(1/k)$ rate is the same asymptotic order as projected gradient descent, but Frank--Wolfe's constant $2LD^2$ involves the domain diameter $D$ rather than the initial distance $\|x_0 - x^{\star}\|$ in PGD's bound. For our box constraint, $D \approx 16.2$, so $2LD^2 \approx 2 \times 10 \times 263 = 5260$ (for the interior objective), while $\|x_0 - x^{\star}\|^2 = \|(1,1)-(1,5)\|^2 = 16$ gives PGD a constant $16/(2k) = 8/k$, three orders of magnitude tighter. The advantage of Frank--Wolfe is structural, not asymptotic: the LMO is often much cheaper than a projection for complex constraint sets (e.g., nuclear norm balls require a rank-1 SVD, while projection requires full SVD; simplex constraints require only sorting).

The implementation here uses a fixed $\beta$ (retention fraction) rather than the standard $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$ schedule. The effective step size is $1 - \beta$; with $\beta = 0.90$, the step towards $z_k$ is 10\%, while $\beta = 0.985$ gives only 1.5\%.

\subsection{Interior vs.\ Boundary Behaviour}

\textbf{Interior optimum:} When $x^{\star}$ is in the interior of $\mathcal{X}$, the Frank--Wolfe gap $h(x_k) = \nabla f(x_k)^T(x_k - z_k) \to 0$ as $x_k \to x^{\star}$, but the LP vertex $z_k$ still jumps to an extreme point of $\mathcal{X}$ at each iteration. The convex combination averages these extremal directions, creating an indirect, zig-zagging approach to the optimum. The Frank--Wolfe gap provides an \emph{optimality certificate}: $f(x_k) - f^{\star} \leq h(x_k)$, which can be computed without knowing $f^{\star}$. As $\nabla f(x_k) \to 0$ (first-order optimality), $h(x_k) \to 0$ and the iterate must be converging to the unconstrained optimum (which lies in the interior).

\textbf{Boundary optimum:} Frank--Wolfe is structurally better suited to boundary optima because the LMO systematically selects the nearest constrained vertex to the unconstrained optimum. The iterate naturally approaches the boundary without explicit projection: once $x_{1,k} \approx 0.5$, the LMO selects $z_k = (0.5, -5)$ or $(0.5, 5)$ depending on the sign of $\nabla f_{x_2}$, and the convex combination gradually reduces $x_{2,k}$ towards 0. The residual gap (0.1137 after 140 iterations, above $f^{\star} = 0.25$) reflects two limitations: (1) the fixed $\beta = 0.93$ prevents the step size from diminishing, causing persistent oscillation; (2) the boundary optimum is on the \emph{edge} of $\mathcal{X}$ (not a vertex), so Frank--Wolfe's vertex-jumping mechanism is inefficient for converging to non-vertex boundary points. Using $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$ would provide the $O(LD^2/k)$ guarantee and converge to $f^{\star} = 0.25$.

\textbf{Frank--Wolfe vs.\ Projected GD:} Frank--Wolfe is preferred when: (i)~the LMO is cheap (structured constraint sets), (ii)~a sparse or vertex-supported solution is desired (the iterate is a convex combination of at most $k+1$ vertices after $k$ steps, a property called \emph{sparsifiability}), or (iii)~projection is expensive. Projected GD is preferred when: (i)~a projection oracle is available, (ii)~faster convergence is needed, or (iii)~the function has strong convexity (projected GD achieves $O((1-\mu/L)^k)$ vs.\ Frank--Wolfe's $O(1/k)$).

\subsection{Algorithmic Design Principles}

The experiments across six algorithm families and three benchmarks support the following design principles:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Condition number is the critical diagnostic.} Well-conditioned problems ($\kappa \approx 1$) are trivial for any gradient method; ill-conditioned problems ($\kappa \gg 1$) require curvature-aware approaches. Always estimate $\kappa$ before selecting an algorithm.

\item \textbf{Second-order methods dominate when applicable.} Newton's method with 20 damped iterations outperforms all first-order methods with 200 iterations on Rosenbrock. The cost is computing and inverting the Hessian ($O(d^3)$ per step), which becomes prohibitive for $d \gg 10^3$.

\item \textbf{Momentum is the most cost-effective first-order accelerator.} Nesterov acceleration achieves $O(1/k^2)$ convergence --- optimal for first-order methods on convex functions --- using only one additional gradient evaluation per iteration (at the lookahead point) compared to GD.

\item \textbf{Adaptive step sizes trade theoretical guarantees for robustness.} Adagrad and Adam adapt per-coordinate step sizes, reducing sensitivity to anisotropic curvature. However, Adam's regret bounds are weaker than Nesterov's convergence guarantees on strongly convex problems, and both require careful tuning of base rates.

\item \textbf{Exact $f^{\star}$ knowledge enables optimal Polyak steps.} When the true minimum value is known (e.g., supervised learning with ground truth), Polyak's step is extremely effective. The common practice of setting $f^{\star} = 0$ induces divergence unless the noise floor is genuinely zero.

\item \textbf{Derivative-free methods scale poorly with dimension.} Nelder--Mead and grid search are suitable for $d \leq 10$; Nesterov random search scales to high $d$ but converges $d$ times slower than exact GD. Finite differences with proper $\delta$ closely approximates exact gradients at $d+1$ function evaluations per step.

\item \textbf{Constraint handling: exact vs.\ approximate.} Projected gradient descent enforces feasibility exactly at every step but requires a projection oracle. Penalty methods require only gradient evaluations but trade exact feasibility for simplicity, with the condition number of the penalised objective growing proportionally to $\lambda$.

\item \textbf{Frank--Wolfe leverages constraint structure.} The algorithm is most powerful when the LMO is cheaper than the projection: for example, for nuclear norm or $\ell_1$ ball constraints, the LMO is a rank-1 SVD or soft-thresholding, while projection is more expensive. For box constraints the two have equal cost, but Frank--Wolfe's vertex-traversal produces sparse solutions.
\end{enumerate}

def gradient_descent(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        x = x - alpha * grad_fn(x)
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Polyak Step Size}]
def polyak_step(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, f_star, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        alpha_k = (loss_fn(x) - f_star) / (np.dot(g, g) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(alpha_k)
        x = x - alpha_k * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy()); f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Adagrad}]
def adagrad(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha0, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    G = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        G += g**2
        eff_alpha = alpha0 / (np.sqrt(G) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(np.mean(eff_alpha))
        x = x - eff_alpha * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy()); f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={RMSprop}]
def rmsprop(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha0, beta, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    v = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        v = beta * v + (1 - beta) * g**2
        eff_alpha = alpha0 / (np.sqrt(v) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(np.mean(eff_alpha))
        x = x - eff_alpha * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy()); f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Heavy Ball / Polyak Momentum}]
def heavy_ball(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    z = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        z = beta * z + alpha * g
        x = x - z
        x_hist.append(x.copy()); f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Nesterov Momentum}]
def nesterov_momentum(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta_max, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    z = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for k in range(1, n_iters + 1):
        beta_k = min((k - 1) / (k + 2), beta_max)
        lookahead = x + beta_k * z          # gradient evaluated at lookahead
        g = grad_fn(lookahead)
        z = beta_k * z - alpha * g
        x = x + z
        x_hist.append(x.copy()); f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Adam Optimiser}]
def adam_optimiser(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta1, beta2, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    m_vec, v_vec = np.zeros_like(x), np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for t in range(1, n_iters + 1):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        m_vec = beta1 * m_vec + (1 - beta1) * g
        v_vec = beta2 * v_vec + (1 - beta2) * g**2
        m_hat = m_vec / (1 - beta1**t)      # bias correction
        v_hat = v_vec / (1 - beta2**t)
        x = x - alpha * m_hat / (np.sqrt(v_hat) + eps)
        x_hist.append(x.copy()); f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Newton's Method with Tikhonov Regularisation}]
def newtons_method(grad_fn, hess_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, n_iters, damping=1e-8):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, update_norms = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        H = hess_fn(x) if callable(hess_fn) else hess_fn
        H_reg = H + damping * np.eye(len(x))   # Tikhonov regularisation
        try:
            p = np.linalg.solve(H_reg, g)       # solve H p = g
        except np.linalg.LinAlgError:
            p = g
        update_norms.append(np.linalg.norm(alpha * p))
        x = x - alpha * p
        x_hist.append(x.copy()); f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(update_norms)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Frank--Wolfe Algorithm}]
def frank_wolfe(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, bounds, beta, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, z_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        # Linear minimisation oracle: select vertex minimising g^T s
        z = np.array([bounds[i][0] if g[i] > 0 else bounds[i][1]
                       for i in range(len(g))])
        z_hist.append(z.copy())
        x = beta * x + (1 - beta) * z      # convex combination
        x_hist.append(x.copy()); f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(z_hist)
\end{lstlisting}
