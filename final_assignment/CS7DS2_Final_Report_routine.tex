\begin{abstract}
This report presents a comprehensive comparative study of first-order, second-order, derivative-free,
constrained, and projection-free optimisation algorithms applied to three benchmark problems: a linear
regression quadratic loss (Benchmark~A, $\kappa \approx 1.12$), a toy neural network loss with
sinusoidal perturbation (Benchmark~B, $\kappa \leq 10$), and the Rosenbrock function
(Benchmark~C, $\kappa \approx 2508$).
The three benchmarks span four orders of magnitude in condition number, providing a rigorous test
of algorithm sensitivity to ill-conditioning.
Six algorithm families are studied, each with formal convergence guarantees and experimental results
totalling 49 generated figures and proofs or proof sketches for 8 key theorems/propositions.

(1)~\emph{Adaptive step-size methods} (Q1): Polyak step achieves the fastest convergence on Rosenbrock
when $f^\star$ is known exactly (formal distance-monotone guarantee derived), but diverges when
$f^\star$ is underspecified. Adagrad's per-coordinate regret bound $O(\alpha_0^{-1}D_\infty\sqrt{TG_\infty})$
explains its advantage on sparse gradients. Heavy Ball achieves $O(e^{-2k/\sqrt{\kappa}})$ convergence
with optimal parameters (vs GD's $O(((\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1))^k)$), and RMSprop's exponential moving
average provides a steady-state effective rate $\alpha_0/\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[g^2]}$.

(2)~\emph{Momentum methods} (Q2): Nesterov achieves $f = 0.479$ on Rosenbrock vs GD's $f = 3.388$
(7$\times$ improvement), confirmed by the Lyapunov energy-dissipation proof of the $O(1/k^2)$ rate.
Adam's non-convergence is illustrated via the Reddi et al.\ AMSGrad counterexample, and SVRG/SAGA
variance reduction is shown to achieve linear convergence with fixed step sizes.

(3)~\emph{Newton's method} (Q3): one-step convergence on quadratics, 4--5 iterations on Benchmark~B,
and $5.4\times$ improvement on Rosenbrock in 20 vs 80 iterations. BFGS update derived from secant
condition and weighted Frobenius minimisation. Damped Newton vs quadratic-convergence regions analysed.

(4)~\emph{Derivative-free methods} (Q4): central FD achieves $O(\delta^2)$ truncation vs forward FD's
$O(\delta)$, with optimal $\delta^\star_{\text{central}} = \varepsilon_{\text{mach}}^{1/3}$.
NRS is proved to be an unbiased gradient estimate with $1/d$ scaling and $O(\|\nabla f\|^2 d)$
variance; zeroth-order oracle lower bound $\Omega(d/\epsilon)$ quantifies the cost of gradient ignorance.
Nelder--Mead lacks general convergence guarantees (McKinnon counterexample noted).

(5)~\emph{Constrained optimisation} (Q5): KKT conditions are necessary under LICQ (verified).
Since $x_1^\star \approx 0.582 > 0.5$, the constraint is inactive at the optimum ($\mu^\star = 0$,
strong duality via Slater's condition). Projection operator formal properties derived.
Augmented Lagrangian method algorithm and dual ascent update included.

(6)~\emph{Frank--Wolfe} (Q6): formal $O(1/k)$ proof via $L$-smoothness decrease lemma.
FW gap $\gamma_k$ provides a computable convergence certificate and stopping criterion.
Vertex-cycling near interior optima is explained and illustrated. Sparsity-inducing property
demonstrated on the simplex: FW iterates have $\leq k+1$ non-zero atoms.
\end{abstract}

\section{Benchmark Problems and Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:benchmarks}
% ============================================================

All experiments use a fixed random seed (\texttt{np.random.seed(42)}) for reproducibility.
The three benchmarks span a range of difficulty: a well-conditioned convex quadratic,
a mildly anisotropic non-quadratic function, and a highly ill-conditioned nonconvex valley.

\subsection{Benchmark A: Linear Regression Quadratic Loss}
A synthetic dataset with $m = 1000$ samples and two parameters is generated:
\begin{equation}
\theta^{\star} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
x^{(i)} \in \mathbb{R}^2,\quad
y^{(i)} = \theta^{\star\top} x^{(i)} + \varepsilon^{(i)},\quad
\varepsilon^{(i)} \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0,1).
\end{equation}
The loss is the mean squared error:
\begin{equation}
J(\theta) = \frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\!\left(\theta^\top x^{(i)} - y^{(i)}\right)^2
          = \frac{1}{2m}\|X\theta - y\|^2.
\end{equation}
This is a \emph{strongly convex quadratic} with constant Hessian $H = \tfrac{1}{m}X^\top X \succ 0$.
The gradient is $\nabla J(\theta) = \tfrac{1}{m}X^\top(X\theta - y)$.
Because the data matrix $X$ has full column rank (almost surely for Gaussian data), $H$ is positive
definite and the unique minimiser is the OLS solution
$\theta^\star_{\mathrm{OLS}} = H^{-1}\tfrac{1}{m}X^\top y \approx [3.028, 4.017]^\top$.
The noise term creates an irreducible loss floor
$J^\star = \tfrac{1}{2m}\|y - X\theta^\star_{\mathrm{OLS}}\|^2 \approx 0.4826$.
Starting point: $\theta_0 = [0, 0]^\top$.

\subsection{Benchmark B: Toy Neural Network Quadratic Loss}
\begin{equation}
f(x_1, x_2) = (x_1 - 1)^2 + 5(x_2 - 2)^2 + \sin(x_1).
\label{eq:benchB}
\end{equation}
The gradient is
$\nabla f = \bigl[2(x_1-1)+\cos(x_1),\; 10(x_2-2)\bigr]^\top$
and the Hessian is
$\nabla^2 f = \mathrm{diag}\!\bigl(2 - \sin(x_1),\, 10\bigr)$.
The sinusoidal perturbation $\sin(x_1)$ breaks the purely quadratic structure and causes the
eigenvalue $\lambda_1(x) = 2 - \sin(x_1)$ to vary with location. On the domain of interest
($x_1 \in [-1.5, 3]$) the condition number $\kappa(\nabla^2 f) = 10\,/\,(2-\sin(x_1))$ ranges
from approximately $10/3 \approx 3.3$ (at $x_1$ where $\sin(x_1) \approx -1$) to
$10/1 = 10$ (where $\sin(x_1) = 1$).
The minimum satisfies $2(x_1^\star-1)+\cos(x_1^\star) = 0$ and $x_2^\star = 2$.
Newton's method on the scalar equation gives $x_1^\star \approx 0.582$, so
$f^\star \approx (0.582-1)^2 + \sin(0.582) \approx 0.1747 + 0.5494 = 0.7241$.
Starting point: $x_0 = [-1, 4]^\top$.

\subsection{Benchmark C: Rosenbrock Function}
\begin{equation}
f(x_1, x_2) = (1 - x_1)^2 + 100(x_2 - x_1^2)^2.
\label{eq:benchC}
\end{equation}
The global minimum is at $(1, 1)$ with $f^\star = 0$.
The Hessian is
\begin{equation}
\nabla^2 f(x) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 + 1200x_1^2 - 400x_2 & -400x_1 \\ -400x_1 & 200 \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
At the minimiser $(1,1)$ and the starting point $(-1,1)$, the Hessian has entries
$H_{11} = 2 + 1200x_1^2 - 400x_2 = 802$, $|H_{12}| = 400|x_1| = 400$, $H_{22} = 200$.
The eigenvalues of the symmetric matrix $\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}802 & \pm400 \\ \pm400 & 200\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]$
satisfy $\lambda^2 - 1002\lambda + 400 = 0$, giving $\lambda_1 \approx 1001.6$ and
$\lambda_2 \approx 0.40$, hence the condition number $\kappa \approx 2508$ throughout the
trajectory from $(-1,1)$ to $(1,1)$.
This extreme ill-conditioning, combined with a narrow curved valley along the parabola
$x_2 = x_1^2$, makes Rosenbrock one of the most challenging standard test functions.
Starting point: $x_0 = [-1, 1]^\top$.

\subsection{Theoretical Background}

Two key regularity conditions underpin all convergence analyses in this report:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{$L$-smoothness}: $f$ has $L$-Lipschitz gradient, i.e.,
      $\|\nabla f(x) - \nabla f(y)\| \leq L\|x-y\|$ for all $x,y$.
      Equivalently: $f(y) \leq f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top(y-x) + \tfrac{L}{2}\|y-x\|^2$
      (the function lies below its second-order upper envelope).
\item \textbf{$\mu$-strong convexity}: $f(y) \geq f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top(y-x) + \tfrac{\mu}{2}\|y-x\|^2$
      (the function lies above its second-order lower envelope).
\end{itemize}
These bounds bracket the function between two parabolas, enabling the contraction analysis below.

For gradient descent with constant step size $\alpha$ applied to an $L$-smooth, $\mu$-strongly convex
function, the convergence rate is geometric:
\begin{equation}
f(x_{k}) - f^\star \;\leq\; \left(1 - \frac{\mu}{L}\right)^{\!k}\bigl[f(x_0) - f^\star\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\mu$ and $L$ are the strong convexity constant and the Lipschitz constant of the gradient,
respectively, and $\kappa = L/\mu$ is the condition number.
For a quadratic loss with Hessian $H$, $L = \lambda_{\max}(H)$ and $\mu = \lambda_{\min}(H)$,
and the stability condition $\alpha < 2/L$ determines the maximum usable step size.

For Benchmark~A, $H = \tfrac{1}{m}X^\top X$ has eigenvalues $\lambda_{\min} \approx 0.925$
and $\lambda_{\max} \approx 1.033$, giving a condition number $\kappa_A \approx 1.12$
(nearly perfectly conditioned), which explains why GD converges very fast on this benchmark.
Stability requires $\alpha < 2/L = 2/1.033 \approx 1.94$; the chosen $\alpha = 0.08$ is
conservative, leaving room for acceleration.
For Benchmark~B, $L = 10$ (the constant large eigenvalue of the diagonal Hessian), so
stability requires $\alpha < 0.2$; the chosen $\alpha = 0.06$ is safely within this bound.
The Lipschitz constant $L = 10$ can be verified empirically: for any $x, y$, the gradient
difference $\|\nabla f(x) - \nabla f(y)\| \leq \max_z \lambda_{\max}(\nabla^2 f(z))\cdot\|x-y\|
= \max_z (2-\sin(z_1)) \vee 10 \cdot \|x-y\| = 10\|x-y\|$ (Figure~\ref{fig:q1_lipschitz}).
For Benchmark~C (Rosenbrock), $L \approx 1001.6$ (computed numerically at $x_0 = [-1,1]$),
requiring $\alpha < 2/L \approx 0.002$; hence the conservative $\alpha = 0.0012$.

\subsubsection{Connection to Backtracking Line Search}

A classical alternative to fixed step sizes is \emph{Armijo backtracking}: starting from a
candidate step $\alpha_{\text{try}}$, halve it until the sufficient decrease condition
\begin{equation}
f(x_k - \alpha_{\text{try}} \nabla f(x_k)) \;\leq\; f(x_k) - c\, \alpha_{\text{try}}\, \|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2
\end{equation}
holds (typically $c = 0.1$--$0.5$). This automatically adapts to the local Lipschitz constant
$L_{\text{local}}$ without requiring global knowledge of~$L$, and guarantees sufficient decrease
at each step without divergence.
In the experiments, fixed step sizes are used for simplicity and reproducibility,
but backtracking would eliminate the need to specify separate $\alpha$ for each benchmark.
The Wolfe conditions (additionally enforcing a curvature condition on the gradient) are stronger
than Armijo alone and are used in quasi-Newton methods such as BFGS.

Adaptive step-size methods aim to overcome the dependence on~$\kappa$ by adjusting the effective
learning rate per coordinate or per iteration.

\subsubsection{Formal Convergence Proof Sketch}

\begin{theorem}[GD linear convergence on strongly convex functions]
\label{thm:gd_conv}
Let $f : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$ be $\mu$-strongly convex and $L$-smooth. With step size
$\alpha \in (0, 2/L)$, gradient descent satisfies:
\[
f(x_k) - f^\star \;\leq\; \left(1 - \alpha\mu\right)^{\!k}\bigl[f(x_0) - f^\star\bigr].
\]
The optimal step size $\alpha^\star = 2/(L+\mu)$ achieves the tightest bound
$\rho^\star = (\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1)$, giving convergence in $O(\kappa \log(1/\epsilon))$ iterations.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
By $L$-smoothness, $f(x - \alpha g) \leq f(x) - \alpha\|\nabla f(x)\|^2 + \tfrac{\alpha^2 L}{2}\|\nabla f(x)\|^2
= f(x) - \alpha(1 - \alpha L/2)\|\nabla f(x)\|^2$.
By $\mu$-strong convexity, $\|\nabla f(x)\|^2 \geq 2\mu(f(x)-f^\star)$.
Combining: $f(x_{k+1}) - f^\star \leq (1 - 2\mu\alpha(1-\alpha L/2))(f(x_k) - f^\star)$.
Minimising the contraction factor over $\alpha \in (0, 2/L)$ gives $\alpha^\star = 1/L$
with rate $(1-1/\kappa)$, or the two-point stepsize $\alpha^\star = 2/(L+\mu)$ with rate
$(\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1) < (1-1/\kappa)$, which is strictly better for $\kappa > 1$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Algorithm Formulations}

Four adaptive methods are compared against a constant step-size gradient descent (GD) baseline
across all three benchmarks for 120 iterations.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Polyak Step Size}
\label{alg:polyak}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, optimal value $f^{\star}$, tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $\alpha_k \gets \dfrac{f(x_k) - f^{\star}}{\|g_k\|^2 + \varepsilon}$
    \Comment{Self-tuning step: exact if $f^*$ known}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha_k\, g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The Polyak step is derived from the sub-gradient method: choosing $\alpha_k$ to minimise the
distance to the optimum in the steepest-descent direction under a linearisation gives the formula
above. For convex functions where $f^\star$ is known exactly, Polyak's step achieves convergence
without requiring knowledge of any Lipschitz constant.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Adagrad}
\label{alg:adagrad}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, base learning rate $\alpha_0$, tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$, iterations $N$
\State $G_0 \gets \mathbf{0} \in \mathbb{R}^d$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $G_{k+1} \gets G_k + g_k \odot g_k$ \Comment{Accumulated squared gradients (per coordinate)}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \dfrac{\alpha_0}{\sqrt{G_{k+1}} + \varepsilon} \odot g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Adagrad's key property is \emph{monotonically decreasing} effective step sizes per coordinate:
since $G_{k,i}$ is non-decreasing in $k$ for every coordinate $i$, the effective rate
$\alpha_0/(\sqrt{G_{k,i}}+\varepsilon)$ can only decrease.
This provides stability but may cause premature convergence on long optimisation runs.

Adagrad was originally derived for the \emph{online learning} setting.
The \emph{regret} after $T$ rounds is $R_T = \sum_{t=1}^T [f_t(x_t) - f_t(x^\star)]$.
For the diagonal Adagrad update with base rate $\alpha_0$, the per-coordinate bound gives:
\begin{equation}
R_T \;\leq\; \frac{1}{\alpha_0}\sum_{i=1}^d \|x_{0,i} - x_i^\star\|^2 \sqrt{G_{T,i}}
\;\leq\; \frac{D_\infty}{\alpha_0}\sqrt{T\sum_{i=1}^d G_{T,i}}
\;=\; O\!\left(\alpha_0^{-1} D_\infty \sqrt{T G_\infty}\right),
\end{equation}
where $G_\infty = \sum_{t=1}^T \|g_t\|_\infty^2$ and $D_\infty = \max_i |x_{0,i} - x_i^\star|$.
For \emph{sparse} gradients where most $g_{t,i} = 0$, $G_\infty \ll T d$, giving much better
than the $O(\sqrt{Td})$ rate of standard SGD---the key motivation for Adagrad in NLP.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{RMSprop}
\label{alg:rmsprop}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, learning rate $\alpha_0$, decay $\beta \in (0,1)$, tolerance $\varepsilon$, iterations $N$
\State $v_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $v_{k+1} \gets \beta\, v_k + (1 - \beta)\, g_k \odot g_k$ \Comment{Exponential moving average}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \dfrac{\alpha_0}{\sqrt{v_{k+1}} + \varepsilon} \odot g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

RMSprop improves on Adagrad by replacing the cumulative sum of squared gradients with an exponential
moving average with forgetting factor $\beta$.
As $k \to \infty$, $v_k \to (1-\beta)\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\beta^j g_{k-j}^2$, so the effective
normaliser reflects only \emph{recent} gradient magnitudes.
This allows the effective step size to increase again if recent gradients become smaller, addressing
Adagrad's excessive decay.

\begin{remark}[RMSprop steady-state effective learning rate]
Expanding the geometric series: $v_k = (1-\beta)\sum_{j=0}^{k}\beta^{k-j}g_j^2$.
In steady state (stationary gradient distribution), $\mathbb{E}[v_k] \approx \mathbb{E}[g^2]$
(independent of $\beta$), so the expected effective learning rate is
$\alpha_0/\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[g^2]}$---a root-mean-square normalisation that gives the method its name.
The forgetting factor $\beta = 0.9$ means the effective window spans $1/(1-\beta) = 10$ recent
gradient evaluations, adapting to non-stationary gradient statistics over a time horizon of 10 steps.
\end{remark}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Polyak Momentum / Heavy Ball}
\label{alg:heavyball}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, momentum $\beta$, iterations $N$
\State $z_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets \beta\, z_k + \alpha\, \nabla f(x_k)$
    \Comment{Accumulate gradient with decay}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - z_{k+1}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Heavy Ball builds up velocity in the direction of consistent gradient descent.
For a strongly convex quadratic with condition number $\kappa = L/\mu$,
the optimal Heavy Ball parameters are:
\begin{equation}
\beta_{\text{opt}} = \left(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa}+1}\right)^{\!2}, \qquad
\alpha_{\text{opt}} = \frac{4}{(\sqrt{L}+\sqrt{\mu})^2},
\end{equation}
achieving the convergence rate $O\!\left(\mathrm{e}^{-2k/\sqrt{\kappa}}\right)$ versus
$O\!\left(\mathrm{e}^{-2k/\kappa}\right)$ for plain GD.
For Benchmark~A with $\kappa \approx 1.12$, this gives $\beta_{\text{opt}} \approx 0.005$ and
$\alpha_{\text{opt}} \approx 0.96$---far from the experiment's $\beta = 0.88$, $\alpha = 0.045$.
The conservative hyperparameters are appropriate for the non-quadratic benchmarks where optimal
quadratic parameters would cause divergence.
Indeed, for non-quadratic functions, Heavy Ball may \emph{not converge at all}
(unlike the provably accelerated Nesterov method, Section~\ref{sec:q2}),
making conservative parameter choices important for robustness.

Figure~\ref{fig:q1_hb_optimal} demonstrates the gap between optimal and experimental Heavy Ball
parameters on a synthetic quadratic with $\kappa = 50$---large enough to reveal the $O(\mathrm{e}^{-2k/\sqrt{\kappa}})$
advantage of optimal tuning over GD's $O((\kappa-1)^k/(\kappa+1)^k)$.

\begin{remark}[Polyak step and $f^\star$ sensitivity]
The Polyak step size is theoretically well-motivated but practically sensitive.
For a subgradient method applied to a convex function $f$ with minimiser $x^\star$,
the Polyak step $\alpha_k = (f(x_k) - f^\star)/\|g_k\|^2$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}
\|x_{k+1} - x^\star\|^2 \;\leq\; \|x_k - x^\star\|^2 - \frac{(f(x_k) - f^\star)^2}{\|g_k\|^2},
\end{equation}
guaranteeing monotone decrease in distance to the optimum when $f^\star$ is exact.
This is derived from: $\|x_{k+1} - x^\star\|^2 = \|x_k - \alpha_k g_k - x^\star\|^2
= \|x_k - x^\star\|^2 - 2\alpha_k g_k^\top(x_k - x^\star) + \alpha_k^2\|g_k\|^2$.
Using the subgradient inequality $g_k^\top(x_k - x^\star) \geq f(x_k) - f^\star$ and
substituting $\alpha_k = (f(x_k)-f^\star)/\|g_k\|^2$ yields the bound.
Figure~\ref{fig:q1_polyak} illustrates the effect of $f^\star$ misspecification.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Adagrad Sparse Gradient Advantage}

The principal motivation for Adagrad is problems with \emph{sparse or heterogeneous gradients},
where different coordinates update with vastly different frequencies or magnitudes.
In such settings, a uniform learning rate either moves too slowly in the infrequent
(but important) coordinates or causes instability in the frequent (small-gradient) ones.
Adagrad's coordinate-wise step $\alpha_0/(\sqrt{G_{k,i}}+\varepsilon)$ automatically
assigns large steps to infrequent coordinates ($G_{k,i}$ stays small) and small steps to
frequent ones ($G_{k,i}$ grows large), matching the effective curvature without tuning.

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Convergence speed:} When $f^{\star}$ is known exactly (Rosenbrock), Polyak converges
      fastest. Otherwise, Adagrad and Heavy Ball match or exceed GD.
\item \textbf{Smoothness:} RMSprop produces the smoothest convergence curves due to exponential
      gradient averaging, which dampens oscillations. Heavy Ball shows characteristic overshoot
      followed by damped oscillation near the minimum.
\item \textbf{Stability:} GD baseline is most stable but slowest. Polyak is \emph{unstable} when

      step size, though this also causes stagnation on difficult problems if steps become too small
      prematurely.
\item \textbf{Per-coordinate adaptation:} Adagrad and RMSprop both adapt per coordinate, which is
      particularly beneficial for anisotropic loss surfaces like Benchmark~B. Heavy Ball uses a
      single scalar step, so it cannot independently rescale different coordinates.
\item \textbf{Practical implication:} In practice, exact $f^{\star}$ is rarely known, limiting
      Polyak's applicability. RMSprop and Adagrad are more robust choices for unknown-minimum
      problems, while Heavy Ball is effective when the step size and momentum are well-tuned.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Theoretical Background}

Standard GD achieves the $O(1/k)$ convergence rate for convex functions. Nesterov~(1983)
proved that by incorporating a \emph{lookahead} gradient evaluation, one can achieve a
dramatically faster rate:
\begin{equation}
f(x_k) - f^\star \leq \frac{2L\|x_0 - x^\star\|^2}{(k+1)^2} = O(1/k^2).
\end{equation}
Moreover, Nesterov proved a \emph{matching lower bound}: for any first-order method
using only $\{\nabla f(x_0), \ldots, \nabla f(x_{k-1})\}$ (the gradient oracle model),
there exists an $L$-smooth convex $f$ such that
\begin{equation}
f(x_k) - f^\star \geq \frac{3L\|x_0 - x^\star\|^2}{32(k+1)^2}.
\end{equation}
Thus the $O(1/k^2)$ rate is \emph{information-theoretically optimal} for first-order methods
on smooth convex functions---no algorithm using only gradient evaluations can do better
(in the worst case). This is why Nesterov acceleration represents the \emph{gold standard}
for convex first-order optimisation.

\subsubsection{Why $O(1/k^2)$: Proof Sketch}

Nesterov's analysis introduces an energy-like Lyapunov function. Let $t_k$ satisfy
$t_{k+1}^2 = t_k^2 + t_{k+1}$ (the FISTA recurrence, giving $t_k \sim k/2$) and define
$E_k = t_k^2 (f(x_k) - f^\star) + \tfrac{L}{2}\|z_k - x^\star\|^2$ where
$z_k = x_k + (t_k-1)/t_k(x_k - x_{k-1})$ is the momentum iterate.
The key identity is:
\begin{equation}
E_{k+1} \;\leq\; E_k \qquad \text{for all } k,
\end{equation}
which follows from the $L$-smoothness decrease lemma combined with the specific FISTA step-size
choice $\gamma_k = (t_k-1)/t_{k+1}$.
Since $E_0 = \tfrac{L}{2}\|x_0 - x^\star\|^2$, monotone decrease gives
$t_k^2(f(x_k) - f^\star) \leq E_0$, and using $t_k \geq (k+1)/2$:
\begin{equation}
f(x_k) - f^\star \;\leq\; \frac{2L\|x_0-x^\star\|^2}{(k+1)^2}.
\end{equation}
The crucial insight is the \emph{energy-dissipation coupling}: each Nesterov step distributes
the energy decrease between the objective gap and the distance to the optimum, whereas GD
only decreases the objective gap, wasting the available dissipation budget.

\subsection{Algorithm Formulations}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Nesterov Momentum / Acceleration}
\label{alg:nesterov}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, max momentum $\beta_{\max}$, iterations $N$
\State $z_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 1, 2, \ldots, N$}
    \State $\beta_k \gets \min\!\left(\frac{k-1}{k+2},\ \beta_{\max}\right)$
    \Comment{Increasing schedule approximating $(t_k-1)/t_{k+1}$}
    \State $\tilde{x}_k \gets x_k + \beta_k\, z_k$
    \Comment{Lookahead (``momentum extrapolation'') point}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets \beta_k\, z_k - \alpha\, \nabla f(\tilde{x}_k)$
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k + z_{k+1}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The key distinction from Heavy Ball is the \emph{lookahead}: the gradient is evaluated at
$\tilde{x}_k = x_k + \beta_k z_k$ rather than at $x_k$.
This ``look before you leap'' strategy allows the algorithm to correct its direction
\emph{before} committing to the full momentum step, which is why Nesterov's method can be
proved convergent with optimal rate while Heavy Ball cannot in general.

\begin{remark}[Why lookahead enables $O(1/k^2)$]
Intuitively, Heavy Ball computes the gradient at $x_k$ (the \emph{current} position)
and then takes a step combining this gradient with the accumulated momentum.
If momentum has overshot, the gradient at $x_k$ does not correct for this until the
\emph{next} iteration, causing oscillations.
Nesterov evaluates the gradient at $\tilde{x}_k = x_k + \beta_k z_k$ (the \emph{predicted}
position after momentum), so the gradient immediately reflects where the iterate
will be after the momentum step. This causal alignment enables the Lyapunov
energy-dissipation argument to close, yielding $O(1/k^2)$ without oscillations.
Formally, this corresponds to ensuring that the Lyapunov function $E_k$ satisfies
$E_{k+1} \leq E_k$ at each step---a property that fails for Heavy Ball on general functions.
\end{remark}

The schedule $\beta_k = (k-1)/(k+2)$ closely approximates the classic FISTA schedule
$\beta_k = (t_k - 1)/t_{k+1}$ where $t_k = (1+\sqrt{1+4t_{k-1}^2})/2$;
both achieve the $O(1/k^2)$ rate (Figure~\ref{fig:q2_schedule}).
The maximum clamp $\beta_{\max}$ prevents the momentum from exceeding a safe value
on non-convex problems, trading some theoretical acceleration for practical stability.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{figures/q2_nesterov_schedule.pdf}
\caption{Q2: Comparison of Nesterov momentum schedules. The implemented schedule $(k-1)/(k+2)$
closely tracks the FISTA schedule $(t_k-1)/t_{k+1}$ (which is optimal for the $O(1/k^2)$ rate)

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Adam Optimiser}
\label{alg:adam}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, learning rate $\alpha$, moments $\beta_1, \beta_2$, tolerance $\varepsilon$, iterations $N$
\State $m_0 \gets \mathbf{0},\ v_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$t = 1, 2, \ldots, N$}
    \State $g_t \gets \nabla f(x_t)$
    \State $m_t \gets \beta_1\, m_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_1)\, g_t$
    \Comment{Exponential moving average of gradient (first moment)}
    \State $v_t \gets \beta_2\, v_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_2)\, g_t \odot g_t$
    \Comment{Exponential moving average of squared gradient (second moment)}
    \State $\hat{m}_t \gets m_t\, /\, (1 - \beta_1^t)$
    \Comment{Bias correction: $\mathbb{E}[m_t] = \mathbb{E}[g_t](1-\beta_1^t)$}
    \State $\hat{v}_t \gets v_t\, /\, (1 - \beta_2^t)$
    \State $x_{t+1} \gets x_t - \alpha\, \hat{m}_t\, /\, (\sqrt{\hat{v}_t} + \varepsilon)$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Adam combines RMSprop's per-coordinate adaptive scaling (via $v_t$) with gradient momentum (via $m_t$),
inheriting the advantages of both.
The bias correction is essential in early iterations: without it, $m_1 = (1-\beta_1)g_1 \ll g_1$
(for $\beta_1 \approx 0.9$), creating spuriously small effective steps.
The correction $\hat{m}_t = m_t/(1-\beta_1^t)$ removes this initialisation bias, making
the effective step size approximately $\alpha$ throughout training rather than $\alpha(1-\beta_1)$ initially.

\subsection{Mini-Batch Stochastic Gradient Descent}

Mini-batch SGD uses a randomly shuffled subset of the data at each update step.
The gradient estimator is:
\begin{equation}
\hat{g}_k = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}_k|}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{B}_k} \nabla_\theta \ell(\theta_k; x^{(i)}, y^{(i)}),
\end{equation}
which is an unbiased estimate of the full gradient ($\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_k] = \nabla J(\theta_k)$)
with variance $\mathrm{Var}(\hat{g}_k) = \sigma_g^2/b$ inversely proportional to batch size~$b$.

The trade-off is \emph{gradient quality} vs \emph{update frequency}: small $b$ gives noisy
but frequent updates (more passes through the data per epoch), while large $b$ gives accurate
but infrequent updates. For a fixed computational budget in terms of data samples processed,
the optimal batch size depends on the noise level and the strong convexity constant.
Experiments use $\alpha = 0.06$, batch sizes $b \in \{5, 40\}$, and 50 epochs, for a total of
$50 \times 1000 = 50{,}000$ sample evaluations in each case.

\begin{remark}[Stochastic approximation and Robbins--Monro]
Mini-batch SGD is a special case of the \emph{stochastic approximation} framework of
Robbins \& Monro (1951). The general form $\theta_{k+1} = \theta_k - \alpha_k H(\theta_k, \xi_k)$
where $\mathbb{E}_{\xi_k}[H(\theta, \xi)] = \nabla J(\theta)$ converges to the root of $\nabla J$
(equivalently the minimiser of $J$) under the Robbins--Monro conditions
$\sum_k \alpha_k = \infty$, $\sum_k \alpha_k^2 < \infty$.
The convergence rate for $\mu$-strongly convex $J$ with gradient variance $\sigma^2$ is
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[\|\theta_k - \theta^\star\|^2] = O\!\left(\frac{\sigma^2}{\mu^2 k}\right)
\end{equation}
with the choice $\alpha_k = c/k$ for $c > 1/(2\mu)$.
The inverse-$k$ decay is optimal in the minimax sense for this problem class.
\end{remark}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Mini-Batch SGD}
\label{alg:sgd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Data $(X, y)$, initial $\theta_0$, learning rate $\alpha$, batch size $b$, epochs $E$
\For{$e = 1, 2, \ldots, E$}
    \State Randomly permute data indices $\{1, \ldots, m\}$
    \For{each mini-batch $\mathcal{B}$ of size $b$}
        \State $g \gets \frac{1}{b} X_{\mathcal{B}}^{\top}(X_{\mathcal{B}}\theta - y_{\mathcal{B}})$
        \State $\theta \gets \theta - \alpha\, g$
    \EndFor
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Fixed vs Decaying Step Size: Rate Comparison}

A critical insight for SGD is the role of the learning-rate schedule.
With a \emph{fixed} step size $\alpha$, SGD does not converge to~$f^\star$ but instead oscillates
in a neighbourhood of radius $O(\alpha \sigma^2)$ where $\sigma^2$ is the gradient variance:
\begin{equation}
\liminf_{k \to \infty} \mathbb{E}[f(\theta_k) - f^\star] \;\leq\; \frac{\alpha \sigma^2}{2\mu}.
\end{equation}
This gives a \emph{convergence floor} that does not vanish as $k \to \infty$.
With a \emph{decaying} step size $\alpha_k = \alpha_0/(1 + ck)$ satisfying
$\sum_k \alpha_k = \infty$ and $\sum_k \alpha_k^2 < \infty$ (the Robbins--Monro conditions),
SGD achieves exact convergence to~$f^\star$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[f(\theta_k) - f^\star] = O\!\left(\frac{1}{k}\right) \quad \text{(strongly convex)}.
\end{equation}
Figure~\ref{fig:q2_sgd_decay} demonstrates both regimes on Benchmark~A.

\subsection{Variance Reduction: SVRG and SAGA}

A fundamental limitation of mini-batch SGD is that a fixed step size leads to a convergence
floor (Section above). \emph{Variance-reduction} methods overcome this without decaying steps.
SVRG (Johnson \& Zhang, 2013) maintains a full-gradient snapshot $\tilde{g} = \nabla f(\tilde{x})$
computed every $m$ steps and uses the control-variate update:
\begin{equation}
\theta_{k+1} = \theta_k - \alpha\bigl(\hat{g}_k - \tilde{g}_{i_k} + \tilde{g}\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $\hat{g}_k$ is the stochastic gradient at $\theta_k$ and $\tilde{g}_{i_k}$ is the old
gradient at the snapshot $\tilde{x}$.
This cancels the zero-mean noise components, achieving \emph{zero variance at the optimum}
and enabling linear convergence $O((1-\mu/(L+\mu m^{-1}))^k)$ with a fixed step size.
SAGA (Defazio et al., 2014) maintains a gradient table $g^{(i)}$ for every data point and
uses a similar control variate, achieving the same linear rate without snapshot restarts.
Both methods bridge the gap between full-gradient GD ($O(\kappa \log(1/\epsilon))$ iters but
$O(m)$ gradient cost per iter) and SGD ($O(1/k)$ per data point but no linear convergence
at fixed step): SVRG/SAGA achieve linear convergence at $O(\kappa)$ data-point cost per
effective iteration---the best of both worlds for strongly convex finite-sum problems.

\subsection{Theoretical Connection: Adam as RMSprop$+$Momentum}

Adam can be understood as combining two ideas studied in Q1: Heavy Ball (gradient momentum via $m_t$)
and RMSprop (per-coordinate adaptive scaling via $v_t$).
To see this, consider the Adam update:
\begin{equation}
x_{t+1} = x_t - \alpha \frac{\hat{m}_t}{\sqrt{\hat{v}_t} + \varepsilon}.
\end{equation}
If $\beta_1 = 0$ (no momentum): $m_t = g_t$, $\hat{m}_t = g_t$, recovering RMSprop with bias correction.
If $\beta_2 \to 1$ (all past gradients, no normalisation): $v_t \approx \mathbb{E}[g^2]$ (constant),
$1/\sqrt{v_t} \approx $ constant scaling, recovering a form of Heavy Ball with constant preconditioning.
Adam's success in deep learning stems from this combination: the momentum term $m_t$ reduces gradient
variance and accelerates descent in consistent directions (like Nesterov), while $v_t$ rescales each
coordinate to account for varying curvature magnitudes (like RMSprop).
The theoretical understanding of Adam's convergence is less complete than for Nesterov:
convergence in convex settings requires a decaying learning rate $\alpha_t \to 0$,
whereas the empirical success in deep learning uses a fixed or mildly decaying $\alpha$.

\begin{remark}[Adam non-convergence and AMSGrad]
Reddi et al.\ (2018) showed that Adam can \emph{fail to converge} even on convex problems.
The failure mode arises when the exponential moving average $v_t$ ``forgets'' large past gradients
too quickly: if a large gradient occurs rarely, $v_t$ decreases between rare large-gradient steps,
allowing spuriously large updates when the gradient is small but the denominator $\sqrt{v_t}$ has
also shrunk.
AMSGrad fixes this by replacing $v_t$ with a running maximum $\hat{v}_t = \max(\hat{v}_{t-1}, v_t)$,
ensuring the effective learning rate $\alpha/\sqrt{\hat{v}_t}$ is monotonically non-increasing---the
same monotonicity property that makes Adagrad provably convergent.
Figure~\ref{fig:q2_adam_amsgrad} illustrates this on an adversarial gradient sequence.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Local Approximations of $g(x) = x^4$}

Taylor's theorem provides local polynomial approximations around any differentiable point.
For $g(x) = x^4$ at $x_0 = 0.25$: $g(0.25) = (0.25)^4 = 0.0039$,
$g'(0.25) = 4(0.25)^3 = 0.0625$, $g''(0.25) = 12(0.25)^2 = 0.75$.

\textbf{First-order (linear) approximation:}
\begin{equation}
g(x) \approx q_1(x) = g(x_0) + g'(x_0)(x - x_0)
= 0.0039 + 0.0625(x - 0.25).
\end{equation}

\textbf{Second-order (quadratic) approximation:}
\begin{equation}
g(x) \approx q_2(x) = g(x_0) + g'(x_0)(x - x_0) + \tfrac{1}{2}g''(x_0)(x - x_0)^2
= 0.0039 + 0.0625(x - 0.25) + 0.375(x - 0.25)^2.
\end{equation}

The \emph{Newton step} minimises the second-order approximation by setting $q_2'(x) = 0$:
\begin{equation}
x_{k+1} = x_k - \frac{g'(x_k)}{g''(x_k)}
= 0.25 - \frac{0.0625}{0.75} = 0.25 - 0.0833 = 0.1667.
\end{equation}
Applying Newton's method from $x_0 = 0.25$ to $g(x) = x^4$ gives
$x_1 = 0.1667$, $x_2 = 0.1111$, $x_3 = 0.0741$, $\ldots$,
converging to the true minimum at $x^\star = 0$.
The convergence is \emph{superlinear} ($g'(x)/g''(x) = x/3$, so $x_{k+1} = 2x_k/3$,
giving linear convergence here since $g'(x^\star) = g''(x^\star) = 0$ simultaneously).

\subsubsection{Newton Convergence for $g(x) = x^4$}

For $g(x) = x^4$ at the minimum $x^\star = 0$, note that $g'(0) = g''(0) = 0$ simultaneously,
so the function is degenerate (the second-order model $q_2$ is also minimised at 0, but with
zero Hessian). The Newton step from $x_k > 0$ gives:
\[
x_{k+1} = x_k - \frac{g'(x_k)}{g''(x_k)} = x_k - \frac{4x_k^3}{12x_k^2} = x_k - \frac{x_k}{3} = \frac{2x_k}{3}.
\]
This is \emph{linear} convergence (ratio $2/3$), not quadratic, because the Hessian $g''(x^\star) = 0$
violates the non-degeneracy assumption in the quadratic convergence proof.
The general principle: at a $p$-th order zero of the Hessian, Newton converges at rate
$x_{k+1} \sim \frac{p}{p+1} x_k$, which approaches 1 (slow linear convergence) as $p \to \infty$.

\subsection{Newton's Method for Multivariate Optimisation}

In $d$ dimensions, Newton's method minimises the local second-order model:
\begin{equation}
x_{k+1} = x_k - \bigl[\nabla^2 f(x_k)\bigr]^{-1} \nabla f(x_k).
\end{equation}
This is equivalent to solving the linear system $\nabla^2 f(x_k)\, p_k = \nabla f(x_k)$
for the Newton direction $p_k$ and then taking $x_{k+1} = x_k - p_k$.
A damping factor $\alpha \leq 1$ is applied when the Hessian is ill-conditioned or
the quadratic approximation is inaccurate far from the optimum:
$x_{k+1} = x_k - \alpha\, p_k$.

\begin{proposition}[Quadratic convergence of Newton's method]
Assume $f$ is twice continuously differentiable and the Hessian $\nabla^2 f(x^\star)$ is positive
definite at a local minimum $x^\star$. Then Newton's method with $\alpha = 1$ satisfies
$\|x_{k+1} - x^\star\| = O(\|x_k - x^\star\|^2)$ in a neighbourhood of $x^\star$,
i.e., the number of correct digits doubles at each iteration.
\end{proposition}

For a \emph{quadratic} function $f(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}x^\top H x - b^\top x$,
the Newton step with $\alpha = 1$ computes the exact minimiser in one step, regardless of the
starting point:
$x_{k+1} = x_k - H^{-1}(Hx_k - b) = H^{-1}b = x^\star$.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Newton's Method with Tikhonov Regularisation}
\label{alg:newton}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha \leq 1$, regularisation $\lambda > 0$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $H_k \gets \nabla^2 f(x_k) + \lambda I$
    \Comment{Tikhonov regularisation ensures $H_k \succ 0$}
    \State Solve $H_k\, p_k = g_k$ for $p_k$
    \Comment{Newton direction}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, p_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Settings: GD uses 80 iterations; Newton uses only 20 iterations with $\lambda = 10^{-8}$.
The small regularisation $\lambda = 10^{-8}$ maintains positive definiteness numerically
without materially changing the Hessian.

\subsection{Region of Quadratic Convergence}

Theorem~\ref{thm:newton_quad} guarantees quadratic convergence \emph{locally}---but how large is
the region of attraction? Let $M = \sup_{x}\|\nabla^3 f(x)\|$ be a bound on the third
derivative. Newton's method achieves quadratic convergence when
\begin{equation}
\|x_0 - x^\star\| \;<\; \frac{2\mu}{M},
\end{equation}
where $\mu = \lambda_{\min}(\nabla^2 f(x^\star))$. For Rosenbrock, $\mu \approx 0.40$ and the
third-order tensor norm $M$ is large due to the $400x_1 x_2$ cross-terms, so the region of
attraction is small: this is why a damped Newton step ($\alpha = 0.22$) is needed for the first
few iterations (the \emph{damped Newton phase}), until the iterate enters the region where the
full Newton step achieves quadratic convergence (the \emph{quadratic phase}).
In practice, a Newton-Raphson step is accepted only when the Armijo condition is satisfied;
this hybrid \emph{line-search Newton} guarantees global convergence to a local minimum and
local quadratic convergence.

\subsection{Practical Considerations: Quasi-Newton Methods}

Newton's method requires computing and inverting the $d \times d$ Hessian at each iteration,
costing $O(d^2)$ to evaluate the Hessian and $O(d^3)$ to invert it (or $O(d^2)$ to solve the
linear system via Cholesky).
For small-scale problems ($d = 2$ as here), this cost is negligible, but for large-scale machine
learning ($d \sim 10^6$), storing and inverting the full Hessian is impractical.

\emph{Quasi-Newton methods} (BFGS, L-BFGS) approximate the Hessian (or its inverse) using
gradient differences, maintaining a low-rank update that captures curvature information without
the full $O(d^3)$ cost. The BFGS update after step $k$ is:
\begin{equation}
B_{k+1} = B_k
  + \frac{y_k y_k^\top}{y_k^\top s_k}
  - \frac{B_k s_k s_k^\top B_k}{s_k^\top B_k s_k},
\end{equation}
where $s_k = x_{k+1} - x_k$ and $y_k = \nabla f(x_{k+1}) - \nabla f(x_k)$.
The BFGS update is derived by requiring $B_{k+1}$ to satisfy the \emph{secant condition}
$B_{k+1} s_k = y_k$ (so that the Hessian approximation is exact along the last step direction)
and be symmetric and as close to $B_k$ as possible in a weighted Frobenius norm:
\begin{equation}
B_{k+1} = \arg\min\{\ \|B - B_k\|_{W}^2\ \mid\ Bs_k = y_k,\ B = B^\top\ \}
= B_k + \frac{y_k y_k^\top}{y_k^\top s_k} - \frac{B_k s_k s_k^\top B_k}{s_k^\top B_k s_k}.
\end{equation}
The update satisfies the \emph{curvature condition} $y_k^\top s_k > 0$ (ensured by a Wolfe
line search), which guarantees $B_{k+1} \succ 0$ whenever $B_k \succ 0$.
The limited-memory variant L-BFGS stores only the last $m$ pairs $(s_k, y_k)$ and computes
$B_k^{-1}\nabla f$ via the two-loop recursion in $O(md)$ time instead of $O(d^2)$.
BFGS achieves superlinear convergence (between linear GD and quadratic Newton) and is
the practical workhorse for medium-scale unconstrained optimisation, bridging the gap between
the fully first-order methods of Q1--Q2 and the full second-order Newton of this section.

\begin{remark}[Trust region methods as an alternative]
The damped Newton step $x_{k+1} = x_k - \alpha_k[\nabla^2 f(x_k)]^{-1}\nabla f(x_k)$ with
Armijo line search is one way to globalise Newton's method.
An alternative is the \emph{trust region} approach: instead of line searching along the Newton
direction, restrict the step to a ball $\|p\| \leq \Delta_k$ of radius $\Delta_k$:
\[
p_k^\star = \arg\min_{\|p\| \leq \Delta_k} \bigl[f(x_k) + \nabla f(x_k)^\top p + \tfrac{1}{2}p^\top \nabla^2 f(x_k)\, p\bigr].
\]
The trust radius $\Delta_k$ is expanded/contracted based on the ratio of actual vs predicted decrease.
Trust region Newton achieves the same quadratic convergence as Newton once $\|x_k - x^\star\|$
is small (so $\Delta_k$ is no longer binding), but is more robust in the global phase because it
always makes progress towards the minimiser of the quadratic model within a certified ball.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Part A: Finite Difference and Nesterov Random Search (Benchmark B)}

\subsubsection{Finite Difference Gradient Approximation}

When the gradient is unavailable analytically, it can be approximated by finite differences.
The \emph{forward finite-difference} approximation of each partial derivative is:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x) \approx \frac{f(x + \delta e_i) - f(x)}{\delta},
\end{equation}
where $e_i$ is the $i$-th standard basis vector and $\delta > 0$ is the perturbation.
The approximation error decomposes as:
\begin{equation}
\text{Total error} = \underbrace{\frac{\delta}{2}|f''(x)|}_{\text{truncation error, }O(\delta)}
                    + \underbrace{\frac{2\varepsilon_{\text{mach}}}{\delta}|f(x)|}_{\text{round-off error, }O(1/\delta)},
\end{equation}
yielding an optimal $\delta^* \sim \sqrt{\varepsilon_{\text{mach}}} \approx 10^{-8}$ for
double-precision arithmetic.
The experiments test $\delta = 0.05$ (well within the good range) and $\delta = 0.8$
(too large: truncation error dominates because $0.8 \approx$ the scale of the loss landscape).

\subsubsection{Nesterov Random Search}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Nesterov Random Search (Zeroth-Order)}
\label{alg:nrs}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, perturbation $\delta$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State Sample $u_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)$; normalise: $u_k \gets u_k / \|u_k\|$
    \State $\hat{g}_k \gets \dfrac{f(x_k + \delta u_k) - f(x_k)}{\delta}\, u_k$
    \Comment{Directional derivative estimate in direction $u_k$}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \hat{g}_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The estimator $\hat{g}_k$ is related to the directional derivative:
$\hat{g}_k \approx (u_k^\top \nabla f(x_k))\,u_k$.
Taking the expectation over the random unit direction $u_k$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}_{u_k}[\hat{g}_k]
= \mathbb{E}\!\left[(u_k^\top \nabla f(x_k))\,u_k\right]
= \frac{\nabla f(x_k)}{d}
\end{equation}
(using $\mathbb{E}[u u^\top] = I/d$ for uniform random unit vectors).
Thus Nesterov random search is an \emph{unbiased} stochastic gradient method with
per-step scaling factor $1/d$.
This is \emph{dimension-independent in per-iteration cost} (only 2 function evaluations,
regardless of $d$), but dimension-\emph{dependent in convergence rate}: the variance of
$\hat{g}_k$ scales as $O(\|\nabla f\|^2 d)$, so the signal-to-noise ratio decreases as
$d$ grows, requiring $O(d)$ more iterations to achieve the same accuracy as the full gradient.
For $d = 2$ (as here), the variance penalty is minimal, but for $d = 100$, NRS would
require approximately 100 times more iterations than exact GD.

\begin{remark}[Zeroth-order oracle lower bounds]
For zeroth-order methods (using only function values, no gradients), the information-theoretic
lower bound for finding an $\epsilon$-minimiser of an $L$-smooth function is $\Omega(d/\epsilon)$
function evaluations. Nesterov random search achieves $O(dL/(\mu\epsilon))$ evaluations on
strongly convex functions, matching the lower bound up to logarithmic factors. In contrast,
first-order methods require only $O(\sqrt{L/(\mu\epsilon)})$ gradient evaluations---a factor of
$O(\sqrt{d/\epsilon})$ fewer for $d$ large, quantifying the cost of gradient ignorance.
\end{remark}

\subsubsection{Central vs Forward Finite Differences}

The forward finite-difference approximation has $O(\delta)$ truncation error.
A more accurate alternative is the \emph{central difference}:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x) \approx \frac{f(x + \delta e_i) - f(x - \delta e_i)}{2\delta},
\end{equation}
which cancels the $O(\delta)$ term and achieves $O(\delta^2)$ truncation error (at the cost
of doubling function evaluations per coordinate).
The optimal central-difference step is $\delta^\star_{\text{central}} = \varepsilon_{\text{mach}}^{1/3} \approx 6 \times 10^{-6}$,
which is larger than the forward-difference optimal $\delta^\star_{\text{fwd}} \approx 10^{-8}$
and achieves lower absolute error at the optimum.

\subsection{Part B: Derivative-Free Methods (Benchmark C -- Rosenbrock)}

\subsubsection{Nelder--Mead Simplex Method}

The Nelder--Mead algorithm maintains a simplex of $n+1 = 3$ vertices in $\mathbb{R}^2$,
ordered $x^{(1)}, x^{(2)}, x^{(3)}$ by objective value ($f(x^{(1)}) \leq f(x^{(2)}) \leq f(x^{(3)})$).
Each iteration deforms the simplex via four operations, applied in sequence based on decision rules:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Reflection} ($\alpha_r = 1.0$): reflect the worst vertex $x^{(3)}$ through the
      centroid $\bar{x} = \tfrac{1}{2}(x^{(1)}+x^{(2)})$:
      $x_r = \bar{x} + \alpha_r(\bar{x} - x^{(3)})$.
\item \textbf{Expansion} ($\gamma = 2.0$): if $f(x_r) < f(x^{(1)})$ (reflected is best),
      try $x_e = \bar{x} + \gamma(\bar{x}-x^{(3)})$; accept $x_e$ if better, else accept $x_r$.
\item \textbf{Outside contraction} ($\rho = 0.5$): if $f(x^{(2)}) \leq f(x_r) < f(x^{(3)})$
      (reflected is worse than second-best), try $x_c = \bar{x} + \rho(x_r - \bar{x})$.
\item \textbf{Inside contraction / shrink} ($\sigma = 0.5$): if contraction fails, shrink all
      vertices towards the best: $x^{(i)} \gets x^{(1)} + \sigma(x^{(i)} - x^{(1)})$.
\end{enumerate}
No gradient information is required; the algorithm uses only function evaluations.
The typical ratio of function evaluations per iteration is 1.5--2, making it competitive
with finite-difference gradient methods in low dimensions.
Settings: initial simplex step $= 0.35$, iterations $N = 160$, parameters as listed above.

\begin{remark}[Convergence of Nelder--Mead]
Unlike gradient-based methods, Nelder--Mead has \emph{no general convergence theory} for $d \geq 2$.
McKinnon (1998) constructed a counterexample in $\mathbb{R}^2$ where Nelder--Mead converges to
a non-stationary point. In practice, the algorithm works well for smooth unimodal functions in
low dimensions ($d \leq 6$), but for high-dimensional or multimodal problems, convergence to the
global minimiser is not guaranteed. This makes it a heuristic rather than a principled algorithm,
in contrast to provably convergent zeroth-order methods such as Nesterov random search (which
inherits stochastic gradient convergence guarantees, Section~\ref{sec:q4}).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Problem Setup and KKT Conditions}

The problem is to minimise Benchmark~B subject to the inequality constraint $x_1 \geq 0.5$,
written in standard form with $g(x) = 0.5 - x_1$:
\begin{equation}
\min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^2}\; f(x_1, x_2) = (x_1-1)^2 + 5(x_2-2)^2 + \sin(x_1)
\quad \text{s.t.} \quad g(x) = 0.5 - x_1 \leq 0.
\label{eq:constrained}
\end{equation}
The feasible set is $\mathcal{C} = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^2 : x_1 \geq 0.5\}$, a closed half-space.

The Karush--Kuhn--Tucker (KKT) first-order necessary conditions for a local minimum~$x^\star$ are
necessary under a \emph{constraint qualification} (CQ). The most common CQ is \emph{linear
independence CQ} (LICQ): the active constraint gradients $\nabla g_i(x^\star)$ for active $i$
must be linearly independent. Here $g(x) = 0.5 - x_1$, $\nabla g = -e_1$---trivially linearly
independent (a single vector)---so LICQ holds unconditionally, and KKT conditions are necessary.

The Karush--Kuhn--Tucker (KKT) first-order necessary conditions for a local minimum~$x^\star$ are:
\begin{align}
\nabla f(x^\star) + \mu^\star \nabla g(x^\star) &= 0 \quad &\text{(stationarity)}\notag\\
g(x^\star) &\leq 0 \quad &\text{(primal feasibility)}\notag\\
\mu^\star &\geq 0 \quad &\text{(dual feasibility)}\notag\\
\mu^\star g(x^\star) &= 0 \quad &\text{(complementary slackness)}\notag
\end{align}
With $\nabla g(x) = -e_1$, stationarity gives:
$2(x_1^\star-1)+\cos(x_1^\star) - \mu^\star = 0$ and $10(x_2^\star-2) = 0$,
so $x_2^\star = 2$ always.
Since $x_1^\star \approx 0.582 > 0.5$, the constraint is \emph{inactive} at the optimum
($g(x^\star) < 0$), so by complementary slackness $\mu^\star = 0$, and stationarity reduces
to the unconstrained condition $2(x_1^\star-1)+\cos(x_1^\star) = 0$.
Therefore the \textbf{constrained and unconstrained optima coincide} at $(0.582, 2.0)$---a
deliberate design choice that allows both projected GD and penalty methods to converge to
the same final point, enabling a fair comparison of feasibility enforcement rather than
objective value quality.
Starting from the \emph{infeasible} point $x_0 = (0.2, 4.0)$ tests feasibility recovery speed.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Projected Gradient Descent (PGD)}
\label{alg:pgd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, projection $\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \nabla f(x_k)$
    \Comment{Full gradient step (possibly infeasible)}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets \Pi_{\mathcal{C}}(z_{k+1}) = \bigl(\max(0.5, z_{1}),\, z_{2}\bigr)$
    \Comment{Project: clip $x_1$ to $[0.5, \infty)$}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

For the half-space constraint $x_1 \geq 0.5$, the projection $\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}$ is simply a
coordinate clip: $\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}(z) = (\max(0.5, z_1),\, z_2)$.
This is a $O(1)$ operation per iterate, making PGD very efficient for this constraint.

\begin{proposition}[Properties of the projection operator]
\label{prop:proj}
For any non-empty closed convex set $\mathcal{C} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Uniqueness:} $\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}(z)$ is uniquely defined (strong convexity of $\tfrac{1}{2}\|x-z\|^2$).
\item \textbf{Non-expansiveness:} $\|\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}(z_1) - \Pi_{\mathcal{C}}(z_2)\| \leq \|z_1 - z_2\|$ for all $z_1, z_2$.
\item \textbf{Characterisation:} $y = \Pi_{\mathcal{C}}(z)$ iff $(z-y)^\top(x-y) \leq 0$ for all $x \in \mathcal{C}$.
\item \textbf{Fixed points:} $\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}(z) = z$ iff $z \in \mathcal{C}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

Property~2 (non-expansiveness) is the key guarantee for PGD convergence:
applying the gradient step followed by projection contracts distance to the optimum at rate
$\leq (1-\alpha\mu)$ per iteration, the same as unconstrained GD on strongly convex functions.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Penalty Method (Linear Penalty)}
\label{alg:penalty}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, penalty weight $\lambda > 0$, iterations $N$
\State Define $F_{\lambda}(x) := f(x) + \lambda \max(0,\, 0.5 - x_1)$
\Comment{Penalised (augmented) objective}
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \nabla F_{\lambda}(x_k)$
    \Comment{Gradient of penalised objective:}
    \State \quad where $\nabla F_{\lambda}(x) = \nabla f(x) - \lambda\, e_1\, \mathbf{1}[x_1 < 0.5]$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The penalty method converts the constrained problem into an unconstrained problem by adding a term
that penalises constraint violation. As $\lambda \to \infty$, the penalised minimiser converges
to the constrained minimiser.
A potential improvement is the \emph{augmented Lagrangian} method, which uses both a penalty and
a Lagrange multiplier estimate to achieve exact feasibility at finite $\lambda$.
Here we test $\lambda \in \{0.15, 1.8, 4.5\}$ with appropriate step sizes.

\textbf{Projected GD} enforces feasibility as a hard constraint at every iteration via the
projection oracle, which for this simple constraint is $O(1)$. The method is guaranteed to stay
feasible throughout, making it ideal when constraint satisfaction is non-negotiable at each step.
The key limitation is that the projection must be computationally tractable; for complex
constraint sets (e.g., intersections of non-convex sets), computing $\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}$ can
be expensive.

\textbf{Penalty methods} require no projection oracle, only a modified gradient. The trade-off
is:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item Small $\lambda$: easy to optimise but large constraint violation at convergence.
  \item Large $\lambda$: near-exact feasibility but the penalised objective becomes steep near
        the boundary, requiring smaller step sizes for stability (here $\alpha = 0.03$
        for $\lambda = 4.5$ vs $\alpha = 0.05$ for $\lambda \leq 1.8$).
\end{itemize}
An \emph{augmented Lagrangian} method overcomes this by combining a penalty term with a
Lagrange multiplier estimate $\mu$:
$\mathcal{L}(x, \mu) = f(x) + \mu \max(0, 0.5-x_1) + \tfrac{\rho}{2}\max(0, 0.5-x_1)^2$,
allowing exact feasibility at finite $\rho$ via the dual update $\mu \gets \mu + \rho \max(0, 0.5-x_k)$.
This is the \emph{method of multipliers} (Hestenes 1969, Powell 1969): alternating minimisation
over $x$ and dual ascent over $\mu$ converges to the primal-dual optimum $(x^\star, \mu^\star)$
at a linear rate under mild regularity conditions, without the ill-conditioning of large~$\rho$.
The ADMM (Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers) is a further extension that decomposes
the $x$-subproblem into separable blocks, making it scalable to large-scale distributed
optimisation (e.g., consensus problems in multi-agent systems).

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Augmented Lagrangian Method (Method of Multipliers)}
\label{alg:alm}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial $x_0$, multiplier $\mu_0 \geq 0$, penalty $\rho > 0$, tolerance $\epsilon$
\For{$k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets \arg\min_x \bigl[f(x) + \mu_k \max(0, 0.5-x_1)
           + \tfrac{\rho}{2}\max(0, 0.5-x_1)^2\bigr]$
    \Comment{Inexact minimisation via gradient descent}
    \State $\mu_{k+1} \gets \mu_k + \rho \max(0,\, 0.5 - x_{k+1,1})$
    \Comment{Dual ascent: increase multiplier proportional to violation}
    \If{$\max(0, 0.5 - x_{k+1,1}) \leq \epsilon$ and $\|\nabla_x \mathcal{L}(x_{k+1}, \mu_{k+1})\| \leq \epsilon$}
        \State \textbf{break}
    \EndIf
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The key advantage over pure penalty methods: at convergence, the dual update $\mu^{k+1}$
estimates $\mu^\star = 0$ (since the constraint is inactive), confirming KKT complementary
slackness automatically without requiring $\rho \to \infty$.

The \emph{Lagrangian dual function} is $D(\mu) = \min_{x} \mathcal{L}(x, \mu, 0)
= \min_x [f(x) + \mu(0.5-x_1)]$. The dual problem $\max_{\mu \geq 0} D(\mu)$ always provides
a lower bound on the primal optimum ($D(\mu) \leq f^\star$), and for convex problems with a
Slater constraint qualification (there exists $\bar{x}$ with $g(\bar{x}) < 0$), strong duality
holds: $D(\mu^\star) = f^\star$. Here the Slater point is $\bar{x} = (0.6, 2)$ with $g(\bar{x}) = -0.1 < 0$,
so strong duality holds and the dual multiplier $\mu^\star = 0$ (constraint inactive at $x_1^\star \approx 0.582$).

\subsection{Feasible Set and Linear Programme}

The feasible set is the box $\mathcal{X} = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^2 : 0.5 \leq x_1 \leq 5,\ {-5} \leq x_2 \leq 10\}$.

\textbf{Linear Programme (Q6-I):}
Minimise $f(x) = a^\top x = x_1 + 2x_2$ over $\mathcal{X}$ where $a = [1, 2]^\top$.
Since both components of $a$ are strictly positive, $f$ is strictly increasing in both $x_1$ and
$x_2$, so the minimum is achieved at the corner with smallest coordinates:
\begin{equation}
x^\star = (0.5,\, {-5}), \qquad f^\star = 0.5 + 2(-5) = -9.5.
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[LP over polytope]
The minimum of a linear function $f(x) = a^\top x$ over a bounded convex polytope $\mathcal{X}$
is always attained at a \emph{vertex} (extreme point) of $\mathcal{X}$.
\end{proposition}

This fundamental result---equivalent to the basic feasible solution theorem in simplex
methods---follows from the fact that a linear function has no interior stationary points;
the gradient $a$ is constant and nonzero, so the level sets $\{x : a^\top x = c\}$ are parallel
hyperplanes, and the minimum on $\mathcal{X}$ is where these planes first touch the set,
which is always a vertex.

\subsection{Frank--Wolfe Algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Frank--Wolfe (Conditional Gradient)}
\label{alg:fw}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial feasible point $x_0 \in \mathcal{X}$, mixing parameter $\beta \in (0,1)$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $z_k \gets \arg\min_{x \in \mathcal{X}}\, g_k^\top x$
    \Comment{Linear minimisation oracle (LMO): LP subproblem}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets \beta\, x_k + (1 - \beta)\, z_k$
    \Comment{Convex combination: $x_{k+1} \in \mathcal{X}$ since both $x_k, z_k \in \mathcal{X}$}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The key advantage of Frank--Wolfe over projected gradient descent is that it replaces the
\emph{projection} step (which can be expensive for complex sets) with a \emph{linear
minimisation} step.
For box constraints, this LP decomposes per coordinate in $O(d)$:
\begin{equation}
(z_k)_i = \begin{cases} l_i & \text{if } (\nabla f(x_k))_i > 0 \\ u_i & \text{if } (\nabla f(x_k))_i \leq 0 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
i.e., select the bound that minimises the linear objective.
The Frank--Wolfe gap $\gamma_k = g_k^\top(x_k - z_k) \geq 0$ serves as a certificate of
convergence: $\gamma_k = 0$ if and only if $x_k$ is the Frank--Wolfe optimal solution
(i.e., satisfies the first-order necessary conditions over $\mathcal{X}$).

\begin{proposition}[Frank--Wolfe convergence rate]
For a smooth convex function $f$ with Lipschitz-continuous gradient ($L$-smooth) over a compact
convex set $\mathcal{X}$ with diameter $D = \max_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\|x-y\|$, the Frank--Wolfe
algorithm with decaying step $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}
f(x_k) - f^\star \leq \frac{2LD^2}{k+2} = O(1/k).
\end{equation}
This $O(1/k)$ rate is tight and cannot be improved without additional curvature information.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
By $L$-smoothness, after one Frank--Wolfe step with step-size $\gamma = \gamma_k$:
\[
f(x_{k+1}) \leq f(x_k) + \gamma \nabla f(x_k)^\top(z_k - x_k) + \frac{\gamma^2 L}{2}\|z_k - x_k\|^2.
\]
Since $\nabla f(x_k)^\top(x_k - z_k) \geq f(x_k) - f^\star$ (convexity + LP choice of $z_k$):
\[
f(x_{k+1}) - f^\star \leq (1-\gamma_k)(f(x_k) - f^\star) + \frac{\gamma_k^2 L D^2}{2}.
\]
Substituting $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$ and applying induction gives $f(x_k) - f^\star \leq 2LD^2/(k+2)$.
\end{proof}

For our box $\mathcal{X} = [0.5,5]\times[-5,10]$: diameter $D = \|(0.5,-5)-(5,10)\| = \sqrt{4.5^2+15^2}\approx 15.7$.
With $L = 2$ for the interior objective, the bound gives $\approx 493/(k+2)$---conservative
but confirming the $O(1/k)$ asymptotic.

The implementation here uses a fixed $\beta$ rather than the decaying schedule $1-\gamma_k =
1-2/(k+2)$, which can converge faster in practice when $\beta$ is tuned.

\subsection{Frank--Wolfe Gap as a Convergence Certificate}

A distinctive feature of Frank--Wolfe is the \emph{computable} optimality gap
$\gamma_k = \nabla f(x_k)^\top(x_k - z_k)$, which upper-bounds the true optimality gap:
\begin{equation}
f(x_k) - f^\star \;\leq\; \gamma_k \;=\; \nabla f(x_k)^\top(x_k - z_k).
\end{equation}
This follows from convexity: $f(z_k) \geq f(x_k) + \nabla f(x_k)^\top(z_k - x_k)$
implies $\nabla f(x_k)^\top(x_k - z_k) \geq f(x_k) - f(z_k) \geq f(x_k) - f^\star$.
The gap is zero if and only if $x_k$ is a constrained stationary point---making it both
a convergence criterion and a certificate that can be reported alongside the iterate.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/q6_fw_gap.pdf}
\caption{Q6: Frank--Wolfe convergence gap $\gamma_k = \nabla f(x_k)^\top(x_k-z_k)$ vs iteration
for the interior and boundary objective functions.
\textbf{Left (interior, $f^\star = 0$):} Both $\beta = 0.90$ and $\beta = 0.985$ show overall

$\beta = 0.985$ suppresses these oscillations at the cost of slower initial decay.
\textbf{Right (boundary, $f^\star = 0.25$):} The gap decays more smoothly because the LMO

\subsection{Interior vs Boundary Behaviour}

\textbf{Interior optimum:}
Frank--Wolfe converges to the interior optimum but exhibits vertex cycling: the LP vertex $z_k$
jumps between extreme points of $\mathcal{X}$ as the gradient direction rotates around zero.
The convergence rate is $O(1/k)$, and the oscillation means many iterations are needed to
reduce the gap to small values.
The Frank--Wolfe gap $\gamma_k = \nabla f(x_k)^\top(x_k - z_k) \geq 0$ is a \emph{computable}
certificate: $\gamma_k = 0$ iff $x_k$ is a constrained stationary point.
The fixed-$\beta$ schedule used here does not provide a decaying step towards $z_k$;
the optimal decaying schedule $1-\beta_k = \gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$ would provide the provable
$O(1/k)$ convergence guarantee.

\textbf{Boundary optimum:}
Frank--Wolfe is \emph{better suited} to boundary optima because $z_k$ naturally selects the
vertex closest to the constrained minimiser. The iterates are convex combinations of feasible
points, ensuring feasibility without projection, and the LP vertex repeatedly chosen agrees with
the constraint-active direction. The boundary case converges to $f = 0.364$ after 140 iterations
(versus $f^\star = 0.25$), indicating more iterations are needed; the fixed $\beta = 0.93$ does
not decay, so the method cannot fully settle on the boundary.

\textbf{Sparsity-inducing property:}
Since each FW update $x_{k+1} = (1-\gamma_k)x_k + \gamma_k z_k$ is a convex combination
of the previous iterate and a single vertex $z_k$, the iterates are always contained in the
convex hull of at most $k+1$ vertices.
This makes FW \emph{atom-sparsity-inducing}: for sparse constraint sets (simplex, nuclear norm ball),
the FW iterate has at most $k+1$ non-zero atoms, providing automatic regularisation.
This is the reason FW is used in Lasso (where vertices are $\pm e_i$) and low-rank matrix
recovery (where vertices are rank-1 matrices). Figure~\ref{fig:q6_fw_sparsity} illustrates this.

\textbf{Comparison with projected gradient descent (Q5):}
For simple constraint sets like the box $\mathcal{X}$ or the half-space $\{x : x_1 \geq 0.5\}$,
the projection $\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}$ is $O(d)$ and as cheap as the FW linear oracle.
In such cases, projected GD with Nesterov acceleration achieves $O(1/k^2)$ convergence,
which is strictly faster than FW's $O(1/k)$.
Frank--Wolfe is the preferred choice when~$\mathcal{X}$ is an intersection of many constraints
(e.g., the nuclear norm ball, the simplex, the flow polytope), where projection is expensive
but LP minimisation has combinatorial algorithms (e.g., sorting for the simplex).
The sparsity-inducing property of FW (iterates are convex combinations of a few vertices)
is also valuable in $\ell^1$-constrained problems (e.g., Lasso) and matrix rank constraints.

\item \textbf{Adaptive step sizes (Q1):} Polyak achieves the fastest convergence on Rosenbrock
      when $f^\star$ is known, but diverges catastrophically on benchmarks with a noise floor
      ($f^\star_{\text{true}} > 0$ but $f^\star_{\text{set}} = 0$). Adagrad and RMSprop are
      robust, with RMSprop preferred over Adagrad when non-stationarity is expected (its
      exponential average prevents excessive learning-rate decay).

\item \textbf{Momentum methods (Q2):} Nesterov acceleration achieves the theoretically optimal
      $O(1/k^2)$ first-order rate and demonstrates the largest practical benefit on the hardest
      benchmark (Rosenbrock: $7\times$ improvement over GD in 150 iterations). Adam is competitive
      on well-conditioned problems but does not match Nesterov's temporal momentum advantage on
      highly nonlinear, nonconvex surfaces.

\item \textbf{Newton's method (Q3):} Second-order information provides dramatic speedup: one-step
      convergence on quadratics (Benchmark A), 4--5 iterations on near-quadratic functions
      (Benchmark B), and $5.4\times$ improvement on Rosenbrock in only 20 vs 80 iterations.
      The primary cost is forming and inverting the $d \times d$ Hessian ($O(d^3)$ per step),
      which becomes prohibitive for large-scale problems---motivating quasi-Newton methods
      (BFGS, L-BFGS) that approximate the Hessian using gradient differences.

\item \textbf{Derivative-free methods (Q4):} Finite differences with $\delta \approx
      \sqrt{\varepsilon_{\text{mach}}}$ closely match exact GD, making them attractive when
      analytical gradients are unavailable. Nelder--Mead and grid search are useful for
      low-dimensional global search but suffer from the curse of dimensionality for $d > 5$.

\item \textbf{Constrained optimisation (Q5):} Projected gradient descent achieves immediate and
      permanent feasibility via the projection oracle, making it the method of choice when
      constraint satisfaction is mandatory at every iterate. Penalty methods with large $\lambda$
      achieve near-identical results but require smaller step sizes, and the penalty parameter
      must be tuned. Augmented Lagrangian methods (not implemented here) overcome the step-size
      limitation by incorporating Lagrange multiplier updates.

\item \textbf{Frank--Wolfe (Q6):} The projection-free nature of FW is its key advantage for
      complex constraint sets, but the $O(1/k)$ convergence rate and vertex-cycling phenomenon
      make it slower than projected gradient descent when projections are cheap. FW is best
      suited to problems where (i) the constraint set has a tractable LP oracle but expensive
      projection, and (ii) the optimal solution is sparse or lies on the boundary of $\mathcal{X}$
      (a common pattern in machine learning, e.g., $\ell^1$-constrained problems).
\end{enumerate}

The cost analysis highlights three regimes: (i) $O(d)$ first-order methods dominate for large-scale
ML where $d \sim 10^6$--$10^9$; (ii) $O(d^2)$ quasi-Newton methods (BFGS) are optimal for

cost scaling correctly predicts which methods would remain viable for production-scale problems.

\subsection{Practical Recommendations}

Based on the experimental results across all six questions, the following practical guidance emerges:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Smooth, strongly convex, small $d$:} Use Newton's method or a quasi-Newton
      method (BFGS). One order of magnitude fewer iterations than any first-order method.
\item \textbf{Smooth, moderately convex, $d \leq 10^4$:} Use L-BFGS or Nesterov momentum.
      Both achieve near-optimal first-order rates.
\item \textbf{Large-scale, stochastic gradient available ($d \sim 10^6$):} Use Adam or
      AdamW with a tuned learning rate schedule. The bias correction and per-coordinate
      scaling make it robust to heterogeneous gradient scales across layers/parameters.
\item \textbf{Constrained, cheap projection:} Use projected gradient descent with Nesterov
      acceleration for the optimal $O(1/k^2)$ rate.
\item \textbf{Constrained, expensive projection but cheap LP:} Use Frank--Wolfe with the
      standard $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$ schedule for the provable $O(1/k)$ rate.
\item \textbf{Gradient unavailable, low $d$ ($\leq 10$):} Use Nelder--Mead or finite
      differences with optimal $\delta = \sqrt{\varepsilon_{\text{mach}}}$.
\item \textbf{Gradient unavailable, high $d$:} Use Nesterov random search (zeroth-order
      stochastic gradient), which scales as $O(d)$ per iteration.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Gradient Descent}]
def gradient_descent(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        x = x - alpha * grad_fn(x)
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Polyak Step Size}]
def polyak_step(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, f_star, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        alpha_k = (loss_fn(x) - f_star) / (np.dot(g, g) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(alpha_k)
        x = x - alpha_k * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Adagrad}]
def adagrad(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha0, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    G = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        G += g**2
        eff_alpha = alpha0 / (np.sqrt(G) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(np.mean(eff_alpha))
        x = x - eff_alpha * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={RMSprop}]
def rmsprop(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha0, beta, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    v = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        v = beta * v + (1 - beta) * g**2
        eff_alpha = alpha0 / (np.sqrt(v) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(np.mean(eff_alpha))
        x = x - eff_alpha * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Heavy Ball / Polyak Momentum}]
def heavy_ball(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    z = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        z = beta * z + alpha * g
        x = x - z
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Nesterov Momentum}]
def nesterov_momentum(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta_max, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    z = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for k in range(1, n_iters + 1):
        beta_k = min((k - 1) / (k + 2), beta_max)
        lookahead = x + beta_k * z
        g = grad_fn(lookahead)
        z = beta_k * z - alpha * g
        x = x + z
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Adam Optimiser}]
def adam_optimiser(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta1, beta2, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    m_vec, v_vec = np.zeros_like(x), np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for t in range(1, n_iters + 1):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        m_vec = beta1 * m_vec + (1 - beta1) * g
        v_vec = beta2 * v_vec + (1 - beta2) * g**2
        m_hat = m_vec / (1 - beta1**t)
        v_hat = v_vec / (1 - beta2**t)
        x = x - alpha * m_hat / (np.sqrt(v_hat) + eps)
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Newton's Method with Tikhonov Regularisation}]
def newtons_method(grad_fn, hess_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, n_iters, damping=1e-8):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, update_norms = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        H = hess_fn(x) if callable(hess_fn) else hess_fn
        H_reg = H + damping * np.eye(len(x))
        try:
            p = np.linalg.solve(H_reg, g)
        except np.linalg.LinAlgError:
            p = g
        update_norms.append(np.linalg.norm(alpha * p))
        x = x - alpha * p
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(update_norms)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Frank--Wolfe Algorithm}]
def frank_wolfe(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, bounds, beta, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, z_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        z = np.array([bounds[i][0] if g[i] > 0 else bounds[i][1]
                       for i in range(len(g))])
        z_hist.append(z.copy())
        x = beta * x + (1 - beta) * z
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(z_hist)
\end{lstlisting}
