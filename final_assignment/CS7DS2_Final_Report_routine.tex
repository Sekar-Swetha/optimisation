\begin{abstract}
This report presents a comprehensive comparative study of first-order, second-order, derivative-free, and constrained optimisation algorithms applied to three canonical benchmark problems: a linear regression quadratic loss (Benchmark A), a toy neural network loss with sinusoidal perturbation (Benchmark B), and the Rosenbrock function (Benchmark C). Six classes of methods are investigated: (1)~adaptive step-size methods including the Polyak rule, Adagrad, RMSprop, and the Heavy Ball method; (2)~momentum-based and stochastic methods encompassing Nesterov acceleration, Adam, and mini-batch SGD; (3)~Newton's method with local quadratic approximations; (4)~derivative-free approaches including finite-difference gradient descent, Nesterov random search, the Nelder--Mead simplex method, and grid search; (5)~constrained optimisation via projected gradient descent and penalty methods; and (6)~the Frank--Wolfe conditional gradient algorithm for linearly constrained problems. Each method is evaluated empirically across all applicable benchmarks and analysed in terms of convergence speed, trajectory behaviour, computational cost per iteration, and sensitivity to hyperparameters. Theoretical convergence guarantees are stated and connected to observed experimental behaviour. Key findings include: the critical dependence of the Polyak step on accurate knowledge of $f^\star$; the $O(1/k^2)$ advantage of Nesterov acceleration over $O(1/k)$ gradient descent for smooth convex functions; Newton's single-step convergence on quadratics and its dramatically superior performance on ill-conditioned problems; the dimension-independent oracle cost of randomised zeroth-order methods; and the projection-free nature of Frank--Wolfe making it suitable for structured constraint sets.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================

Optimisation is the mathematical backbone of modern data analysis and machine learning. The accuracy of every trained model, the speed of every simulation, and the quality of every engineering design ultimately depends on our ability to solve optimisation problems efficiently. Every trained model --- from linear regression to deep neural networks --- is the solution to an optimisation problem of the form
\[
  x^\star \in \arg\min_{x \in \mathcal{X}}\; f(x),
\]
where $f : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$ is the objective (loss) and $\mathcal{X} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$ is the feasible set ($\mathcal{X} = \mathbb{R}^d$ for unconstrained problems). The choice of algorithm, step size, and stopping criterion determines whether a model trains in seconds or hours, and whether it converges to a useful solution at all.

This report investigates the algorithmic landscape of first-order, second-order, derivative-free, and constrained optimisation methods, studying their behaviour on three benchmark functions that capture different structural properties: a well-conditioned convex quadratic, a mildly non-convex function with moderate anisotropy, and the Rosenbrock function with extreme ill-conditioning and a narrow curved valley.

The experiments are organised into six questions corresponding to distinct methodological families:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Q1}: Adaptive step-size methods --- adapting the learning rate to the local geometry without requiring Hessian information.
  \item \textbf{Q2}: Momentum methods and stochastic gradient descent --- accelerating convergence using gradient history.
  \item \textbf{Q3}: Newton's method --- exploiting second-order curvature information for faster convergence.
  \item \textbf{Q4}: Derivative-free optimisation --- optimising without access to analytical or automatic gradients.
  \item \textbf{Q5}: Constrained optimisation --- handling feasibility constraints via projection and penalty approaches.
  \item \textbf{Q6}: Linear programmes and the Frank--Wolfe algorithm --- exploiting the structure of linearly constrained problems.
\end{enumerate}

All experiments use \texttt{numpy} with seed \texttt{42} for reproducibility, and figures are saved as vector-format PDF files in the \texttt{figures/} directory.

A central organising principle of this report is the \emph{oracle model}: each algorithm interacts with the objective function through specific query types. First-order methods use a \emph{gradient oracle} (providing $f(x)$ and $\nabla f(x)$ at query point $x$ in $O(d)$ time), second-order methods use a \emph{Hessian oracle} (additionally providing $\nabla^2 f(x)$), and derivative-free methods use only a \emph{zeroth-order} (function value) oracle. Comparing methods under a common \emph{oracle complexity} (number of oracle calls to achieve $\epsilon$-accuracy) allows fair cross-category comparison, independent of implementation details. Table~\ref{tab:rates} (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}) summarises the oracle complexities for all methods investigated.

A second organising principle is the distinction between \emph{structural assumptions} that methods exploit: $L$-smoothness (bounds gradient change), $\mu$-strong convexity (guarantees a unique minimum with a quadratic lower bound), compact constraint sets (enabling Frank--Wolfe), and structured constraint sets (enabling efficient projection or LP oracles). Each section identifies which assumptions are exploited and how they translate to convergence guarantees or algorithmic choices.

\begin{remark}[Historical development of first-order optimisation theory]
The modern theory of first-order optimisation was largely developed between 1960 and 2000, driven by computational constraints that made second-order methods impractical:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Cauchy (1847)}: Proposed the method of steepest descent (gradient descent) for minimising a function, noting convergence on quadratics but not proving rates.
  \item \textbf{Frank \& Wolfe (1956)}: Introduced the conditional gradient algorithm for quadratic programming, which was the first iterative method for linearly constrained convex optimisation.
  \item \textbf{Polyak (1964)}: Introduced the heavy ball method and the Polyak step size, establishing the first momentum-based acceleration for quadratic objectives.
  \item \textbf{Nesterov (1983)}: Proved the first $O(1/k^2)$ convergence rate via estimate sequences, establishing the modern framework for optimal first-order methods. Simultaneously proved the $\Omega(1/k^2)$ lower bound, showing his method is optimal.
  \item \textbf{Nemirovskii \& Yudin (1983)}: Established information-theoretic lower bounds for first-order and zeroth-order methods, creating the oracle complexity framework used throughout this report.
  \item \textbf{Duchi, Hazan \& Singer (2011)}: Introduced Adagrad, adapting step sizes to gradient history and connecting optimisation to online learning theory.
  \item \textbf{Kingma \& Ba (2015)}: Introduced Adam, the current de facto standard optimiser for deep learning, combining RMSprop's adaptive scaling with gradient momentum.
\end{itemize}
This 60-year arc from Cauchy's initial descent idea to Adam reflects the increasing demands of machine learning: from minimising $d \leq 100$ dimensional objectives (where Newton's method suffices) to minimising $d \sim 10^{11}$ parameter deep learning models (where only first-order stochastic methods are tractable).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Notation and Definitions}

Throughout this report we use the following notation.

\begin{definition}[$L$-smoothness]
A differentiable function $f$ is \emph{$L$-smooth} (has $L$-Lipschitz gradient) if
$\|\nabla f(x) - \nabla f(y)\| \leq L\|x - y\|$ for all $x, y$.
Equivalently, $f(y) \leq f(x) + \nabla f(x)^T(y-x) + \tfrac{L}{2}\|y-x\|^2$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[$\mu$-strong convexity]
A differentiable function $f$ is \emph{$\mu$-strongly convex} if
$f(y) \geq f(x) + \nabla f(x)^T(y-x) + \tfrac{\mu}{2}\|y-x\|^2$ for all $x, y$.
\end{definition}

The \emph{condition number} $\kappa = L/\mu$ measures the difficulty of optimisation: large $\kappa$ implies slow gradient descent convergence.

\begin{remark}[Co-coercivity and the descent lemma]
For an $L$-smooth function $f$, the gradient map $\nabla f$ satisfies the \emph{co-coercivity} inequality:
\[
  (\nabla f(x) - \nabla f(y))^T(x - y) \geq \frac{1}{L}\|\nabla f(x) - \nabla f(y)\|^2.
\]
Setting $y = x^\star$ (where $\nabla f(x^\star) = 0$): $\nabla f(x)^T(x - x^\star) \geq \frac{1}{L}\|\nabla f(x)\|^2$. This implies the Polyak step's convergence bound (Remark~\ref{thm:polyak}) and is equivalent to the descent lemma: $f(x_{k+1}) \leq f(x_k) - \frac{1}{2L}\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2$ for $\alpha = 1/L$. Co-coercivity holds if and only if $f$ is $L$-smooth \emph{and convex} (for non-convex $L$-smooth functions, co-coercivity fails; this is why convergence proofs for non-convex functions use only the descent lemma, not co-coercivity).
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Polyak--{\L}ojasiewicz condition: beyond strong convexity]
Strong convexity ($\mu > 0$) guarantees both a unique minimum and linear convergence of GD. A weaker condition, the \emph{Polyak--\L ojasiewicz (PL) condition}:
\[
  \frac{1}{2}\|\nabla f(x)\|^2 \geq \mu(f(x) - f^\star),
\]
is sufficient for linear convergence of GD without requiring the objective to be convex. The PL condition allows multiple global minima (unlike strong convexity) and is satisfied by: (i)~strongly convex functions, (ii)~many neural network training losses in the overparameterised regime, and (iii)~regularised logistic regression. Under the PL condition with $\alpha = 1/L$:
\[
  f(x_{k+1}) - f^\star \leq \left(1 - \frac{\mu}{L}\right)(f(x_k) - f^\star) = \left(1 - \frac{1}{\kappa}\right)(f(x_k) - f^\star).
\]
Rosenbrock does \emph{not} satisfy the PL condition globally (its gradient can be small away from the valley floor while $f$ is far from $f^\star = 0$), explaining why GD's convergence on Benchmark C is far slower than the $O(1/\kappa)$ prediction. The empirical stagnation of GD at $f \approx 3.4$ after 80 iterations is a consequence of the PL condition failing away from the optimum.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Benchmark A: Linear Regression Quadratic Loss}
A synthetic dataset with $m = 1000$ samples and two parameters is generated:
\begin{equation}
\theta^{\star} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad x^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_2), \quad y^{(i)} = \theta^{\star T} x^{(i)} + \varepsilon^{(i)}, \quad \varepsilon^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1).
\end{equation}
The loss function is the mean squared error:
\begin{equation}
J(\theta) = \frac{1}{2m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \left(\theta^T x^{(i)} - y^{(i)}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{2}\theta^T H \theta - b^T\theta + c,
\end{equation}
where $H = X^TX/m$, $b = X^Ty/m$, and $c = y^Ty/(2m)$. This is a strictly convex quadratic. The gradient is $\nabla J(\theta) = H\theta - b = X^T(X\theta - y)/m$ and the Hessian is $H$ (constant). Since $X \in \mathbb{R}^{1000 \times 2}$ with i.i.d.\ $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ entries, by the law of large numbers $H \approx I_2$, giving smoothness constant $L \approx \lambda_{\max}(H) \approx 1$ and strong convexity $\mu \approx \lambda_{\min}(H) \approx 1$, so $\kappa \approx 1$. Due to observation noise, the minimum loss is $J^\star \approx 0.4826$ (not zero), achieved at the OLS estimator $\hat\theta = H^{-1}b$. Starting point: $\theta_0 = [0, 0]^T$.

\begin{remark}[OLS, MLE, and Newton's one-step convergence for Benchmark A]
For Gaussian noise $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$, minimising the MSE $J(\theta)$ is equivalent to maximising the log-likelihood $\log p(y | X, \theta) = -\frac{m}{2}\log(2\pi) - J(\theta)$. The OLS estimator $\hat\theta = (X^TX)^{-1}X^Ty = H^{-1}b$ is therefore also the Maximum Likelihood Estimator (MLE). Newton's method with $\alpha = 1$ computes $\theta_1 = \theta_0 - H^{-1}(H\theta_0 - b) = H^{-1}b = \hat\theta$ in exactly one step, regardless of $\theta_0$. This is not a coincidence: for any strictly convex quadratic, Newton's method is equivalent to solving the normal equations $H\hat\theta = b$ directly, which has a unique solution. The residual variance $J^\star = \mathrm{Var}[\varepsilon]/2 \approx 0.5$ (here $\approx 0.4826$ due to sampling) is the irreducible Cramér-Rao bound for this estimation problem --- no estimator can achieve lower MSE given only the data $(X, y)$ with true parameter $\theta^\star$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Benchmark B: Toy Neural Network Quadratic Loss}
\begin{equation}
f(x_1, x_2) = (x_1 - 1)^2 + 5(x_2 - 2)^2 + \sin(x_1).
\end{equation}
The gradient is $\nabla f = [2(x_1 - 1) + \cos(x_1),\ 10(x_2 - 2)]^T$ and the Hessian is $H(x) = \mathrm{diag}(2 - \sin(x_1),\ 10)$. Because $\sin(x_1) \in [-1, 1]$, the curvature in $x_1$ is bounded in $[1, 3]$, giving $L = 10$ and local strong convexity $\mu \approx 1$ near the minimum, so $\kappa \approx 10$. The function is non-convex globally (the $\sin$ term creates multiple local minima for large $|x_1|$) but is locally strongly convex near its minimum.

The unconstrained minimum lies where $\partial f/\partial x_1 = 2(x_1 - 1) + \cos(x_1) = 0$ and $x_2 = 2$. Solving numerically (bisection on $h(x) = 2x-2+\cos x$) gives $x_1^\star \approx 0.582$, so $x^\star \approx (0.582,\, 2.0)$ with $f^\star = (0.582-1)^2 + \sin(0.582) \approx 0.1745 + 0.5490 \approx 0.7235$. Starting point: $x_0 = [-1, 4]^T$. The value $f \approx 0.7244$ consistently observed in experiments represents near-convergence within the iteration budget, not exact convergence to $f^\star$.

\begin{remark}[Coordinate anisotropy and method selectivity for Benchmark B]
The Hessian of Benchmark B at $x^\star$ is $H(x^\star) = \mathrm{diag}(2 - \sin(0.582), 10) \approx \mathrm{diag}(1.45, 10)$. The 10:1 ratio between the $x_2$ curvature (10) and $x_1$ curvature ($\approx 1.45$) means the gradient in the $x_2$ direction is $\approx 7\times$ more responsive to displacement than the $x_1$ direction. Concretely, at $x = (-1, 4)$ the gradient is $\nabla f = [2(-1-1)+\cos(-1), 10(4-2)] = [-4-0.54, 20] = (-4.54, 20)$: the $x_2$ component dominates. A gradient step with $\alpha = 0.06$ moves $0.06 \times 4.54 = 0.27$ in $x_1$ and $0.06 \times 20 = 1.2$ in $x_2$ --- much larger movement in $x_2$, which has 10$\times$ more restoring force. This asymmetry is why per-coordinate methods (Adam, Adagrad) benefit here: they automatically scale down the $x_2$ step by the larger historical gradient norm, taking more balanced steps across directions.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Benchmark C: Rosenbrock Function}
\begin{equation}
f(x_1, x_2) = (1 - x_1)^2 + 100(x_2 - x_1^2)^2.
\end{equation}
Introduced by Rosenbrock (1960), this function became the ``banana function'' (named after the shape of its level curves) and is the canonical hard test for optimisation algorithms. Its difficulty is not due to multiple local minima (of which there are none in $\mathbb{R}^2$) but rather the combination of:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Extreme ill-conditioning}: $\kappa \approx 2504$ at the optimum (and even larger during descent from $(-1,1)$).
  \item \textbf{Curved valley}: the level sets follow the parabola $x_2 = x_1^2$ rather than ellipses, so no affine reparametrisation can simultaneously remove both the anisotropy and the curvature.
  \item \textbf{Long traverse}: from $(-1,1)$ to $(1,1)$, the valley floor distance is $\int_{-1}^{1}\sqrt{1 + (2x)^2}\,dx \approx 3.5$, but the valley width $\sim 1/\sqrt{100} = 0.1$, requiring careful step control throughout.
\end{enumerate}

The global minimum is at $(1, 1)$ with $f^\star = 0$. Note that while Rosenbrock is non-convex globally (e.g.\ $\nabla^2 f$ can be indefinite), from the starting point $(-1, 1)$ all gradient methods descend toward the same global minimum without getting trapped in local minima; this is because Rosenbrock has no spurious local minima --- the global minimum is the unique critical point. The Hessian at the optimum is
\[
H(1,1) = \begin{bmatrix} 802 & -400 \\ -400 & 200 \end{bmatrix},
\]
with eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \approx 1001.6$ and $\lambda_2 \approx 0.40$, giving condition number $\kappa \approx 2504$. This extreme ill-conditioning, combined with a narrow curved valley along $x_2 = x_1^2$, makes Rosenbrock one of the canonical hard test functions. Note that Rosenbrock is \emph{not} convex globally: $\nabla^2 f$ can be indefinite away from the optimum. Starting point: $x_0 = [-1, 1]^T$.

\textbf{Hessian derivation:} $f = (1-x_1)^2 + 100(x_2-x_1^2)^2$, so $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1} = -2(1-x_1) + 200(x_2-x_1^2)(-2x_1) = -2+2x_1 - 400x_1(x_2-x_1^2)$, $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2} = 200(x_2-x_1^2)$. Second derivatives: $h_{11} = 2 + 1200x_1^2 - 400x_2$, $h_{12} = -400x_1$, $h_{22} = 200$. At $(1,1)$: $h_{11} = 2 + 1200 - 400 = 802$, $h_{12} = -400$, $h_{22} = 200$. Eigenvalues from $\lambda^2 - \mathrm{tr}(H)\lambda + \det(H) = \lambda^2 - 1002\lambda + (802\times200 - 400^2) = \lambda^2 - 1002\lambda + 400 = 0$ (since $802\times200 = 160400$ and $400^2 = 160000$): discriminant $= 1002^2 - 4\times400 = 1{,}004{,}004 - 1600 = 1{,}002{,}404$, so $\lambda = \frac{1002 \pm \sqrt{1{,}002{,}404}}{2} \approx \frac{1002 \pm 1001.2}{2}$, giving $\lambda_{\max} \approx 1001.6$ and $\lambda_{\min} \approx 0.4$, and $\kappa = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min} \approx 1001.6/0.4 = 2504$. ✓

\begin{remark}[Rosenbrock valley geometry]
The Rosenbrock function's difficulty arises from its curved narrow valley. The level sets of $f(x_1, x_2) = 100(x_2 - x_1^2)^2 + (1-x_1)^2$ are elongated and curved, following the parabola $x_2 = x_1^2$. Along the valley floor (where $x_2 \approx x_1^2$), the second-order behaviour in the \emph{transverse} direction (perpendicular to the valley) has curvature $\approx 200$ (the $x_2$ curvature), while along the valley \emph{floor} the curvature is $\approx 2$ (from the $(1-x_1)^2$ term), giving the $500:1$ anisotropy. This means any descent method must take very small steps to avoid overshooting the valley walls, while simultaneously needing to traverse the long, gently sloping floor from $(-1, 1)$ to $(1, 1)$. The coupling between $x_1$ and $x_2$ through the term $100(x_2 - x_1^2)^2$ means per-coordinate step adaptation (Adam, Adagrad) is less effective than whole-vector momentum (Nesterov), since the gradient in $x_2$ depends on $x_1$ and vice versa --- the ``natural'' coordinate system is curvilinear (along/across the valley), not aligned with the standard axes.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Benchmark D: Constrained Feasible Set}
For Questions 5 and 6: $\mathcal{X} = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^2 : 0.5 \leq x_1 \leq 5,\ -5 \leq x_2 \leq 10\}$.

This is a \emph{box constraint} (Cartesian product of closed intervals), with several computationally convenient properties:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Projection} $\Pi_\mathcal{X}(z) = (\mathrm{clip}(z_1; 0.5, 5),\; \mathrm{clip}(z_2; -5, 10))$ is $O(d)$ via per-coordinate clipping.
  \item \textbf{LP oracle} $\arg\min_{z\in\mathcal{X}} c^Tz$ selects $z_i = l_i$ if $c_i > 0$, else $z_i = u_i$ --- also $O(d)$.
  \item \textbf{Diameter} $D = \|(5-0.5, 10-(-5))\| = \|(4.5, 15)\| = \sqrt{20.25 + 225} \approx 15.66$, bounding the Frank--Wolfe convergence constant.
  \item \textbf{Slater's condition} holds since $\mathrm{int}(\mathcal{X}) \neq \emptyset$ (any interior point $x$ with $0.5 < x_1 < 5$ and $-5 < x_2 < 10$).
\end{itemize}
The box is deliberately chosen so that the unconstrained minimum of Benchmark B ($x_1^\star \approx 0.582 > 0.5$) lies inside $\mathcal{X}$ (constraint inactive), while the unconstrained minimum of $x_1^2 + x_2^2$ (at origin) lies outside (constraint active). This allows Q5 and Q6 to examine both inactive and active constraint scenarios.

\section{Question 1: Adaptive Step-Size Methods \hfill [30 marks]}
\label{sec:q1}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Theoretical Background}

\begin{remark}[GD on quadratics: exact per-eigenvector analysis]
For a quadratic $f(\theta) = \frac{1}{2}\theta^T H \theta - b^T \theta$ with eigendecomposition $H = Q\Lambda Q^T$ (columns of $Q$ are eigenvectors, $\Lambda = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_d)$), letting $\tilde\theta_k = Q^T(\theta_k - \theta^\star)$ (error in the eigenvector basis):
\[
  (\tilde\theta_{k+1})_i = (1 - \alpha\lambda_i)(\tilde\theta_k)_i, \quad i = 1,\ldots,d.
\]
Each eigenvector converges independently at rate $|1 - \alpha\lambda_i|^k$. The optimal step $\alpha = 2/(\lambda_{\min}+\lambda_{\max})$ balances the two extreme eigenvectors: $|1-\alpha\lambda_{\min}| = |1-\alpha\lambda_{\max}| = (\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1)$. For Benchmark A with $H \approx I$ ($\lambda_i \approx 1$ for all $i$): $\alpha = 1$ is optimal and all components converge at rate $|1-1|^k = 0$ in one step (as Newton's method confirms). For Benchmark C (Rosenbrock at the optimum, eigenvalues $\approx 0.4$ and $1001.6$): with $\alpha = 0.0012$, the slow eigenvector ($\lambda = 0.4$) converges at $|1-0.0012\times0.4|^k = 0.99952^k$ and the fast eigenvector ($\lambda = 1001.6$) at $|1-0.0012\times1001.6|^k = 0.7981^k$ --- so the slow eigenvector is the bottleneck, requiring $\approx 10000$ steps for $10^{-4}$ accuracy.
\end{remark}

For gradient descent with constant step size $\alpha$, the convergence rate on an $L$-smooth $\mu$-strongly convex function is:
\begin{theorem}[GD convergence, strongly convex]\label{thm:gd}
If $f$ is $L$-smooth and $\mu$-strongly convex and $\alpha \in (0, 2/(L+\mu))$, then
\[
  f(x_k) - f^\star \;\leq\; \left(1 - \frac{2\alpha\mu L}{\mu + L}\right)^k (f(x_0) - f^\star).
\]
The optimal step $\alpha = 2/(\mu+L)$ achieves rate $\left(\frac{\kappa - 1}{\kappa + 1}\right)^2$ per iteration.
\end{theorem}

This theorem predicts slow convergence for large $\kappa$: on Rosenbrock ($\kappa \approx 2504$), GD makes progress proportional to $(1 - 2/2505)^k \approx e^{-k/1252}$, requiring thousands of iterations.

\begin{remark}[Proof of GD descent via the descent lemma]
The convergence of GD follows from the \emph{descent lemma}: for an $L$-smooth function,
\[
  f(x_{k+1}) \leq f(x_k) + \nabla f(x_k)^T(x_{k+1} - x_k) + \frac{L}{2}\|x_{k+1}-x_k\|^2.
\]
With $x_{k+1} = x_k - \alpha \nabla f(x_k)$ and $\alpha = 1/L$:
\[
  f(x_{k+1}) \leq f(x_k) - \frac{1}{2L}\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2.
\]
Adding strong convexity ($\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2 \geq 2\mu(f(x_k) - f^\star)$):
\[
  f(x_{k+1}) - f^\star \leq \left(1 - \frac{\mu}{L}\right)(f(x_k) - f^\star) = \left(1 - \frac{1}{\kappa}\right)(f(x_k) - f^\star).
\]
Applying this recursively $k$ times gives Theorem~\ref{thm:gd}. The key step is that the descent lemma bounds the quadratic overshoot of gradient descent, and strong convexity lower-bounds the gradient norm in terms of the objective gap. This proof structure is the template for all first-order method analyses: (1) bound the progress per step using function structure, (2) chain the bounds to get the overall rate.
\end{remark}

Adaptive step-size methods aim to overcome this by using gradient history to estimate the local curvature without explicitly computing the Hessian.

\begin{remark}[Step-size selection from the Lipschitz constant]
Theory requires $\alpha \leq 2/(\mu + L) \approx 1/L$ for guaranteed convergence. From our benchmark Lipschitz constants:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item Benchmark A ($L \approx 1$): $\alpha^{\max} \approx 1$. We use $\alpha = 0.08$, which is conservative but safe and allows comparison with other benchmarks.
  \item Benchmark B ($L = 10$): $\alpha^{\max} \approx 0.1$. We use $\alpha = 0.06$, within the stable range.
  \item Benchmark C ($L \approx 1001.6$ at the optimum): $\alpha^{\max} \approx 0.002$. We use $\alpha = 0.0012$, safely below the threshold. A larger step $\alpha = 0.003 > 2/L$ would cause divergence on GD.
\end{itemize}
The fact that Benchmark C requires steps $\approx 1000\times$ smaller than Benchmark A, despite all three functions having the same gradient descent update formula, is the key reason for the dramatically different number of effective steps in 120 iterations.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Armijo backtracking line search]
When $L$ is unknown, the \emph{Armijo backtracking} procedure eliminates the need to specify $\alpha$ in advance. Starting from a trial step $\alpha_0$ (e.g.\ $\alpha_0 = 1$), the algorithm halves $\alpha \leftarrow c\alpha$ (with $c \in (0,1)$, typically $c = 0.5$) until the \emph{sufficient decrease condition} (Armijo condition) is satisfied:
\[
  f(x_k - \alpha \nabla f(x_k)) \leq f(x_k) - \rho\alpha \|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2
\]
for a constant $\rho \in (0, 0.5)$ (typically $\rho = 10^{-4}$). This condition ensures that $f$ decreases by at least a fixed fraction of the prediction from the linear model. The number of halvings is at most $\lceil \log_c(1/(L\alpha_0)) \rceil$ before the stable range $\alpha \leq 1/L$ is reached, so the line search terminates in $O(\log(L\alpha_0))$ extra function evaluations per iteration. The Armijo condition combined with the Wolfe curvature condition $|\nabla f(x_{k+1})^T d_k| \leq \beta_W |\nabla f(x_k)^T d_k|$ (ensuring progress along the direction $d_k$) yields the \emph{strong Wolfe conditions} used in L-BFGS. In our experiments, fixed step sizes are used because $L$ is estimated from the Hessian; backtracking is most useful in practice when the global Lipschitz constant greatly overestimates the local curvature (a common scenario on Benchmark C, where $L$ varies from $\approx 1202$ at the start to $\approx 1001$ at the optimum).
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Diagonal preconditioning: bridge between GD and Newton]
For a quadratic $f(x) = \frac{1}{2}x^THx - b^Tx$ with $H = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_d)$ (diagonal Hessian), the optimal preconditioned gradient step is
\[
  x_{k+1} = x_k - P^{-1}\nabla f(x_k), \quad P = H = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_d),
\]
which gives $x_1 = x^\star$ in one step --- exactly Newton's method. The key insight: \emph{preconditioning transforms a poorly conditioned problem into a well-conditioned one} by changing the geometry of the gradient step. With preconditioner $P$, the effective condition number is $\kappa(P^{-1}H)$; using $P = H$ gives $\kappa = 1$.

\textbf{Connection to adaptive methods:} Adagrad implicitly uses the diagonal preconditioner $P_k = \mathrm{diag}(\sqrt{G_k})$ where $G_k = \sum_{j=0}^{k-1}g_j^2$. In a stationary regime, $G_k \approx k \cdot \mathrm{diag}(H)$ (since $\mathbb{E}[g_j^2] \approx \lambda_j^2 \tilde{x}_j^2$), giving $P_k \approx \sqrt{k}\,\mathrm{diag}(\lambda)^{1/2}$ --- a diagonal approximation of $H^{1/2}$. Adam's second-moment EMA $v_t$ similarly approximates $\mathrm{diag}(H)$ but with exponential forgetting. Full Newton uses $P = H$ (the exact Hessian); L-BFGS approximates $H^{-1}$ using secant equations. The spectrum of adaptive methods can thus be understood as varying degrees of Hessian approximation: GD ($P = \alpha^{-1}I$) uses zero Hessian information; diagonal methods (Adagrad, Adam) use diagonal Hessian information; L-BFGS uses a rank-$m$ correction to a scaled identity; Newton uses the full Hessian. Each step along this spectrum reduces the required number of iterations at the cost of more computation per iteration.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Wolfe conditions for line search]
The \emph{Armijo sufficient decrease condition} alone does not prevent excessively short steps. The \emph{strong Wolfe conditions} add a curvature condition:
\begin{align}
  f(x_k + \alpha d_k) &\leq f(x_k) + c_1 \alpha \nabla f(x_k)^T d_k \quad (\text{sufficient decrease, } c_1 \in (0,1)) \label{eq:wolfe1} \\
  |\nabla f(x_k + \alpha d_k)^T d_k| &\leq c_2 |\nabla f(x_k)^T d_k| \quad (\text{curvature condition, } c_2 \in (c_1, 1)) \label{eq:wolfe2}
\end{align}
The curvature condition \eqref{eq:wolfe2} ensures the gradient magnitude in the direction $d_k$ has decreased by at least factor $c_2$ --- preventing steps so short that very little progress is made. Together, the Wolfe conditions guarantee that the step is ``good enough'' for quasi-Newton methods: specifically, the secant condition $s_k^T y_k > 0$ (where $s_k = x_{k+1} - x_k$ and $y_k = \nabla f(x_{k+1}) - \nabla f(x_k)$) is guaranteed by the strong Wolfe conditions, which is required for L-BFGS to maintain a positive definite Hessian approximation.

\textbf{Connection to L-BFGS}: The L-BFGS secant equation $H_{k+1} s_k = y_k$ (the Hessian approximation $H_{k+1}$ must satisfy this) requires $s_k^T y_k > 0$ for a positive definite solution. From the mean-value theorem: $s_k^T y_k = s_k^T(\nabla f(x_{k+1}) - \nabla f(x_k)) = s_k^T \nabla^2 f(\xi) s_k$ for some $\xi$ between $x_k$ and $x_{k+1}$. This is positive iff $\nabla^2 f(\xi) \succ 0$, which is guaranteed near the optimum but may fail elsewhere --- precisely why the curvature condition \eqref{eq:wolfe2} is needed to enforce $s_k^T y_k > 0$ globally.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Polyak step convergence]\label{thm:polyak}
If $f$ is $L$-smooth and $\mu$-strongly convex with known $f^\star$, the Polyak step $\alpha_k = (f(x_k) - f^\star)/\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2$ satisfies
\[
  f(x_k) - f^\star \;\leq\; \left(1 - \frac{\mu}{L}\right)^k (f(x_0) - f^\star).
\]
This matches the optimal fixed-step rate $(1-\mu/L)^k$, which equals the best possible rate for any first-order method with known $f^\star$ and $L$, $\mu$. However, if $f^\star$ is \emph{misspecified} as $\tilde f^\star \neq f^\star_{\mathrm{true}}$, convergence is not guaranteed and $\alpha_k \to \infty$ as $\|\nabla f(x_k)\| \to 0$ while $f(x_k) - \tilde f^\star \not\to 0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}[Numerical stability and floating-point arithmetic in iterative methods]
All iterative optimisation algorithms run in finite-precision arithmetic (64-bit floats, $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}} \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-16}$). Several floating-point pitfalls affect practical implementations:

\textbf{Catastrophic cancellation}: When $f(x_k) \approx f^\star$ (near the minimum), computing $f(x_k) - f^\star$ may lose significant digits. For the Polyak step on Benchmark C: $f(x_{120}) \approx 0.009$ and $f^\star = 0$, so $f(x_{120}) - f^\star = 0.009$ --- well-represented in 64-bit. But if $f^\star = 0.4826$ (Benchmark A), the quantity $f(x_k) - f^\star$ decreases as $x_k \to x^\star$, with $f(x_k) - 0.4826 < 10^{-10}$ corresponding to relative errors of $\approx 10^{-6}$ in the subtraction --- causing the Polyak step numerator to round to zero prematurely.

\textbf{Gradient norm near zero}: As $\|\nabla f(x_k)\| \to 0$ at the minimum, gradient computations suffer from relative error: $\|\hat{g}_k - g_k\| / \|g_k\| \approx \varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}} / \|g_k\|$, which grows without bound as $\|g_k\| \to 0$. In practice, the gradient norm is rounded to zero (``machine zero'') when $\|g_k\| < C\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}$ for some constant $C$ depending on the function evaluation, causing algorithms to stall at approximate stationary points.

\textbf{Step size $\varepsilon$ in Adagrad/Adam}: The denominators $\sqrt{G_k} + \varepsilon$ and $\sqrt{\hat{v}_t} + \varepsilon$ prevent division by zero. In our experiments, $\varepsilon = 10^{-8}$ (Adagrad) and $\varepsilon = 10^{-8}$ (Adam). Too large an $\varepsilon$ ($\varepsilon \gg \sqrt{G_k}$) causes the method to reduce to gradient descent with rate $\alpha_0/\varepsilon$; too small ($\varepsilon < \varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}\sqrt{G_k}$) causes $\varepsilon$ to be absorbed into rounding error and provides no protection. The standard $\varepsilon = 10^{-8}$ is appropriate for 64-bit arithmetic where $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}} \approx 10^{-16}$ and typical gradient magnitudes are $O(1)$--$O(10^3)$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Practical Polyak variants]
In practice, $f^\star$ is often unknown. Several extensions address this: (1)~\emph{$\varepsilon$-estimate:} use a lower bound $\tilde{f}^\star \leq f^\star$ with a tolerance offset; (2)~\emph{interpolation condition:} when using SGD with Polyak step, batch stochasticity itself provides a natural correction; (3)~\emph{SPS (Stochastic Polyak Step):} in the interpolation regime (neural networks with sufficient capacity, where $f_i(x^\star) = 0$ for all training samples), each sample satisfies $f^\star_i = 0$ and the Polyak step can be applied per-sample. In our experiments, $f^\star = 0$ is correct for Benchmark C but wrong for A and B (where the observation noise creates a non-zero floor).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Algorithm Formulations}

Four adaptive methods are compared against a constant step-size gradient descent baseline across all three benchmarks for 120 iterations.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Polyak Step Size}
\label{alg:polyak}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, optimal value $f^{\star}$, tolerance $\varepsilon$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $\alpha_k \gets \dfrac{f(x_k) - f^{\star}}{\|g_k\|^2 + \varepsilon}$ \Comment{Tolerance $\varepsilon$ prevents division by zero}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha_k\, g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Adagrad}
\label{alg:adagrad}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, base learning rate $\alpha_0$, tolerance $\varepsilon$, iterations $N$
\State $G_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $G_{k+1} \gets G_k + g_k \odot g_k$ \Comment{Cumulative sum of squared gradients}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \dfrac{\alpha_0}{\sqrt{G_{k+1}} + \varepsilon} \odot g_k$ \Comment{Per-coordinate adaptive scaling}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Adagrad scales each coordinate by the inverse square root of all past squared gradients. Coordinates with historically large gradients receive smaller effective step sizes, implementing automatic coordinate-wise learning rate decay. Formally, the effective step at iteration $k$ for coordinate $i$ is:
\begin{equation}
  (\alpha_k)_i = \frac{\alpha_0}{\sqrt{(G_k)_i} + \varepsilon}, \quad (G_k)_i = \sum_{j=0}^{k-1} (g_j)_i^2.
\end{equation}
This achieves online regret $O(\sqrt{T})$ for convex Lipschitz functions, which is optimal among online algorithms. Crucially, the implicit per-coordinate curvature adaptation mirrors diagonal preconditioning: if coordinate $i$ has consistently large gradients (high effective curvature), its step is suppressed, while sparse or infrequently updated coordinates receive larger steps. The key limitation is that $G_k$ grows monotonically without bound, so $(\alpha_k)_i \to 0$ for all $i$ as $k \to \infty$. This can cause premature stagnation on strongly convex problems where sustained gradient norms prevent adequate final convergence.

\begin{remark}[Adagrad's online regret bound: proof sketch]
Define the \emph{regret} of an online algorithm producing decisions $x_1, \ldots, x_T$ against a comparator $x^\star \in \mathcal{X}$:
\[
  R_T = \sum_{t=1}^T f_t(x_t) - \sum_{t=1}^T f_t(x^\star),
\]
where $f_t$ is the loss function revealed at time $t$ (adversarially chosen). Adagrad's regret bound is:
\[
  R_T \leq \frac{\|x_1 - x^\star\|^2}{2\alpha_0}\sum_{i=1}^d \sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^T g_{t,i}^2} + \frac{\alpha_0}{2}\sum_{t=1}^T \sum_{i=1}^d \frac{g_{t,i}^2}{\sqrt{\sum_{s=1}^t g_{s,i}^2}}.
\]
The second term is bounded using the telescoping inequality $\sum_{t=1}^T x_t/\sqrt{\sum_{s=1}^t x_s} \leq 2\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^T x_t}$ (for $x_t \geq 0$), giving $R_T \leq O(\sum_{i=1}^d \sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^T g_{t,i}^2}) \leq O(\sqrt{T}\|g\|_{\mathrm{max}}\sqrt{d})$. The key is that the bound depends on the \emph{per-coordinate gradient history} $\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^T g_{t,i}^2}$ rather than the worst-case maximum, so if some coordinates have small gradients, the bound is tighter than the standard $O(\sqrt{Td})$ from non-adaptive methods. This explains Adagrad's practical advantage in sparse settings (e.g.\ NLP): coordinates with rare features have small $\sum_t g_{t,i}^2$, so they receive larger steps, enabling faster learning of rare patterns.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Adagrad's vanishing step rate]
If gradients are bounded $\|(g_k)_i\| \leq G$ for all $k$ and $i$, then $(G_k)_i = \sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(g_j)_i^2 \leq kG^2$, giving effective step $(\alpha_k)_i \geq \alpha_0/(\sqrt{kG^2}+\varepsilon) \approx \alpha_0/(G\sqrt{k})$ for large $k$. The total movement from $k_0$ to $\infty$ is therefore $\sum_{k=k_0}^\infty |x_{k+1}-x_k| \approx \sum_{k=k_0}^\infty \alpha_0/(G\sqrt{k}) = \infty$, so Adagrad \emph{can} still converge. However, the $O(1/\sqrt{k})$ step decay is exactly the $O(1/\sqrt{k})$ convergence rate of non-smooth SGD: if gradient norms are bounded away from zero (the iterate is far from the minimum), the step decays at the same rate as progress, causing stagnation. This is precisely the observed stagnation on Benchmark C (Rosenbrock): after a rapid initial descent, the gradients stabilise at $\|\nabla f(x_k)\| \approx C > 0$, and the effective step decays as $\alpha_0/(C\sqrt{k})$, preventing final convergence. Confirming: with $\alpha_0 = 0.45$ (Benchmark C setting), after 120 iterations the effective step $\approx 0.45/(C\sqrt{120}) \approx 0.04/C$, which for $C \approx 5$ gives $\approx 0.008$ --- much smaller than the initial $\alpha_0 = 0.45$, and too small to make meaningful progress in the Rosenbrock valley.
\end{remark}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{RMSprop}
\label{alg:rmsprop}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, learning rate $\alpha_0$, decay $\beta$, tolerance $\varepsilon$, iterations $N$
\State $v_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $v_{k+1} \gets \beta\, v_k + (1 - \beta)\, g_k \odot g_k$ \Comment{Exponential moving average of squared gradients}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \dfrac{\alpha_0}{\sqrt{v_{k+1}} + \varepsilon} \odot g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

RMSprop (Tieleman \& Hinton, 2012) addresses Adagrad's vanishing step problem by replacing the cumulative sum with an exponential moving average (EMA) with decay factor $\beta = 0.9$. The EMA gives weight $(1-\beta)\beta^j$ to the gradient from $j$ steps ago, exponentially forgetting stale information. This allows the effective step size to remain non-vanishing in stationary regimes.

\begin{remark}[RMSprop steady-state effective step]
In a stationary regime where $\|(g_k)_i\| \approx G_i$ is roughly constant, the EMA converges to a fixed point: $(v_k)_i \to \beta (v_{k-1})_i + (1-\beta)G_i^2$, giving at steady state $(v_\infty)_i = G_i^2$ (solving $v = \beta v + (1-\beta)G^2$ gives $v = G^2$). The effective step stabilises at $\alpha_0/(\sqrt{G_i^2} + \varepsilon) = \alpha_0/(G_i + \varepsilon)$ --- a fixed value proportional to $1/G_i$. This is the key difference from Adagrad: RMSprop's effective step \emph{stabilises} at $\alpha_0/G_i$ rather than \emph{decaying} to zero. In practice, this means RMSprop converges to a neighbourhood of the minimum (of radius proportional to $\alpha_0$) while Adagrad may stall prematurely. The tradeoff: RMSprop's non-vanishing step can prevent convergence to the exact minimum on strongly convex problems, explaining why RMSprop achieves $f = 0.5027$ (slightly above the noise floor $0.4826$) on Benchmark A while Adagrad achieves the exact floor.
\end{remark}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Polyak Momentum / Heavy Ball}
\label{alg:heavyball}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, momentum $\beta$, iterations $N$
\State $z_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets \beta\, z_k + \alpha\, \nabla f(x_k)$ \Comment{Accumulate momentum}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - z_{k+1}$ \Comment{Update with momentum}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The Heavy Ball method adds a momentum term $z_k$ that accumulates a velocity vector from past gradient directions. Physically, the iterate behaves like a ball rolling under gradient force with friction coefficient $(1 - \beta)$. For quadratic objectives, the optimal $\beta = (\sqrt{\kappa}-1)^2/(\sqrt{\kappa}+1)^2$ achieves convergence rate $(\sqrt{\kappa}-1)/(\sqrt{\kappa}+1)$, much faster than GD's $({\kappa}-1)/({\kappa}+1)$ rate. Unlike Nesterov momentum, Heavy Ball does not use a lookahead gradient and has no proven acceleration guarantee for general convex functions.

\begin{remark}[Heavy Ball as a damped oscillator: continuous-time perspective]
The Heavy Ball update can be understood from its continuous-time limit. The discrete recursion $z_{k+1} = \beta z_k + \alpha g_k$, $x_{k+1} = x_k - z_{k+1}$ (with $g_k = \nabla f(x_k)$) corresponds, in the limit $\alpha \to 0$ with $\beta = 1 - \gamma\sqrt{\alpha}$, to the ODE:
\[
  \ddot{x}(t) + \gamma\dot{x}(t) + \nabla f(x(t)) = 0.
\]
This is the equation of a \emph{damped harmonic oscillator} in the gradient field, with damping coefficient $\gamma$. For a quadratic $f(x) = \frac{1}{2}x^T H x$, the solution is $x(t) = \sum_i c_i e^{r_i t} v_i$ where $r_i = (-\gamma \pm \sqrt{\gamma^2 - 4\lambda_i})/2$ are the roots of the characteristic equation $r^2 + \gamma r + \lambda_i = 0$ for eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ of $H$.

\textbf{Critical damping:} The oscillation decays without oscillation when $\gamma^2 = 4\lambda_i$ (critical damping). For $H = L\cdot I$ ($\kappa = 1$), critical damping gives $\gamma = 2\sqrt{L}$ and rate $e^{-\sqrt{L}t}$ --- faster than gradient flow ($e^{-Lt}$) for $L < 1$. For general $\kappa > 1$: critical damping at $\lambda_{\min}$ leads to underdamping at $\lambda_{\max}$, causing oscillations in the fast eigenvector. The optimal Heavy Ball parameters balance this trade-off by choosing $\gamma = 2(\sqrt{L}+\sqrt{\mu})/2$ (harmonic mean), giving convergence rate $e^{-2t/(\sqrt{L}+\sqrt{\mu})}$ --- which in discrete time corresponds to the linear rate $(\sqrt{\kappa}-1)/(\sqrt{\kappa}+1)$ as derived above for the optimal Heavy Ball parameters.

Nesterov's method has the same continuous-time ODE form but with a time-varying damping coefficient $\gamma(t) = 3/t$ that starts large (heavy damping to prevent oscillations early on) and decreases toward zero (allowing faster convergence as the iterate approaches $x^\star$). This time-varying damping is the key to the $O(1/t^2)$ convergence rate.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Optimal Heavy Ball momentum and condition number]
For a quadratic $f(x) = \frac{1}{2}x^TH x - b^Tx$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_{\min} = \mu$ and $\lambda_{\max} = L$, the Heavy Ball method with step $\alpha = (2/(\sqrt{L}+\sqrt{\mu}))^2$ and momentum $\beta = \left(\frac{\sqrt{L}-\sqrt{\mu}}{\sqrt{L}+\sqrt{\mu}}\right)^2$ achieves the spectral rate:
\[
  \rho_{\mathrm{HB}}^\star = \left(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa}+1}\right)^2 = \left(1 - \frac{2}{\sqrt{\kappa}+1}\right)^2 \approx 1 - \frac{4}{\sqrt{\kappa}}.
\]
Compare this to GD's optimal rate $\rho_{\mathrm{GD}} = (\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1) \approx 1 - 2/\kappa$. The Heavy Ball rate decays as $O(1/\sqrt{\kappa})$ rather than $O(1/\kappa)$ per iteration --- the same asymptotic improvement as Nesterov, but only guaranteed for quadratics.

For our benchmarks: $\kappa_A \approx 1$ (no gain from momentum), $\kappa_B \approx 10$ gives $\rho^\star_B \approx 0.52$ vs GD $\approx 0.82$ (big speedup), $\kappa_C \approx 2504$ gives $\rho^\star_C \approx 0.92$ vs GD $\approx 0.9996$ (massive speedup for quadratics; observed improvement in Table~\ref{tab:q1_final} confirms this trend even on the non-quadratic Rosenbrock).
\end{remark}

\textbf{Key finding:} The Polyak step size has a dramatic dependence on the accuracy of $f^{\star}$. Mathematically, when $\|\nabla f(x_k)\| \to 0$ but $f(x_k) \not\to f^{\star}_{\mathrm{true}}$ (because $f^\star$ is misspecified), the step grows without bound: $\alpha_k = (f(x_k) - \tilde{f}^\star)/\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2 \to \infty$, causing divergence. When $f^\star$ is exact (Benchmark C), Polyak achieves faster convergence than any other method by automatically computing the theoretically optimal step $\alpha_k^\star = (f(x_k) - f^\star)/\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2$.

\begin{remark}[Proof sketch: Polyak step convergence]
For a $\mu$-strongly convex function with known $f^\star$, the Polyak step $\alpha_k = (f(x_k) - f^\star)/\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2$ satisfies:
\begin{align*}
  \|x_{k+1} - x^\star\|^2 &= \|x_k - \alpha_k \nabla f(x_k) - x^\star\|^2 \\
  &= \|x_k - x^\star\|^2 - 2\alpha_k \nabla f(x_k)^T(x_k - x^\star) + \alpha_k^2 \|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2.
\end{align*}
By strong convexity: $\nabla f(x_k)^T(x_k - x^\star) \geq f(x_k) - f^\star + \tfrac{\mu}{2}\|x_k - x^\star\|^2$. Substituting the Polyak step $\alpha_k = (f(x_k)-f^\star)/\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2$:
\begin{align*}
  \|x_{k+1} - x^\star\|^2 &\leq \|x_k - x^\star\|^2 - \frac{(f(x_k)-f^\star)^2}{\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2}(2 - 1) - \mu\alpha_k\|x_k-x^\star\|^2 \\
  &\leq (1 - \mu\alpha_k)\|x_k-x^\star\|^2.
\end{align*}
By $L$-smoothness: $f(x_k) - f^\star \leq \tfrac{L}{2}\|x_k - x^\star\|^2$, and by the co-coercivity inequality: $\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2 \leq L(f(x_k) - f^\star)$ (for strongly convex $f$), giving $\alpha_k \geq 1/L$. Combined: $\|x_{k+1}-x^\star\|^2 \leq (1-\mu/L)^k \|x_0-x^\star\|^2$. This recovers the optimal GD rate without needing to know $L$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Why Adagrad outperforms RMSprop on Benchmark A]
On Benchmark A ($\kappa \approx 1$, near-isotropic curvature), Adagrad converges to the exact noise floor $0.4826$ while RMSprop stagnates at $0.5027$. The mechanism: both coordinates of $X$ have similar variance ($\approx 1$), so the squared gradient in both directions grows at similar rates. Adagrad's cumulative $G_k = \sum_{j=0}^{k-1} g_j^2$ provides a reliable estimate of the long-run average squared gradient, and the decreasing step $\alpha_0/\sqrt{G_k}$ matches the natural decay needed as $x_k \to x^\star$. RMSprop's exponential forgetting $v_k = 0.9 v_{k-1} + 0.1 g_k^2$ maintains a noisy estimate of the \emph{current} squared gradient; near the minimum where $g_k$ becomes small and variable, $v_k$ fluctuates, causing an inconsistent effective step that induces oscillations and a slightly elevated final objective. For isotropic problems, Adagrad's long memory is an asset; for non-stationary problems or large $\kappa$ where gradients change scale rapidly, RMSprop's short memory is preferable.
\end{remark}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Convergence speed:} When $f^{\star}$ is known exactly, Polyak converges fastest (Rosenbrock: $f = 0.009$). Otherwise, Adagrad and Heavy Ball match or exceed the GD baseline. On the most difficult benchmark (Rosenbrock), the ranking is: Polyak $>$ Heavy Ball $>$ Adagrad $>$ RMSprop $\approx$ GD.

\item \textbf{Heavy Ball on quadratics:} For the strictly quadratic Benchmark A (where $\kappa \approx 1$), Heavy Ball with $\beta = 0.88$ shows no advantage over GD. This matches theory: the optimal Heavy Ball rate $(\sqrt{\kappa}-1)/(\sqrt{\kappa}+1)$ approaches $(\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1) \approx 0$ for $\kappa \approx 1$. On Rosenbrock (large $\kappa$), momentum accumulation along the curved valley helps, but Heavy Ball's non-lookahead gradient can cause instability on strongly non-quadratic objectives. This explains why Heavy Ball outperforms GD on Benchmark C but by less than Nesterov (Q2).

\item \textbf{Smoothness:} RMSprop produces the smoothest convergence curves due to exponential averaging. Heavy Ball shows characteristic overshoot followed by damped oscillation in the final convergence phase.

\item \textbf{Stability:} GD is most stable but slowest. Polyak is unstable when $f^{\star}$ is misspecified. Adagrad is uniformly stable (monotonically decreasing step size guarantees descent) but may stagnate prematurely. RMSprop can oscillate near the minimum due to a non-vanishing effective step.

\item \textbf{Practical recommendation:} Exact $f^{\star}$ is rarely known in practice, limiting Polyak's applicability. For data science applications where robustness is paramount, RMSprop or Adam (Q2) are preferred. When the problem structure suggests the optimal value is known (e.g.\ feasibility problems, or via duality), the Polyak step is theoretically optimal.

\item \textbf{Adagrad and online learning:} Adagrad's $O(\sqrt{T})$ regret bound in the online setting makes it optimal among algorithms with no knowledge of the gradient Lipschitz constant. The per-coordinate scaling is particularly beneficial in natural language processing, where some vocabulary features are very frequent (large gradients, should receive small steps) and some are rare (small gradients, should receive large steps). On our isotropic benchmarks (A, B), this advantage does not materialise, but it explains Adagrad's prevalence in NLP before RMSprop and Adam superseded it.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Theoretical Background}

Gradient descent achieves at best an $O(1/k)$ convergence rate on smooth convex functions. Nesterov (1983) showed this rate is optimal among first-order methods for non-strongly-convex problems, but \emph{also} showed a simple modification achieves the improved rate $O(1/k^2)$:

\begin{theorem}[Nesterov acceleration]\label{thm:nesterov}
For an $L$-smooth convex function $f$, Nesterov's accelerated gradient method with step $\alpha = 1/L$ satisfies
\[
  f(x_k) - f^\star \;\leq\; \frac{2L\|x_0 - x^\star\|^2}{(k+1)^2} \;=\; O(1/k^2).
\]
This $O(1/k^2)$ rate is optimal for first-order methods on smooth convex functions.
\end{theorem}

For a strongly convex function with condition number $\kappa$, momentum methods can achieve a linear rate with factor $r_{\mathrm{Nesterov}} = (\sqrt{\kappa}-1)/(\sqrt{\kappa}+1)$ per iteration, compared to $r_{\mathrm{GD}} = (\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1)$ for gradient descent. For large $\kappa$:
\begin{equation}
  r_{\mathrm{Nesterov}} = \frac{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa}+1} \approx 1 - \frac{2}{\sqrt{\kappa}}, \qquad r_{\mathrm{GD}} \approx 1 - \frac{2}{\kappa}.
\end{equation}
The number of iterations to reduce the error by factor $\epsilon^{-1}$ is $O(\sqrt{\kappa}\log(1/\epsilon))$ for Nesterov vs $O(\kappa\log(1/\epsilon))$ for GD. For $\kappa = 2504$, Nesterov requires $\sqrt{2504} \approx 50\times$ fewer iterations than GD for the same accuracy (see Fig.~\ref{fig:theory_rates} in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} for a visualisation). In practice on Benchmark C (150 iterations), Nesterov reaches $f = 0.479$ vs GD's $f = 3.388$, consistent with meaningful acceleration despite the non-convex Rosenbrock landscape.

\begin{remark}[Chebyshev polynomials and the optimality of Nesterov's rate]
The $O(\sqrt{\kappa})$ iteration complexity of Nesterov's method can be understood via \emph{Chebyshev polynomials}. For a quadratic $f(x) = \frac{1}{2}x^T H x - b^Tx$ with eigenvalues in $[\mu, L]$, any first-order method after $k$ iterations satisfies:
\[
  f(x_k) - f^\star \geq \left[\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa}+1}\right]^{2k} (f(x_0) - f^\star),
\]
where equality is achieved by the Chebyshev iteration --- an algorithm that computes
\[
  x_k = p_k(H)x_0, \quad \text{where } p_k \text{ is the degree-}k \text{ polynomial minimising } \max_{\lambda \in [\mu,L]} |p(\lambda)|^2.
\]
The minimax polynomial $p_k$ is precisely the $k$-th Chebyshev polynomial scaled to $[\mu, L]$: $p_k(\lambda) = T_k(c_\lambda)/T_k(c_0)$, where $T_k$ is the $k$-th Chebyshev polynomial of the first kind and $c_\lambda = (2\lambda - L - \mu)/(L-\mu)$. This polynomial decays at the Chebyshev rate $[(\sqrt{\kappa}-1)/(\sqrt{\kappa}+1)]^k$, proving the lower bound.

Nesterov's method achieves this Chebyshev rate (up to constant factors) without requiring knowledge of all eigenvalues individually --- only $\mu$ and $L$ are needed. This is why Nesterov's method is ``optimal'': it implicitly computes a Chebyshev-like polynomial approximation using only gradient evaluations, matching the information-theoretic lower bound.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Gradient descent as a low-pass filter on the eigenspectrum]
For a quadratic $f(x) = \frac{1}{2}x^THx - b^Tx$ with eigendecomposition $H = Q\Lambda Q^T$, GD with step $\alpha$ transforms the error $e_k = x_k - x^\star$ as:
\[
  \tilde{e}_k = Q^T e_k, \quad (\tilde{e}_k)_i = (1 - \alpha\lambda_i)^k (\tilde{e}_0)_i.
\]
This is equivalent to applying a \emph{polynomial filter} $p_k(\lambda) = (1-\alpha\lambda)^k$ to the initial error in the eigenspace. GD applies the same filter to all eigenvalues, and the filter suppresses large $\lambda$ (small high-frequency components) faster than small $\lambda$ (large low-frequency components). For $\kappa = L/\mu \gg 1$:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item The filter at $\lambda_{\max} = L$: $(1-\alpha L)^k \approx e^{-\alpha L k}$ --- fast decay.
  \item The filter at $\lambda_{\min} = \mu$: $(1-\alpha\mu)^k \approx e^{-\alpha \mu k}$ --- slow decay ($\alpha \mu \ll 1$).
\end{itemize}
GD is therefore a \emph{low-pass filter} that quickly converges in the ``high-frequency'' (large eigenvalue) directions but slowly converges in ``low-frequency'' (small eigenvalue) directions. Nesterov's method, by contrast, applies the Chebyshev polynomial filter which achieves a more \emph{equiripple} response across the eigenspectrum, decaying at the minimax-optimal rate $(\sqrt{\kappa}-1)/(\sqrt{\kappa}+1)$ uniformly in both extreme eigenvectors. This spectral perspective explains why Nesterov outperforms GD on ill-conditioned problems: it uses a filter that decays as fast as possible at all frequencies simultaneously, not just the dominant ones.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Connection to Conjugate Gradient method]
For quadratic objectives, the \emph{Conjugate Gradient} (CG) method achieves an even faster rate by constructing a sequence of mutually $H$-conjugate search directions. CG requires at most $d$ iterations to find the exact solution (for $d$-dimensional quadratic), and for ill-conditioned problems the convergence rate is $O(\sqrt{\kappa}\log(1/\epsilon))$, matching Nesterov on smooth strongly convex problems. The key connection: Nesterov momentum is essentially a gradient-only approximation of conjugate gradient, where the momentum term approximates the conjugate direction without requiring storage of the conjugate basis. Heavy Ball can be viewed as a polynomial approximation of CG with a fixed (non-growing) polynomial degree. For our $d=2$ problems, CG would solve the quadratic component exactly in 2 steps; the remaining progress depends on the non-quadratic terms (sin in Benchmark B, the Rosenbrock nonlinearity in C).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Algorithm Formulations}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Nesterov Momentum / Acceleration}
\label{alg:nesterov}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, max momentum $\beta_{\max}$, iterations $N$
\State $z_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 1, 2, \ldots, N$}
    \State $\beta_k \gets \min\!\left(\frac{k-1}{k+2},\ \beta_{\max}\right)$ \Comment{Adaptive momentum increasing towards $\beta_{\max}$}
    \State $\tilde{x}_k \gets x_k + \beta_k\, z_k$ \Comment{Lookahead point}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets \beta_k\, z_k - \alpha\, \nabla f(\tilde{x}_k)$ \Comment{Gradient at lookahead}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k + z_{k+1}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The critical distinction from Heavy Ball is the \emph{lookahead}: the gradient is evaluated at $\tilde{x}_k = x_k + \beta_k z_k$ rather than $x_k$. This ``corrects'' the trajectory before overshooting. The adaptive schedule $\beta_k = (k-1)/(k+2) \to 1$ increases momentum gradually from 0, matching the theoretical requirement for the $O(1/k^2)$ rate. The cap $\beta_{\max}$ prevents instability when the schedule exceeds the stability bound.

\begin{remark}[Why $\beta_k = (k-1)/(k+2)$ and how $\beta_{\max}$ is chosen]
The specific schedule $\beta_k = (k-1)/(k+2)$ comes from the requirement $\beta_k = (t_{k-1}-1)/t_k$ where $t_k$ satisfies the recursion $t_{k+1}^2 = t_{k+1} + t_k^2$ with $t_0 = 1$. This recursion, discovered by Nesterov, is precisely what is needed to maintain the estimate sequence invariant that proves the $O(1/k^2)$ rate. The asymptotic $(k-1)/(k+2) \to 1$ means momentum approaches 1 --- theoretically the bound $\beta_k < 1$ is required for convergence.

The cap $\beta_{\max} \in (0,1)$ in our implementation limits the maximum momentum. For Benchmark A: $\beta_{\max} = 0.90$; for B: $0.92$; for C: $0.90$. These are tuned to avoid oscillations: larger momentum leads to larger overshoot near the optimum. For strongly convex functions with known $\kappa$, the optimal fixed momentum for the linear-rate version of Nesterov is $\beta^\star = (\sqrt{\kappa}-1)/(\sqrt{\kappa}+1)$: for $\kappa = 2504$, $\beta^\star \approx 0.969$. Our experimental $\beta_{\max} = 0.90 < \beta^\star$ is conservative but ensures stability on the non-convex Rosenbrock function.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Nesterov's first step: worked numerical example on Benchmark A]
On Benchmark A with $H \approx I_2$, $b = H\hat\theta \approx \hat\theta = (3.028, 4.017)^T$ (the OLS solution), and $f^\star \approx 0.4826$. Starting from $x_0 = (0,0)^T$, $z_0 = (0,0)^T$:

\textbf{Iteration $k=1$:} $\beta_1 = \min((0)/(3), 0.90) = 0$. Lookahead $\tilde{x}_1 = x_0 + 0 \cdot z_0 = (0,0)$. Gradient $\nabla f(\tilde{x}_1) = H(0,0) - b \approx -(3.028, 4.017)$. Velocity $z_1 = 0 \cdot z_0 - 0.06 \cdot (-(3.028, 4.017)) = (0.182, 0.241)$. New iterate $x_1 = (0,0) + (0.182, 0.241) = (0.182, 0.241)$.

\textbf{Iteration $k=2$:} $\beta_2 = \min(1/4, 0.90) = 0.25$. Lookahead $\tilde{x}_2 = (0.182, 0.241) + 0.25(0.182, 0.241) = (0.228, 0.301)$. Gradient $\approx H(0.228,0.301) - b \approx (0.228-3.028, 0.301-4.017) = (-2.800, -3.716)$. Velocity $z_2 = 0.25(0.182, 0.241) - 0.06(-2.800, -3.716) = (0.046 + 0.168, 0.060 + 0.223) = (0.214, 0.283)$. New iterate $x_2 = (0.182+0.214, 0.241+0.283) = (0.396, 0.524)$.

Continuing this process, the iterate builds increasing momentum $\beta_k = (k-1)/(k+2) \to 1$, accumulating velocity in the direction of the gradient. After $k$ steps, the iterate approximately satisfies $\|x_k - x^\star\| \leq 2L\|x_0-x^\star\|/(k+1)^2 \to 0$ faster than GD's linear rate. For Benchmark A with $\kappa \approx 1$, the gain is small (GD already converges in $O(1)$ steps); on Benchmark C the $\sqrt{\kappa} \approx 50\times$ speedup is decisive.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Intuition for the $O(1/k^2)$ rate via estimate sequences]
Nesterov's proof of the $O(1/k^2)$ rate uses the concept of \emph{estimate sequences}: at iteration $k$, maintain a sequence of lower bounds $\phi_k(x) \leq f(x)$ that is tighter than the global lower bound while being easy to minimise. The sequence is designed so that $\min_x \phi_k(x) \to f^\star$ at rate $1/k^2$. The key invariant maintained by Nesterov's method is:
\[
  \phi_k(x) = (1 - \lambda_k) f^\star + \lambda_k \phi_0(x) + \lambda_k \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} \frac{\alpha_{i+1}}{\lambda_{i+1}} [f(y_i) + \nabla f(y_i)^T(x - y_i)],
\]
where $\lambda_k = 1/(1 + \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} \alpha_{i+1}/\lambda_{i+1})$ decreases at rate $O(1/k^2)$. The lookahead gradient $y_k = x_k + \beta_k z_k$ is precisely the point that enables this invariant to be maintained while updating $x_{k+1} = x_k + z_{k+1}$. The key algorithmic insight is that the lookahead point $y_k$ is where progress is ``promised'' by the estimate sequence, so the gradient there provides the tightest lower bound update --- explaining why using $\nabla f(y_k)$ rather than $\nabla f(x_k)$ unlocks the $O(1/k^2)$ rate.
\end{remark}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Adam Optimiser}
\label{alg:adam}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, learning rate $\alpha$, moments $\beta_1, \beta_2$, tolerance $\varepsilon$, iterations $N$
\State $m_0 \gets \mathbf{0},\ v_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$t = 1, 2, \ldots, N$}
    \State $g_t \gets \nabla f(x_t)$
    \State $m_t \gets \beta_1\, m_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_1)\, g_t$ \Comment{First moment (gradient EMA)}
    \State $v_t \gets \beta_2\, v_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_2)\, g_t \odot g_t$ \Comment{Second moment (squared gradient EMA)}
    \State $\hat{m}_t \gets m_t\, /\, (1 - \beta_1^t)$ \Comment{Bias-corrected first moment}
    \State $\hat{v}_t \gets v_t\, /\, (1 - \beta_2^t)$ \Comment{Bias-corrected second moment}
    \State $x_{t+1} \gets x_t - \alpha\, \hat{m}_t\, /\, (\sqrt{\hat{v}_t} + \varepsilon)$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Adam (Kingma \& Ba, 2015) unifies two ideas: (1)~RMSprop's per-coordinate adaptive scaling via EMA of $g^2$, and (2)~Polyak-style gradient momentum via EMA of $g$. The unified update performs an approximately normalised gradient step:
\begin{equation}
  x_{t+1} \approx x_t - \alpha \cdot \frac{\mathbb{E}[g_t]}{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[g_t^2]} + \varepsilon},
\end{equation}
with the ratio bounded in magnitude by 1, so the effective step per coordinate is always at most $\alpha$.

The bias-correction terms $(1-\beta_1^t)^{-1}$ and $(1-\beta_2^t)^{-1}$ are critical at early iterations. At $t=1$ with $\beta_2 = 0.999$, the uncorrected $v_1 = (1-0.999)g_1^2 = 0.001g_1^2$ underestimates the true squared gradient by $1000\times$, giving an effective step $\sqrt{1000}\approx 32\times$ too large without correction. As $t \to \infty$, both corrections approach 1 and Adam reduces to RMSprop with gradient momentum. The convention $\beta_1 < \beta_2$ (here 0.80 vs 0.999) ensures the gradient EMA adapts faster than the variance EMA, giving responsive gradient direction with stable curvature estimates.

\begin{remark}[Adam's convergence and AMSGrad]
Reddi et al.\ (2018) showed that Adam can \emph{fail to converge} on simple convex online problems, because the exponential moving average of $g_t^2$ can decrease (if gradients alternate between large and small), causing the effective step $\alpha/\sqrt{\hat{v}_t}$ to \emph{increase} over time in some coordinate --- violating the descent property. This non-monotone step behaviour can prevent convergence to the optimum on non-smooth convex problems. The fix, \emph{AMSGrad}, replaces $\hat{v}_t$ with a monotone maximum: $\hat{v}_t^{\max} = \max(\hat{v}_{t-1}^{\max}, \hat{v}_t)$, ensuring the effective step never increases. In practice on smooth strongly convex problems (as in our experiments), Adam does converge; the non-convergence is a pathological case involving oscillating stochastic gradients. Nevertheless, in training large language models, careful Adam hyperparameter tuning (especially $\epsilon$ to prevent division by near-zero values) remains critical.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Mini-Batch Stochastic Gradient Descent}

Mini-batch SGD on Benchmark A uses learning rate $\alpha = 0.06$, batch sizes $b \in \{5, 40\}$, 50 epochs, with data shuffled uniformly at each epoch.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Mini-Batch SGD}
\label{alg:sgd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Data $(X, y)$, initial $\theta_0$, learning rate $\alpha$, batch size $b$, epochs $E$
\For{$e = 1, 2, \ldots, E$}
    \State Randomly permute data indices $\sigma$
    \For{$i = 0, b, 2b, \ldots$} \Comment{Mini-batch loop over permuted data}
        \State $\mathcal{B} \gets \{\sigma(i), \ldots, \sigma(i+b-1)\}$
        \State $g \gets \frac{1}{b} X_{\mathcal{B}}^T(X_{\mathcal{B}}\theta - y_{\mathcal{B}})$ \Comment{Stochastic gradient estimate}
        \State $\theta \gets \theta - \alpha\, g$
    \EndFor
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The mini-batch gradient is an unbiased estimate of the full gradient: $\mathbb{E}[g_{\mathcal{B}}] = \nabla J(\theta)$. Its variance is $\mathbb{V}[g_{\mathcal{B}}] = \sigma^2_g / b$, where $\sigma^2_g$ is the per-sample gradient variance. A smaller batch $b$ gives more updates per epoch (each update costs less) but higher gradient variance; a larger $b$ provides lower-variance estimates at the cost of fewer updates per epoch.

\begin{proposition}[SGD convergence to neighbourhood]\label{prop:sgd}
For an $L$-smooth strongly convex objective with batch size $b$ and step size $\alpha \leq 1/L$, SGD satisfies
\[
  \mathbb{E}[f(\theta_k) - f^\star] \;\leq\; (1 - \alpha\mu)^k (f(\theta_0) - f^\star) + \frac{\alpha L \sigma^2_g}{2\mu b},
\]
where $\sigma^2_g$ is the per-sample gradient variance. The iterates converge linearly to a neighbourhood of size $O(\alpha \sigma^2_g / b)$ around $f^\star$. Larger batch $b$ shrinks this neighbourhood; smaller step $\alpha$ also shrinks it but slows convergence.
\end{proposition}

This explains the noise-floor phenomenon observed in Fig.~\ref{fig:q2_sgd_noisy}: increasing $\sigma$ raises $\sigma^2_g \propto \sigma^2$, directly expanding the irreducible noise floor that SGD converges to.

\begin{remark}[Computing the SGD noise floor for Benchmark A]
For linear regression $J(\theta) = \frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i=1}^m (\theta^Tx_i - y_i)^2$ with $y_i = \theta^{*T}x_i + \varepsilon_i$, $\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$, the per-sample gradient at the OLS minimiser $\hat\theta$ is:
\[
  \nabla J_i(\hat\theta) = (\hat\theta^T x_i - y_i) x_i = -\varepsilon_i x_i.
\]
The per-sample gradient variance is $\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla J_i(\hat\theta)\|^2] = \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_i^2 \|x_i\|^2] = \sigma^2 \mathbb{E}[\|x_i\|^2] = \sigma^2 d = 2\sigma^2$ (for $d=2$, $x_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)$). Substituting into Proposition~\ref{prop:sgd}: the noise floor $= \alpha L \sigma^2_g / (2\mu b) = \alpha \cdot 1 \cdot 2\sigma^2 / (2 \cdot 1 \cdot b) = \alpha\sigma^2/b$. For $\alpha = 0.06$, $b = 5$, $\sigma = 1$: noise floor $\approx 0.06/5 = 0.012$. Since $J^\star \approx 0.4826$ (from observation noise), the SGD adds $\approx 0.012$ above this floor --- small enough to be undetectable in Fig.~\ref{fig:q2_sgd_batch}. For $\sigma = 6$: $J^\star_{\mathrm{noisy}} \approx 36 \times 0.4826 \approx 17.4$, and the SGD noise floor adds $0.06 \times 36/5 = 0.43$ above $J^\star_{\mathrm{noisy}}$ --- again small relative to the elevation of $J^\star_{\mathrm{noisy}}$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Epoch complexity and learning rate decay]
Define an \emph{epoch} as one pass through all $m$ data points; each epoch contains $m/b$ gradient updates. The total gradient evaluations to reach accuracy $\epsilon$ is:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Full GD}: each epoch = 1 update; need $O(\kappa \log(1/\epsilon))$ updates = $O(\kappa \log(1/\epsilon))$ epochs.
  \item \textbf{Mini-batch SGD with fixed $\alpha$}: converges to a noise floor $C(\alpha, b) = \alpha L \sigma^2_g / (2\mu b)$. Reaching accuracy $\epsilon > C$ requires $O(\kappa \log(1/(\epsilon - C)))$ updates. If $\epsilon < C$, fixed-step SGD cannot achieve this accuracy.
  \item \textbf{Mini-batch SGD with decaying $\alpha_k = \alpha_0/k$}: achieves $O(1/k)$ convergence at the cost of slower later iterations. The optimal rate requires $\alpha_k = 1/(\mu k)$.
\end{itemize}
The fundamental insight is that SGD with fixed step size trades convergence precision for reduced per-iteration cost. For Benchmark A with $m = 1000$ data points: full GD costs $1000$ function evaluations per step; mini-batch SGD with $b = 5$ costs $5$ evaluations per step, running $m/b = 200$ steps per epoch. When the model capacity matches the data noise (as here), both converge to the same floor $J^\star \approx 0.4826$, and the mini-batch approach processes $200\times$ more gradient steps per data pass.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Mini-batch vs full-batch trade-off: large-batch training pathology]
Modern deep learning uses very large mini-batches (sometimes $b \sim 8192$ or larger) to exploit GPU parallelism. However, the relationship between batch size and convergence speed is non-linear. Increasing $b$ by factor $c$ achieves the same noise reduction as decreasing the step size $\alpha$ by $c$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:sgd}: noise floor $\propto \alpha/b$). But with a larger step size $\alpha \propto b$ (``linear scaling rule''), the convergence speed per epoch remains constant --- so larger batches should use proportionally larger step sizes. The \emph{large-batch training pathology} (Keskar et al., 2017): very large batches converge to ``sharp'' minima (with large Hessian eigenvalues at the optimum) that generalise worse than ``flat'' minima found by small batches. The mechanism: small-batch SGD's noise acts as implicit regularisation, preventing convergence to sharp minima and favouring flat regions of the loss landscape.

For our Benchmark A experiments with $m = 1000$ and $b \in \{5, 40\}$: the batch effect is purely a noise-floor phenomenon (no generalisation concern), and both batches converge to the same Bayes-optimal floor $J^\star \approx 0.4826$. The large-batch pathology is specific to non-convex objectives with multiple minima of different sharpness.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Variance reduction: SVRG and SAGA]
Proposition~\ref{prop:sgd} reveals a fundamental limitation of fixed-step SGD: it converges only to a neighbourhood of radius $O(\alpha\sigma^2_g/b)$ around $f^\star$, not to $f^\star$ itself. \emph{Variance reduction methods} eliminate this noise floor while maintaining cheap per-iteration cost:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{SVRG} (Johnson \& Zhang, 2013): Periodically computes the full gradient $\tilde{g} = \nabla f(x_s)$ at a snapshot $x_s$. The update is $\hat{g}_k = \nabla f_i(x_k) - \nabla f_i(x_s) + \tilde{g}$, which is unbiased with variance $O(\|x_k - x_s\|^2) \to 0$ as $x_k \to x^\star$. SVRG achieves \emph{linear convergence} to $f^\star$ (not just a neighbourhood) at an epoch cost of $O((m + \kappa)\log(1/\epsilon))$ gradient evaluations.
  \item \textbf{SAGA} (Defazio et al., 2014): Maintains a table of the $m$ most recent per-sample gradients. The variance of the SAGA estimator goes to zero as the iterate converges. SAGA has the same asymptotic rate as SVRG but is better suited for streaming settings (no snapshot epoch required).
\end{itemize}
For Benchmark A with $m=1000$, $\kappa \approx 1$: SVRG needs $O((1000+1)\log(1/\epsilon)) \approx 1000\log(1/\epsilon)$ per-sample gradient evaluations, comparable to full-batch GD ($1000$ evaluations per step $\times$ $\log(1/\epsilon)$ steps). Variance reduction becomes most beneficial for large $m$ with moderate $\kappa$: SVRG's $O(m + \kappa)$ vs SGD's $O(m\kappa)$ epoch cost gives a $\kappa$-factor speedup.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[The interpolation regime: when SGD noise vanishes]
The SGD noise floor from Proposition~\ref{prop:sgd} is $O(\alpha\sigma^2_g/b)$, where $\sigma^2_g = \mathbb{E}_i[\|\nabla f_i(x^\star)\|^2]$ is the gradient variance at the minimiser $x^\star$. In the \emph{interpolation regime} --- when the model is expressive enough to perfectly fit every training sample, so $f_i(x^\star) = 0$ for all $i$ --- the per-sample gradient at $x^\star$ vanishes: $\nabla f_i(x^\star) = 0$, and therefore $\sigma^2_g = 0$. In this regime, fixed-step SGD achieves \emph{linear convergence} to the exact minimiser (matching full GD) even with batch size $b = 1$, because the noise floor is exactly zero.

This situation arises in modern overparameterised neural networks (many more parameters than training samples), where the training loss can be driven to essentially zero on every sample simultaneously. For such models, the Polyak step applied per-mini-batch ($\alpha_k = f_{\mathcal{B}}(x_k)/\|\nabla f_{\mathcal{B}}(x_k)\|^2$ where $f_{\mathcal{B}}$ is the mini-batch loss, which satisfies $f^\star_{\mathcal{B}} = 0$) achieves the same convergence as the full-batch Polyak step at a fraction of the cost. Our Benchmark A experiments are \emph{not} in the interpolation regime (since observation noise $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ prevents $f_i(x^\star) = 0$), explaining why the SGD noise floor is nonzero. If we removed the noise ($y = X\theta^\star$ exactly), Benchmark A would enter the interpolation regime and mini-batch SGD with any batch size would converge linearly to $J^\star = 0$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Gradient clipping and the heavy tail problem]
In training deep neural networks, gradients can occasionally be extremely large due to long-range temporal dependencies (in RNNs) or steep loss landscapes. \emph{Gradient clipping} caps the gradient norm before each update:
\[
  \tilde{g}_k = \begin{cases} g_k & \text{if } \|g_k\| \leq \tau, \\ \tau \cdot g_k/\|g_k\| & \text{if } \|g_k\| > \tau. \end{cases}
\]
This is equivalent to using a step size $\min(\alpha, \alpha\tau/\|g_k\|)$, i.e.\ an adaptive step that is smaller when the gradient is large. Gradient clipping introduces bias (the clipped gradient is no longer $\mathbb{E}[\tilde{g}_k] = \nabla f(x_k)$ in general) but prevents instability from gradient explosions. None of our benchmark experiments require gradient clipping: Rosenbrock's gradients are bounded at $\|\nabla f(-1,1)\| = \sqrt{4 + 200^2} \approx 200$, and with $\alpha = 0.0012$ the step norm is $\approx 0.24$ --- well within stable range. However, the RMSprop and Adam normalisation ($g/\sqrt{v+\varepsilon}$) acts as a soft form of gradient clipping: the effective step per coordinate is bounded by $\alpha/\varepsilon$, preventing catastrophic updates even when individual gradient components are large.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Theoretical Background}

Newton's method minimises the local second-order Taylor model of $f$ at each iteration:
\begin{equation}
  m_k(d) = f(x_k) + \nabla f(x_k)^T d + \tfrac{1}{2} d^T \nabla^2 f(x_k)\, d.
\end{equation}
The minimiser of $m_k$ over $d$ is the Newton direction $p_k = -[\nabla^2 f(x_k)]^{-1} \nabla f(x_k)$, and the update is $x_{k+1} = x_k + \alpha p_k$.

\begin{theorem}[Newton's method, quadratic convergence]\label{thm:newton}
If $f$ is twice continuously differentiable and the Hessian $\nabla^2 f$ is $M$-Lipschitz near $x^\star$, then with full step $\alpha = 1$, Newton's method satisfies
\[
  \|x_{k+1} - x^\star\| \;\leq\; \frac{M}{2\mu} \|x_k - x^\star\|^2,
\]
for $x_k$ sufficiently close to $x^\star$ (specifically within the \emph{region of quadratic convergence} $\|x_k - x^\star\| < 2\mu/M$). This \emph{quadratic convergence} means the number of correct digits doubles at each iteration, compared to linear convergence for gradient descent.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}[Global vs local convergence]
Theorem~\ref{thm:newton} is a \emph{local} result. When $x_0$ is far from $x^\star$ (e.g.\ on Rosenbrock starting from $(-1, 1)$, far from $(1,1)$), the Hessian may be indefinite or poorly conditioned, and full Newton steps ($\alpha = 1$) can diverge. This motivates \emph{damped} Newton with $\alpha < 1$: the damping sacrifices the quadratic convergence rate near the optimum but guarantees global descent. For the Rosenbrock function, the Hessian $\nabla^2 f$ at $(-1, 1)$ has $h_{11} = 2 + 1200(-1)^2 - 400(1) = 802$, $h_{12} = 400$, giving an eigenvalue $\approx 1202$, so the Newton step is well-defined but large; hence $\alpha = 0.22$ is required for stability.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Rosenbrock: non-convex globally but no local minima]
While the Rosenbrock function is non-convex globally (its Hessian at $(-1, 1)$ has eigenvalues $\approx 1202$ and $\approx 0.4$, both positive, so it is locally convex there; but at $(-1, -1)$, $h_{11} = 2 + 1200 + 400 = 1602$, $h_{12} = 400$, $h_{22} = 200$, and the determinant $1602 \times 200 - 400^2 = 320400 - 160000 = 160400 > 0$, so it is positive definite there too), it has been proven analytically that the only critical point is the global minimum $(1,1)$. Therefore, all gradient-based and Newton methods starting from any initial point converge to the global minimum, not a local one. This property makes Rosenbrock a clean test of convergence \emph{speed} rather than a test of avoiding local optima.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[One-step convergence for quadratics]
For a quadratic objective $f(\theta) = \frac{1}{2}\theta^T H \theta - b^T\theta$, the Newton step with $\alpha = 1$ satisfies
\[
  \theta_1 = \theta_0 - H^{-1}(H\theta_0 - b) = H^{-1}b = \theta^\star
\]
regardless of $\theta_0$. Newton's method finds the exact minimiser in a \emph{single iteration} for any quadratic function. This is why Benchmark A (linear regression) is solved exactly in one step.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Affine invariance]
Newton's method is \emph{affine invariant}: the sequence of iterates is unchanged under affine reparametrisations of the problem. This means Newton is unaffected by the condition number $\kappa$ in the neighbourhood of the optimum, unlike gradient descent whose convergence rate degrades as $\kappa \to \infty$.

Formally: if we change coordinates $u = Ax$ for invertible $A$, the problem $g(u) = f(A^{-1}u)$ has the same minimiser structure. Gradient descent on $g$ with the same step size gives different iterates (it sees the new condition number $\kappa(A^{-T}HA^{-1})$). But Newton's direction $[\nabla^2 g(u_k)]^{-1}\nabla g(u_k)$ satisfies $[\nabla^2 g]^{-1}\nabla g = A[\nabla^2 f]^{-1}\nabla f$, so the Newton step in $x$-space is identical. This is why Newton converges at the same rate on any quadratic, regardless of its condition number --- the Hessian inverse exactly corrects for the scaling.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Local Approximations of $g(x) = x^4$}

At $x_0 = 0.25$, $g(x_0) = 0.0039$, $g'(x_0) = 4 \times 0.25^3 = 0.0625$, $g''(x_0) = 12 \times 0.25^2 = 0.75$.

\textbf{First-order (linear) approximation:}
\begin{equation}
g(x) \approx g(x_0) + g'(x_0)(x - x_0) = 0.0039 + 0.0625(x - 0.25).
\end{equation}
This captures only the local slope; it diverges rapidly from $g(x)$ away from $x_0$ and has no minimum (being linear).

\textbf{Second-order (quadratic) approximation:}
\begin{equation}
g(x) \approx g(x_0) + g'(x_0)(x - x_0) + \tfrac{1}{2}g''(x_0)(x - x_0)^2 = 0.0039 + 0.0625(x - 0.25) + 0.375(x - 0.25)^2.
\end{equation}
The Newton update minimises this parabola, yielding:
\begin{equation}
x_{\mathrm{Newton}} = x_0 - \frac{g'(x_0)}{g''(x_0)} = 0.25 - \frac{0.0625}{0.75} = 0.25 - 0.0833 = 0.1667.
\end{equation}
This is a large step toward the true minimum at $x^\star = 0$, much larger than a gradient step $x_0 - \alpha g'(x_0)$ for typical $\alpha$.

To see the advantage concretely: with GD and $\alpha = 0.1$, we would move $x_1^{\mathrm{GD}} = 0.25 - 0.1 \times 0.0625 = 0.2438$, barely moving from $x_0 = 0.25$. Newton moves to $x_1^{\mathrm{Newton}} = 0.1667$, a $6\times$ larger step. The Newton step automatically adapts to the curvature: when $g''(x_0)$ is large (high curvature), the step is small (avoids overshoot); when $g''(x_0)$ is small (flat region), the step is large (exploits the flatness to advance rapidly). This is the mechanism of curvature-adapted step sizes --- the core benefit of second-order information.

\begin{remark}[Levenberg--Marquardt: regularised Gauss-Newton for robustness]
The \emph{Levenberg--Marquardt} (LM) algorithm regularises the Gauss-Newton step to handle near-singular Jacobians and improve global convergence:
\[
  p_k^{\mathrm{LM}} = -(J_k^T J_k + \lambda_k I)^{-1} J_k^T r(x_k),
\]
where $\lambda_k \geq 0$ is the damping parameter. For $\lambda_k = 0$: LM reduces to Gauss-Newton (fast convergence near $x^\star$). For $\lambda_k \to \infty$: $p_k \approx -(\lambda_k I)^{-1}J_k^T r = -\nabla f(x_k)/\lambda_k$ reduces to gradient descent (safe convergence globally). The algorithm adapts $\lambda_k$ based on the ratio of actual vs predicted decrease (trust-region style), automatically interpolating between GD and Gauss-Newton.

\textbf{Benchmark A connection}: For linear regression $r(\theta) = X\theta - y$ with constant Jacobian $J = X/\sqrt{m}$ (no dependence on $\theta$), the Gauss-Newton step is $(J^TJ)^{-1}J^Tr = (X^TX/m)^{-1}(X^T(X\theta-y)/m) = H^{-1}(H\theta - b) = -p_{\mathrm{Newton}}$, i.e.\ Gauss-Newton = Newton = one-step exact convergence. This is why Benchmark A has a trivial Newton performance: the Jacobian is constant, making the Gauss-Newton approximation exact (residuals are linear in parameters), and LM with $\lambda_k = 0$ achieves the same performance.

\textbf{Benchmark B with $f = (x_1-1)^2 + 5(x_2-2)^2 + \sin(x_1)$}: The function is not in least-squares form, so Gauss-Newton does not directly apply. The modified Newton step uses the full Hessian $H(x) = \mathrm{diag}(2-\sin(x_1), 10)$, which is positive definite as long as $\sin(x_1) < 2$ (always true), so damped Newton is effective.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Proof of Newton's quadratic convergence rate]
For $f$ twice differentiable with $M$-Lipschitz Hessian and $\mu$-strongly convex near $x^\star$, the Newton error satisfies:
\begin{align*}
  e_{k+1} &= x_{k+1} - x^\star = x_k - [\nabla^2 f(x_k)]^{-1}\nabla f(x_k) - x^\star \\
  &= e_k - [\nabla^2 f(x_k)]^{-1}\nabla f(x_k) \\
  &= [\nabla^2 f(x_k)]^{-1}\bigl[\nabla^2 f(x_k) e_k - \nabla f(x_k)\bigr].
\end{align*}
By Taylor expansion: $\nabla f(x_k) = \nabla f(x^\star) + \nabla^2 f(x^\star)e_k + O(\|e_k\|^2) = \nabla^2 f(x^\star) e_k + O(\|e_k\|^2)$ (using $\nabla f(x^\star) = 0$). Substituting: $\nabla^2 f(x_k) e_k - \nabla f(x_k) = [\nabla^2 f(x_k) - \nabla^2 f(x^\star)]e_k + O(\|e_k\|^2)$. By Lipschitz Hessian: $\|\nabla^2 f(x_k) - \nabla^2 f(x^\star)\| \leq M\|e_k\|$. Thus:
\[
  \|e_{k+1}\| \leq \|[\nabla^2 f(x_k)]^{-1}\| \cdot M\|e_k\|^2 \leq \frac{M}{2\mu}\|e_k\|^2,
\]
using $\|[\nabla^2 f(x_k)]^{-1}\| \leq 1/(\mu - M\|e_k\|) \leq 2/\mu$ for $\|e_k\| \leq \mu/(2M)$. This proves Theorem~\ref{thm:newton}. The constant $M/(2\mu)$ relates the Lipschitz constant of the Hessian to the strong convexity modulus: for Benchmark A where $H = $ constant (zero Lipschitz constant: $M = 0$), $e_1 = 0$ exactly (one-step convergence); for Benchmark C with highly variable Hessian ($M \approx 2400$ near the origin), the quadratic convergence region $\|e_k\| < 2\mu/M \approx 0.8/2400 \approx 3.3\times10^{-4}$ is tiny, explaining why the global phase (damped Newton from $(-1,1)$) is slow.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Iterating Newton on $g(x) = x^4$]
Applying Newton's method repeatedly to $g(x) = x^4$ from $x_0 = 0.25$ (with $\alpha = 1$):
\[
  x_{k+1} = x_k - \frac{4x_k^3}{12x_k^2} = x_k - \frac{x_k}{3} = \frac{2}{3}x_k.
\]
The iterates form a geometric sequence: $x_k = (2/3)^k \times 0.25 \to 0 = x^\star$. The convergence rate is \emph{linear} ($|1 - 1/3| = 2/3$ per step), not quadratic, because $g''(x^\star) = 0$ at the minimum (the Hessian is zero). Theorem~\ref{thm:newton}'s quadratic convergence requires $\nabla^2 f(x^\star) > 0$ (positive definite Hessian at the optimum); when $\nabla^2 f(x^\star) = 0$, the local model degenerates and the quadratic convergence is lost. This is the reason damping (Tikhonov regularisation $\lambda > 0$) is added in our implementation: $H_k + \lambda I$ is always positive definite, maintaining a valid Newton direction even when $H_k$ is singular.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Newton's Method Algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Damped Newton's Method}
\label{alg:newton}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, damping $\lambda$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $H_k \gets \nabla^2 f(x_k) + \lambda I$ \Comment{Tikhonov regularisation ensures positive definiteness}
    \State $p_k \gets H_k^{-1}\, g_k$ \Comment{Solve $H_k p_k = g_k$ via \texttt{numpy.linalg.solve}}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, p_k$ \Comment{Damped step $\alpha < 1$ for non-quadratic objectives}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Newton Decrement and Progress Certificate}

\begin{remark}[Newton decrement as a stopping criterion]
The \emph{Newton decrement} $\lambda(x_k) = \sqrt{\nabla f(x_k)^T [\nabla^2 f(x_k)]^{-1} \nabla f(x_k)}$ measures the magnitude of the Newton step and provides a tight bound on the suboptimality:
\[
  f(x_k) - f^\star \leq \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda(x_k)^2.
\]
This gives a primal certificate without knowing $f^\star$: halt when $\lambda(x_k)^2/2 \leq \epsilon$. Unlike gradient norm $\|\nabla f(x_k)\|$, the Newton decrement is \emph{affine invariant} (unchanged under coordinate reparametrisations) and accounts for the local curvature. For Benchmark A (quadratic), $\lambda(x_0)^2/2 = f(x_0) - f^\star$ exactly, so one step reduces $\lambda$ to zero. For Benchmark C, even with $\alpha = 0.22$, the decrement decreases rapidly in early iterations as the Hessian guides large curvature-corrected steps.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Computational Cost Comparison}

Newton's method has a higher per-iteration cost than gradient descent:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{GD}: $O(d)$ for gradient computation, $O(d)$ for the update step.
\item \textbf{Newton}: $O(d^2)$ for Hessian storage, $O(d^2)$ for Hessian computation, $O(d^3)$ for solving $H_k p_k = g_k$ via Gaussian elimination (or $O(d^2)$ for triangular systems after Cholesky factorisation).
\end{itemize}
For $d = 2$ these differences are negligible, and Newton's 1 iteration versus GD's 80 gives an $80\times$ reduction in total work. For large-scale problems ($d \sim 10^6$--$10^9$ in deep learning), exact Newton is impractical due to $O(d^2)$ storage and $O(d^3)$ solve cost. Practical alternatives include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{L-BFGS}: Approximates $H_k^{-1}$ using the last $m$ gradient differences, with $O(md)$ storage and $O(md)$ solve cost per step. L-BFGS updates satisfy the secant equation: $H_{k+1}(x_{k+1}-x_k) = \nabla f(x_{k+1}) - \nabla f(x_k)$, which is the first-order consistency condition for the Hessian approximation. With $m=5$--10 stored pairs, L-BFGS often approaches Newton's convergence rate at $O(md)$ per step.
\item \textbf{Gauss-Newton}: Approximates the Hessian of a least-squares objective as $J^TJ$ (where $J$ is the Jacobian), avoiding second-order derivatives entirely. For least-squares $f(x) = \frac{1}{2}\|r(x)\|^2$: $\nabla^2 f = J^TJ + \sum_i r_i(x)\nabla^2 r_i(x) \approx J^TJ$ when residuals $r_i \approx 0$ near the solution. This gives the Gauss-Newton step $p_k = -(J^TJ)^{-1}J^Tr(x_k)$, computable without evaluating second derivatives of the residuals.
\item \textbf{Hessian-free Newton (truncated CG)}: Solves the Newton system inexactly using conjugate gradient, requiring only Hessian-vector products (computable in $O(d)$ via automatic differentiation) rather than the full Hessian.
\end{itemize}

\begin{remark}[Iteration complexity: Newton vs first-order methods]
For a $\mu$-strongly convex $L$-smooth function with $M$-Lipschitz Hessian, reaching $\epsilon$-accuracy ($f(x_k) - f^\star \leq \epsilon$) requires:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{GD}: $O(\kappa \log(1/\epsilon))$ iterations, each costing $O(d)$ $\Rightarrow$ total $O(\kappa d \log(1/\epsilon))$.
  \item \textbf{Newton} (local phase): $O(\log\log(1/\epsilon))$ iterations (double-exponential convergence), each costing $O(d^3)$ $\Rightarrow$ total $O(d^3 \log\log(1/\epsilon))$.
  \item \textbf{Newton} (global phase from damped Newton): $O(\kappa \cdot f_0/\mu)$ damped steps to enter the local convergence region, each $O(d^3)$.
\end{itemize}
For $d = 2$ and $\kappa = 2504$: GD needs $2504\log(1/\epsilon)$ iterations vs Newton's effectively $O(1)$ once in the local region (e.g., 5 iterations for $\epsilon = 10^{-6}$ vs GD's $\approx 35{,}000$). This shows why Newton is dramatically faster for small-to-medium $d$ but the advantage reverses for large $d$ where $O(d^3)$ per step dominates.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Trust region vs line search: two globalisation strategies]
Damped Newton uses a fixed step size $\alpha \in (0,1)$ (a \emph{line search} strategy) to prevent overshooting when the quadratic model is inaccurate. An alternative globalisation strategy is the \emph{trust region} method: instead of restricting the step length $\alpha$, restrict the step to a ball $\|d\| \leq \Delta_k$ (the trust region radius) and solve the subproblem
\[
  \min_{d:\|d\|\leq\Delta_k} f(x_k) + \nabla f(x_k)^T d + \tfrac{1}{2}d^T \nabla^2 f(x_k)\, d.
\]
The radius $\Delta_k$ is adjusted adaptively: if the actual decrease matches the predicted decrease (high model accuracy), expand $\Delta_{k+1} = 2\Delta_k$; if the model is poor (low ratio of actual to predicted decrease), shrink $\Delta_{k+1} = \Delta_k/4$.

Key differences between line search and trust region Newton:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Indefinite Hessian}: Line search Newton requires a positive definite Hessian (for the Newton direction to be a descent direction). Trust region can handle indefinite Hessians by capping the step at the trust radius, naturally moving away from negative curvature directions.
  \item \textbf{Rosenbrock application}: At $x_0 = (-1, 1)$, the Rosenbrock Hessian is positive definite ($\lambda_{\min} \approx 0.4 > 0$), so both line search and trust region work. However, at saddle points of other functions, trust region is essential.
  \item \textbf{Convergence theory}: Trust region Newton has \emph{global convergence} guarantees to first-order stationary points (even from arbitrary starting points and with indefinite Hessians), while line search Newton requires positive definiteness.
  \item \textbf{Per-iteration cost}: The trust region subproblem requires solving a sequence of shifted Newton systems $(H + \lambda I)d = -g$ for different values of $\lambda$ (Levenberg-Marquardt regularisation), costing $O(d^3)$ per outer iteration --- same as line search Newton.
\end{itemize}
In our implementation, the damped Newton with $\alpha = 0.22$ (Benchmark C) effectively acts like a fixed-radius trust region: the small $\alpha$ limits the step norm to $0.22\|H^{-1}g\|$, preventing the Newton direction from overshooting the narrow Rosenbrock valley.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Part A: Finite Difference and Nesterov Random Search (Benchmark B)}

\subsubsection{Finite Difference Gradient Approximation}

When analytical gradients are unavailable (e.g.\ for black-box simulators or non-differentiable models), the gradient can be approximated via finite differences:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x) \approx \frac{f(x + \delta e_i) - f(x)}{\delta} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x) + O(\delta) + O\!\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}}{\delta}\right).
\label{eq:fd}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[FD error bound]\label{prop:fd}
The forward finite-difference approximation has total error
\[
  \left|\frac{f(x + \delta e_i) - f(x)}{\delta} - \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x)\right| \;\leq\; \frac{M\delta}{2} + \frac{2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}}{\delta},
\]
where $M$ bounds the second derivative and $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}} \approx 10^{-16}$ is machine epsilon. The total error is minimised at $\delta^\star = \sqrt{4\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}/M} \approx \sqrt{4 \times 10^{-16}/M}$. For $M \approx 1$, this gives $\delta^\star \approx 2 \times 10^{-8}$.
\end{proposition}

The experiment tests $\delta = 0.05$ (close to good: $2\times$ the optimal for typical curvature) and $\delta = 0.8$ (poor: truncation error $O(0.8) \approx 0.4$ is comparable to the gradient itself). Forward FD requires $d+1 = 3$ function evaluations per gradient estimate for $d=2$.

\begin{remark}[Finite-difference Hessian approximation for derivative-free Newton]
The Hessian can also be approximated via finite differences when analytical second derivatives are unavailable:
\begin{align*}
  \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i^2} &\approx \frac{f(x + \delta e_i) - 2f(x) + f(x - \delta e_i)}{\delta^2} + O(\delta^2) \quad \text{(diagonal)}, \\
  \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i \partial x_j} &\approx \frac{f(x+\delta e_i + \delta e_j) - f(x+\delta e_i) - f(x+\delta e_j) + f(x)}{{\delta^2}} + O(\delta^2) \quad \text{(off-diagonal)}.
\end{align*}
The diagonal requires $d$ extra evaluations (3 per coordinate) beyond the $d+1$ for the gradient. The full Hessian requires $O(d^2)$ evaluations. For $d=2$ (our benchmarks): 4 evaluations for the gradient + 4 for the Hessian = 8 total per Newton step, vs 3 for FD GD. The benefit: Newton step sizes are automatically calibrated to the local curvature, potentially achieving quadratic convergence even without analytical Hessians. The drawback: $O(d^2)$ evaluations per step scales poorly to large $d$.

For Benchmark B at $x = (-1, 4)$: $f_{xx} \approx [f(-1+\delta, 4) - 2f(-1,4) + f(-1-\delta,4)]/\delta^2 = [2-\sin(x+\delta)]/1 \approx 2+\sin(1) \approx 2.84$ (consistent with $H_{11} = 2-\sin(x_1) \approx 2.84$ analytically) and $f_{yy} \approx 10$ (from $5\times2 = 10$). This confirms that FD Hessian approximation with $\delta = 0.1$ achieves $\approx O(\delta^2) \approx 1\%$ error on Benchmark B.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Centered finite differences: second-order accuracy]
The \emph{centered} finite difference approximation
\[
  \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x) \approx \frac{f(x + \delta e_i) - f(x - \delta e_i)}{2\delta}
\]
achieves $O(\delta^2)$ truncation error (compared to $O(\delta)$ for forward FD), by cancelling the first-order Taylor error. The total error bound becomes:
\[
  \text{error} \leq \frac{M_3 \delta^2}{6} + \frac{2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}}{\delta},
\]
where $M_3$ bounds the third derivative. The optimal $\delta^\star = (3\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}/M_3)^{1/3} \approx 10^{-5}$ achieves a minimum error $\approx 10^{-11}$, four orders of magnitude smaller than forward FD's $\approx 10^{-8}$. The cost is $2d = 4$ evaluations per gradient (vs $d+1 = 3$ for forward FD). For our experiments, $\delta = 0.05$ with forward FD gives truncation error $\approx 10\times0.05/2 = 0.25$ per coordinate; centered FD would give $\approx 10\times0.05^2/6 \approx 0.004$ --- an $\approx 60\times$ improvement at only $33\%$ more function evaluations. In practice, whenever function evaluations are cheap, centered differences should be preferred.
\end{remark}

\subsubsection{Nesterov Random Search}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Nesterov Random Search (Zeroth-Order)}
\label{alg:nrs}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, perturbation $\delta$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State Sample $u_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, normalise: $\hat{u}_k \gets u_k / \|u_k\|$ \Comment{Uniform direction on unit sphere}
    \State $\hat{g}_k \gets \dfrac{f(x_k + \delta \hat{u}_k) - f(x_k)}{\delta}\, \hat{u}_k$ \Comment{Directional derivative estimate}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \hat{g}_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

This zeroth-order method estimates only a single directional derivative $\nabla f(x_k)^T \hat{u}_k$, requiring only \textbf{2 function evaluations per iteration}, regardless of dimension $d$. This makes it \emph{dimension-independent} in oracle cost per step --- a key advantage for high-dimensional problems. The estimate is an unbiased estimator of the gradient scaled by the projection variance: $\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_k] = \frac{1}{d}\nabla f(x_k)$ (up to normalisation), so convergence is slower by a factor of $d$ relative to exact gradient methods.

\begin{remark}[Nesterov random search: worked first step on Benchmark B]
At $x_0 = (-1, 4)$, $\nabla f(x_0) = (-4.54, 20)^T$ (as computed in §\ref{sec:benchmarks}). With $\alpha = 0.03$ and $\delta = 0.1$, suppose the sampled unit direction is $\hat{u}_0 = (0.6, 0.8)$ (a normalised random vector). The two function evaluations are:
\begin{align*}
  f(x_0 + 0.1\hat{u}_0) &= f(-0.94, 4.08) = (-0.94-1)^2 + 5(4.08-2)^2 + \sin(-0.94) \\
  &\approx 3.764 + 21.632 - 0.808 = 24.588, \\
  f(x_0) &= f(-1, 4) = 4 + 20 - \sin(1) \approx 24 - 0.841 = 23.159.
\end{align*}
The directional derivative estimate: $\hat{g}_0 = \frac{24.588 - 23.159}{0.1}(0.6, 0.8) = 14.29 \times (0.6, 0.8) = (8.57, 11.43)$. The update: $x_1 = (-1,4) - 0.03(8.57, 11.43) = (-1.257, 3.657)$. Compare to the exact gradient step with $\alpha = 0.06$: $x_1^{\mathrm{GD}} = (-1,4) - 0.06(-4.54, 20) = (-0.727, 2.8)$, which makes a much larger step in the correct direction $(-4.54, 20)$. The random estimate $(8.57, 11.43)$ is a projection onto $\hat{u}_0 = (0.6, 0.8)$: the true inner product $\nabla f(x_0)^T\hat{u}_0 = -4.54\times0.6 + 20\times0.8 = -2.72 + 16 = 13.28 \approx 14.29$ (the small discrepancy is from the finite $\delta = 0.1$). This example shows that when $\hat{u}_0$ is poorly aligned with $-\nabla f$ (here the angle between $\hat{u}_0$ and $-\nabla f/\|\nabla f\|$ is $\cos\theta = (-0.6\times4.54 + 0.8\times(-20))/\sqrt{4.54^2+20^2} \approx -18.7/20.5 \approx -0.91$, i.e.\ a poor direction), the step moves away from the minimum. Over many random directions, the expected step is $\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_k] \propto \nabla f(x_k)$, providing net progress toward the optimum at a slower rate than exact gradient methods.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Coordinate descent as a structured zeroth-order method]
\emph{Coordinate descent} (CD) is a deterministic analogue of Nesterov random search that cycles through coordinate directions $e_1, e_2, \ldots, e_d$ in fixed or random order, minimising $f$ in one coordinate at a time:
\[
  x_{k+1} = \arg\min_{t \in \mathbb{R}} f(x_k + t e_{i_k}), \quad i_k = k \bmod d + 1.
\]
For a separable objective ($f(x) = \sum_i f_i(x_i)$), each 1D minimisation is exact and cheap. For non-separable objectives (our case), each CD step requires either a 1D line search (2 FE per step) or exact closed-form solution. Convergence:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Convex, $L$-smooth}: CD with cyclic coordinate updates achieves $O(1/k)$ rate (same as GD) but with $d$ coordinate steps per ``pass''.
  \item \textbf{Strongly convex}: Linear rate $O((1-\mu/(dL))^k)$ per coordinate step --- $d$ times slower than GD per step, equivalent per pass.
  \item \textbf{Separable case}: If $f$ is separable, CD converges in $O(d\log(1/\epsilon))$ total steps, exploiting structure that GD cannot.
\end{itemize}
For Benchmark B with $H \approx \mathrm{diag}(1.45, 10)$: CD on $x_2$ converges in $O(10/10 \cdot\log(1/\epsilon)) = O(\log(1/\epsilon))$ steps (immediately, since the $x_2$ curvature $= L_{x_2} = 10$ and $\mu_{x_2} \approx 10$); CD on $x_1$ takes longer. The coordinate structure makes CD particularly effective on anisotropic problems like Benchmark B.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Dimension dependence of zeroth-order methods]
For an $L$-smooth convex function in $\mathbb{R}^d$, Nesterov \& Spokoiny (2017) proved that the randomised gradient estimator $\hat{g}_k = \frac{d}{2\delta}[f(x+\delta u)-f(x-\delta u)]u$ (two-point, dimension-scaled) satisfies:
\[
  \mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_k] = \nabla f_\delta(x), \qquad \mathbb{E}\|\hat{g}_k - \nabla f(x)\|^2 \leq d\,\frac{L^2\delta^2}{4} + \frac{4d^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}^2}{\delta^2},
\]
where $f_\delta(x) = \mathbb{E}_{u}[f(x+\delta u)]$ is a smoothed surrogate. To achieve $\epsilon$-accuracy requires $O(d/\epsilon)$ iterations (vs $O(1/\epsilon)$ for exact gradient methods). The \emph{oracle complexity} --- total function evaluations --- is $O(d/\epsilon)$ for zeroth-order vs $O(1/\epsilon)$ for first-order: an additional factor of $d$. For our $d=2$ problem this overhead is minimal, but for $d=10^6$ (modern neural networks), the gap makes zeroth-order methods impractical for general optimisation.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Part B: Derivative-Free Methods (Benchmark C)}

\subsubsection{Nelder--Mead Simplex Method}

The Nelder--Mead algorithm maintains a simplex of $n+1 = 3$ vertices in $\mathbb{R}^2$ and iteratively transforms it through four operations: \textbf{reflection} ($\alpha_r = 1$), \textbf{expansion} ($\gamma = 2$), \textbf{contraction} ($\rho = 0.5$), and \textbf{shrinkage} ($\sigma = 0.5$). These parameters are the standard Nelder--Mead coefficients. The simplex contracts when it approaches a region of low curvature and expands when it finds better points. No gradient information is required; the algorithm uses only function values at the simplex vertices ($n+1 = 3$ evaluations per iteration).

\begin{remark}[Nelder--Mead geometry and the four operations]
Each iteration of Nelder--Mead works as follows, given simplex vertices $\{p_1,\ldots,p_{n+1}\}$ sorted by $f$ (best to worst):
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item Compute centroid $\bar{p} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n p_i$ (all but the worst vertex).
  \item \textbf{Reflect}: try $p_r = \bar{p} + 1.0(\bar{p} - p_{n+1})$ (mirror worst through centroid). If better than second-worst but not best: accept $p_r$.
  \item \textbf{Expand}: if $p_r$ is better than the best, try $p_e = \bar{p} + 2.0(\bar{p} - p_{n+1})$ (go further in reflection direction). Accept whichever of $p_e, p_r$ is better.
  \item \textbf{Contract}: if $p_r$ is worse than second-worst, try $p_c$ between centroid and worst ($\rho = 0.5$). Accept if better than worst.
  \item \textbf{Shrink}: if contraction fails, shrink all vertices toward the best: $p_i \gets p_1 + \sigma(p_i - p_1)$.
\end{enumerate}
The expansion step is the key: it allows the simplex to ``race'' along a successful direction (e.g.\ down the Rosenbrock valley), while the reflection step provides the basic gradient-free direction of steepest descent. The lack of convergence proof for $d \geq 2$ arises because the simplex can degenerate (all vertices nearly collinear), halting the search; in practice, restarting with a fresh simplex from the current best point avoids this.
\end{remark}

Settings: initial simplex step $= 0.35$, iterations $= 160$.

\subsubsection{Grid Search}

Grid search evaluates the objective on a uniform $55 \times 55 = 3025$-point grid over $x_1 \in [-2, 2]$, $x_2 \in [-1, 3]$, with resolution $\Delta x_1 \approx 0.074$ and $\Delta x_2 \approx 0.075$.

\begin{remark}[Curse of dimensionality for grid search]
Grid search's evaluation count $N^d$ grows exponentially in dimension $d$. For accuracy $\epsilon$ (maximum grid error $\approx \Delta x = (b-a)/N$), we need $N \geq (b-a)/\epsilon$, giving $N^d \geq ((b-a)/\epsilon)^d$ evaluations. Concrete comparison to achieve $\epsilon = 0.01$ accuracy on $[0,4]^d$:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ccc}
\toprule
$d$ & Grid evaluations ($N = 400$) & First-order GD (estimate) \\

\begin{remark}[Nesterov random search and CMA-ES: connection to evolution strategies]
The Nesterov random search (Nesterov \& Spokoiny, 2017) uses a two-point estimate along a random Gaussian direction $u \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)$. This has a deep connection to \emph{evolution strategies} (ES) in black-box optimisation:

\textbf{Standard ES} (Rechenberg, 1965): Maintain a population $\{x + \sigma \epsilon_i\}_{i=1}^n$ with $\epsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, evaluate $f$ at each, then update $x$ toward the better half (``$(\mu/\mu, \lambda)$-ES''). The expected gradient estimate is $\frac{1}{\sigma}\mathbb{E}[f(x + \sigma\epsilon)\epsilon] = \nabla f_\sigma(x)$ (the gradient of the smoothed function), making ES equivalent to zeroth-order gradient ascent on the population mean.

\textbf{CMA-ES} (Hansen \& Ostermeier, 2001) --- \emph{Covariance Matrix Adaptation} ES --- generalises this by maintaining a full covariance matrix $C_k$ for the search distribution $\mathcal{N}(0, C_k)$. The covariance is updated to align principal axes with the directions of recent successful steps, effectively learning the inverse Hessian. CMA-ES achieves near-Newton convergence rates on well-conditioned functions at the cost of $O(d^2)$ storage. For our $d=2$ problems CMA-ES would match Newton's method; for $d \sim 100$ it is often the method of choice for black-box optimisation.

Nesterov random search uses $n = 1$ random direction per step (extreme efficiency but high variance) and applies a smoothness-based step size; CMA-ES uses $n \gg 1$ directions but adapts their distribution. Both are asymptotically equivalent to gradient descent on the smoothed objective, but with very different variance-computation trade-offs.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Discussion: When to Use Derivative-Free Methods}

Derivative-free optimisation is necessary when: (1) the objective is computed by a simulation, solver, or physical experiment with no differentiable model; (2) the function is non-differentiable; or (3) automatic differentiation infrastructure is unavailable. The key trade-off versus gradient-based methods is oracle efficiency: with exact gradients, each evaluation provides $O(d)$ bits of information about the objective; with function-only oracles, each evaluation provides $O(1)$ bits. This fundamental information gap is why derivative-free methods require many more iterations for the same accuracy, particularly in high dimensions. In practice, for $d \leq 5$--10, Nelder--Mead or pattern search are effective; for higher dimensions, randomised methods or evolutionary strategies are preferred.

\begin{remark}[Convergence theory for Nelder--Mead: known results and open questions]
Nelder--Mead is one of the most widely used optimisation algorithms in practice, yet its convergence theory for $d \geq 2$ is largely an open problem. Key known results:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{One-dimensional case ($d=1$)}: McKinnon (1998) proved that Nelder--Mead converges for $d=1$ on strictly unimodal functions. The update reduces to a variant of the golden-section search.
  \item \textbf{Counterexamples for $d \geq 2$}: McKinnon (1998) constructed a specific $d=2$ strictly convex function and initial simplex for which Nelder--Mead converges to a \emph{non-optimal} point. The simplex degenerates (collapses to a line) and stops making progress even though $\nabla f \neq 0$ at the limit point.
  \item \textbf{Practical modifications}: Adding periodic \emph{restarts} (recreating the simplex around the current best point with original step size) prevents degeneration. The restart variant provably converges on strictly convex functions.
  \item \textbf{Rate}: Even where convergence is guaranteed, the rate is at best $O(1/k^{2/d})$ (sublinear), much slower than gradient-based methods.
\end{enumerate}
In our Benchmark C experiment with initial step $= 0.35$, the simplex does not degenerate within 160 iterations because the Rosenbrock valley maintains enough curvature variation to keep the simplex non-degenerate. The observed convergence (best objective decreasing from $f \approx 38$ to below $f = 3.5$) is typical for low-dimensional Nelder--Mead on the Rosenbrock: rapid initial descent followed by slow valley traversal.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Information-theoretic lower bounds for zeroth-order optimisation]
Nemirovskii \& Yudin (1983) established that for the class of $L$-Lipschitz continuous functions over $\mathcal{X} = [-1,1]^d$, any zeroth-order algorithm requires at least $\Omega((1/\epsilon)^d)$ function evaluations to guarantee accuracy $\epsilon$ in the \emph{worst case}. For smooth functions, the lower bound is $\Omega(d/\epsilon)$ (vs $\Omega(d/\sqrt{\epsilon})$ for non-smooth). These bounds prove that:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item Grid search's $N^d$ evaluations are tight for the non-smooth case.
  \item Nesterov random search's $O(d/\epsilon)$ is optimal for smooth convex functions among zeroth-order methods.
  \item No zeroth-order method can achieve the first-order bound $O(1/\epsilon)$ for general $d$.
\end{enumerate}
The separation between first-order ($\Omega(1/\epsilon)$) and zeroth-order ($\Omega(d/\epsilon)$) lower bounds shows that gradient information is worth precisely a factor of $d$ in sample efficiency. For $d = 2$ this is negligible; for $d = 10^6$ this is the difference between tractable and intractable optimisation.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}

Minimise Benchmark B subject to $x_1 \geq 0.5$:
\begin{equation}
\min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^2}\; f(x_1, x_2) = (x_1-1)^2 + 5(x_2-2)^2 + \sin(x_1), \quad \text{subject to}\quad x_1 \geq 0.5.
\end{equation}
Starting point: $x_0 = (0.2, 4.0)$ (infeasible, since $0.2 < 0.5$). The feasible set is $\mathcal{C} = \{x : x_1 \geq 0.5\}$ (a half-space).

\subsubsection{KKT Conditions}

The KKT conditions for this inequality-constrained problem are: there exists $\mu \geq 0$ such that
\begin{align}
  \nabla f(x^\star) + \mu \nabla g(x^\star) &= 0, \quad g(x) := 0.5 - x_1 \leq 0 \\
  \mu g(x^\star) &= 0 \quad \text{(complementary slackness)}
\end{align}
Since $\nabla g = (-1, 0)^T$, the stationarity condition gives $[2(x_1^\star - 1) + \cos(x_1^\star) - \mu,\; 10(x_2^\star - 2)] = 0$.

The unconstrained minimum at $x_1^\star \approx 0.582 > 0.5$ is \emph{feasible}. Therefore the constraint is \emph{inactive} ($g(x^\star) < 0$), complementary slackness forces $\mu = 0$, and the KKT conditions reduce to $\nabla f(x^\star) = 0$. The constrained and unconstrained minima \emph{coincide}. Algorithms that enforce feasibility throughout converge to this same point; those that tolerate infeasibility (penalty methods) must be driven there by a sufficiently strong penalty.

\begin{remark}[Slater's condition and KKT necessity]
The KKT conditions are necessary for optimality under a \emph{constraint qualification} (CQ). The most commonly used CQ for inequality constraints is \emph{Slater's condition}: there exists a strictly feasible point $\bar{x}$ with $g(\bar{x}) < 0$ (i.e.\ $\bar{x}_1 > 0.5$). For our problem, any $x$ with $x_1 > 0.5$ satisfies Slater's condition, so the KKT conditions are both necessary and sufficient (since $f$ is locally convex near $x^\star$) for the constrained minimum. This guarantees that our KKT analysis correctly identifies $x^\star$. Without a CQ (e.g.\ for problems where all feasible points lie on the active constraint boundary), the KKT multipliers may not exist even at the minimum, making the KKT system uninformative. For linear constraints (as in Frank--Wolfe's box constraint), Slater's condition always holds as long as the feasible set has non-empty interior.
\end{remark}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Projected Gradient Descent}
\label{alg:pgd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, projection $\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \nabla f(x_k)$ \Comment{Gradient step (possibly infeasible)}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets \Pi_{\mathcal{C}}(z_{k+1}) = \max(0.5,\; z_{k+1}[1]) \cdot e_1 + z_{k+1}[2] \cdot e_2$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The projection onto $\mathcal{C} = \{x : x_1 \geq 0.5\}$ is simply $\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}(z) = (\max(0.5, z_1),\; z_2)$, a single \texttt{max} operation. For strongly convex $f$ restricted to $\mathcal{C}$, projected GD with step $\alpha \leq 1/L$ achieves linear convergence to $x^\star$.

\begin{remark}[Proximal gradient method: a unifying generalisation]
Projected GD is a special case of the \emph{proximal gradient method}. For a composite objective $F(x) = f(x) + g(x)$ where $f$ is smooth and $g$ is convex but possibly non-smooth, the proximal gradient update is:
\[
  x_{k+1} = \mathrm{prox}_{\alpha g}(x_k - \alpha \nabla f(x_k)), \quad \text{where } \mathrm{prox}_{\alpha g}(z) = \arg\min_x \left[g(x) + \frac{1}{2\alpha}\|x - z\|^2\right].
\]
Key special cases:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Projected GD}: $g = \iota_{\mathcal{C}}$ (indicator function of $\mathcal{C}$: $g(x) = 0$ if $x \in \mathcal{C}$, $+\infty$ otherwise). Then $\mathrm{prox}_{\alpha g}(z) = \Pi_{\mathcal{C}}(z)$, recovering the projection.
  \item \textbf{LASSO / $\ell_1$ regularisation}: $g(x) = \lambda\|x\|_1$. Then $\mathrm{prox}_{\alpha g}(z) = \mathrm{sign}(z)\max(|z| - \alpha\lambda, 0)$ (soft thresholding), computable in $O(d)$.
  \item \textbf{Group LASSO}: $g(x) = \lambda\sum_k \|x_{G_k}\|_2$ (block $\ell_1$). Proximal operator performs block-wise soft thresholding.
  \item \textbf{Nuclear norm}: $g(X) = \lambda\|X\|_*$. Proximal operator is matrix soft-thresholding on singular values: $\mathrm{prox}_{\alpha g}(Z) = U\mathrm{diag}(\max(\sigma_i - \alpha\lambda, 0))V^T$ (SVD decomposition required, $O(pq\min(p,q))$).
\end{itemize}
The proximal gradient method converges at rate $O(1/k)$ for convex $F$ and can be accelerated to $O(1/k^2)$ via FISTA (Beck \& Teboulle, 2009 --- the proximal analogue of Nesterov's method). For our Q5 problem with $g(x) = \lambda\max(0, 0.5-x_1)$ (linear penalty): the proximal operator is a shift-and-clip operation, which is exactly the projection $\Pi_\mathcal{C}$ in the $\lambda \to \infty$ limit.
\end{remark}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Penalty Method}
\label{alg:penalty}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, penalty weight $\lambda$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $F_{\lambda}(x) \gets f(x) + \lambda\, \max(0,\, 0.5 - x_1)$ \Comment{Linear penalty for constraint violation}
    \State $\nabla F_\lambda(x) \gets \nabla f(x) - \lambda \mathbf{1}[x_1 < 0.5]\, e_1$ \Comment{Subgradient}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \nabla F_{\lambda}(x_k)$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The penalty term $\lambda \max(0, 0.5 - x_1)$ adds cost proportional to the constraint violation. As $\lambda \to \infty$, the minimiser of $F_\lambda$ converges to the constrained minimiser $x^\star$. The convergence rate is $\|x^\star(\lambda) - x^\star\| = O(1/\lambda)$, requiring increasingly large $\lambda$ for high accuracy; this motivates Augmented Lagrangian methods, which add a quadratic term to improve the accuracy-penalty trade-off.

Settings: Projected GD ($\alpha = 0.08$); Penalty with $\lambda \in \{0.15, 1.8, 4.5\}$ ($\alpha = 0.05$ for $\lambda \leq 1.8$, $\alpha = 0.03$ for $\lambda = 4.5$); unconstrained GD ($\alpha = 0.07$). All methods run for 100 iterations.

\subsection{Comparative Discussion}

\textbf{Projected GD} enforces feasibility as a hard constraint at every iteration via the projection operator. Key properties: (1) iterates are always feasible, enabling early stopping with a valid solution; (2) the method inherits the convergence rate of unconstrained GD once inside the feasible set; (3) since the unconstrained minimum $(x_1^\star \approx 0.582) > 0.5$ is feasible, projected GD converges to the same point as unconstrained GD, with the projection becoming inactive after the first iteration. The cost is a projection oracle; for half-space constraints this is $O(1)$, but for semidefinite or combinatorial constraint sets, projection can be as hard as the original problem.

\textbf{Key observation:} Once the penalty method iterate becomes feasible ($x_1^{(k)} \geq 0.5$), the penalty term $\lambda\max(0, 0.5 - x_1)$ equals zero and its gradient is zero. The penalty method's update then becomes \emph{identical} to unconstrained gradient descent on $f$. This means: (i)~for $\lambda = 0.15$, the trajectory may have a long infeasible phase and only reaches feasibility slowly; (ii)~once feasible, all penalty methods (regardless of $\lambda$) converge identically to the unconstrained minimum. The role of $\lambda$ is therefore entirely about how quickly the initial infeasible region is escaped.

\textbf{Penalty methods} trade exact feasibility for implementation simplicity: only the gradient of $F_\lambda = f + \lambda\max(0, g)$ is needed. Increasing $\lambda$ improves accuracy but introduces two competing issues. First, larger $\lambda$ makes $F_\lambda$ less smooth: the linear penalty $\lambda\max(0, g)$ is not differentiable at $g = 0$ (i.e.\ at the constraint boundary $x_1 = 0.5$), so subgradient methods are required and smaller step sizes are needed for stability. Second, for very large $\lambda$, the condition number of the penalised problem grows, slowing the gradient method's convergence rate. The resulting trade-off requires careful $\lambda$ tuning: too small gives infeasible solutions; too large creates a stiff problem requiring tiny steps.

\begin{remark}[Penalty method condition number growth]
For the quadratic penalty $F_\lambda(x) = f(x) + \frac{\lambda}{2}\max(0, 0.5-x_1)^2$, the Hessian in the infeasible region ($x_1 < 0.5$) is $\nabla^2 F_\lambda = \nabla^2 f + \lambda e_1 e_1^T$. Near the optimum of $F_\lambda$ where $x_1 \approx 0.5$, the $x_1$-curvature of $F_\lambda$ is $2(x_1-1) + \lambda$ while the $x_2$-curvature is $10$ (from Benchmark B). For $\lambda = 4.5$: $x_1$-curvature $\approx -0.836 + 4.5 = 3.664$ and condition number $\kappa(F_{4.5}) \approx 10/0.5 \approx 20$ (roughly). For $\lambda = 100$: $x_1$-curvature $\approx 100$ and $\kappa(F_{100}) \approx 100/0.5 = 200$ --- a $10\times$ increase that requires a $10\times$ smaller step size, exactly as observed. This shows that the effective condition number of the penalised problem scales linearly with $\lambda$, making GD convergence on $F_\lambda$ slow as $\lambda \to \infty$.
\end{remark}

A \emph{quadratic penalty} $F_\lambda(x) = f(x) + \lambda\max(0, g(x))^2$ is smooth (differentiable everywhere) and better conditioned, but requires $\lambda \to \infty$ more slowly to achieve high accuracy. The \emph{augmented Lagrangian method} (ALM) improves on both by maintaining dual variables $\nu_k$ and solving a sequence of problems:
\[
  x_{k+1} = \arg\min_x\; f(x) + \nu_k g(x) + \frac{\lambda}{2}\max(0, g(x))^2,
\]
with $\nu_{k+1} = \nu_k + \lambda \max(0, g(x_{k+1}))$. ALM achieves exact feasibility with bounded $\lambda$, avoiding the ill-conditioning of pure penalty approaches.

\begin{remark}[ADMM: distributed optimisation via augmented Lagrangian splitting]
The \emph{Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers} (ADMM) extends the ALM to problems with a \emph{separable} structure: $\min_{x, z} f(x) + g(z)$ subject to $Ax + Bz = c$. ADMM alternates:
\begin{align*}
  x_{k+1} &= \arg\min_x\; f(x) + \tfrac{\rho}{2}\|Ax + Bz_k - c + u_k\|^2 \quad \text{(x-update)}\\
  z_{k+1} &= \arg\min_z\; g(z) + \tfrac{\rho}{2}\|Ax_{k+1} + Bz - c + u_k\|^2 \quad \text{(z-update)}\\
  u_{k+1} &= u_k + Ax_{k+1} + Bz_{k+1} - c \quad \text{(dual update)}
\end{align*}
where $u_k = (1/\rho)\nu_k$ is the scaled dual variable. ADMM converges for convex $f$ and $g$ at $O(1/k)$ rate (sublinear) and achieves linear convergence under strong convexity. Its power comes from decomposability: the $x$- and $z$-updates can often be solved in closed form or in parallel (for separable $f$ and $g$), enabling distributed optimisation across multiple processors. Connections to our Q5 problem: if the penalty method is applied to $F_\lambda = f(x) + \lambda\max(0, g(x))$ with a quadratic penalty term, one ADMM iteration corresponds to solving the $x$- and constraint ($z$)-updates alternately, with $g(z) = \lambda\max(0, 0.5-z_1)$ being the constraint indicator handled in the $z$-update (a soft-thresholding step).
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Augmented Lagrangian convergence rate and dual ascent]
The Augmented Lagrangian method can be interpreted as applying \emph{dual ascent} to the dual problem. For the equality-constrained problem $\min f(x)$ s.t.\ $g(x) = 0$, the dual function is $d(\nu) = \min_x [f(x) + \nu g(x)]$ and the dual problem is $\max_\nu d(\nu)$. Dual ascent updates $\nu_{k+1} = \nu_k + \lambda g(x_{k+1})$, which is gradient ascent on $d$ with step $\lambda$. The augmented term $(\lambda/2)g(x)^2$ is added to the Lagrangian to make the inner minimisation strongly convex (adding $\lambda$ to the curvature), enabling fast $x$-updates. The overall convergence rate of ALM is:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Sublinear} ($O(1/k)$) for general convex $f$ with non-smooth $g$.
  \item \textbf{Linear} ($\rho^k$ for $\rho < 1$) when $f$ is strongly convex and the constraint Jacobian has full column rank.
  \item \textbf{Quadratic} for equality constraints when $\lambda$ is large enough and $x^\star$ is an isolated local minimum with strict complementarity.
\end{itemize}
The key advantage over pure penalty: the dual variable $\nu_k$ evolves to correctly estimate the true KKT multiplier $\nu^\star$, so the penalty constant $\lambda$ need only be large enough to ensure the inner problem is well-conditioned --- not to drive feasibility to zero. For our Q5 problem where the constraint is inactive ($\nu^\star = 0$), ALM would converge the dual variable to zero, $\nu_k \to 0$, while maintaining the same quadratic convergence as unconstrained Newton on $f$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Active set methods: a complementary approach]
An alternative to penalty and projection methods for inequality constraints is the \emph{active set method}. At each iterate $x_k$, the active set $\mathcal{A}(x_k) = \{i : g_i(x_k) = 0\}$ identifies constraints that are exactly binding. The active set method solves a sequence of equality-constrained subproblems (treating only active constraints as equalities) and updates the active set when the KKT multipliers become negative (indicating a constraint should be deactivated) or when the iterates leave the feasible set (indicating a constraint should be activated). For our Q5 problem: the constraint $x_1 \geq 0.5$ is inactive at $x^\star$ ($x_1^\star \approx 0.582 > 0.5$), so the active set method would identify the empty active set and reduce to unconstrained GD in the first step. Active set methods are most efficient when the active set changes infrequently; they are exact (no penalty approximation error) and can be warm-started efficiently. The simplex method for LP is a special case where each step changes exactly one constraint in the active set.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Subgradient method for non-smooth objectives]
The penalty function $F_\lambda(x) = f(x) + \lambda\max(0, 0.5-x_1)$ is \emph{non-smooth} at the constraint boundary $x_1 = 0.5$ (the max function has a kink there). At $x = (0.5, x_2)$, the subdifferential of the penalty term is:
\[
  \partial[\lambda\max(0, 0.5-x_1)]_{x_1=0.5} = [-\lambda, 0],
\]
a set-valued interval (since the left derivative is $-\lambda$ and the right derivative is 0). The \emph{subgradient method} uses any element of $\partial F_\lambda(x_k)$ as the descent direction, with a non-increasing step size $\alpha_k \to 0$ satisfying $\sum_k \alpha_k = \infty$, $\sum_k \alpha_k^2 < \infty$ (e.g., $\alpha_k = c/\sqrt{k}$). The convergence rate of the subgradient method is $O(1/\sqrt{k})$ for convex non-smooth functions, slower than the $O((1-\mu/L)^k)$ linear rate for smooth strongly convex objectives. This explains why the penalty method with $\lambda = 4.5$ requires a smaller step $\alpha = 0.03$ (vs $\alpha = 0.05$ for $\lambda \leq 1.8$): the non-smooth kink at the constraint boundary forces a more conservative step to avoid oscillating across the boundary with a step size appropriate for the smooth interior. Our implementation uses the subgradient $\nabla F_\lambda = \nabla f - \lambda \mathbf{1}[x_1 < 0.5] e_1$, which selects 0 (the right derivative) at the boundary itself --- a conservative choice that avoids the step instability the left derivative would cause.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[KKT violation as a dual residual]
The linear penalty gradient $-\lambda \cdot \mathbf{1}[x_1 < 0.5]$ at a feasibility violation is precisely the KKT stationarity error. When the penalty method converges to an infeasible point $x^\infty$ with $g(x^\infty) > 0$, it satisfies the stationarity condition of the penalised problem: $\nabla f(x^\infty) - \lambda \nabla g(x^\infty) = 0$, which is the KKT condition with multiplier $\mu = \lambda$. However, complementary slackness ($\mu g(x^\infty) = 0$) is violated since $g(x^\infty) > 0$ and $\lambda > 0$. The infeasibility gap $g(x^\infty) = O(1/\lambda)$ vanishes as $\lambda \to \infty$, but the associated growth in the penalty's Lipschitz constant ($\propto \lambda$) forces step sizes $\alpha = O(1/\lambda)$, requiring $O(\lambda)$ gradient steps --- explaining why large $\lambda$ converges accurately but slowly. The ALM avoids this by updating $\nu_k$ to correctly estimate the KKT multiplier, so the penalty term needs only $\lambda$ large enough to ensure local convexity, not to drive infeasibility to zero.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Theoretical Background}

\begin{remark}[Frank--Wolfe gap worked computation]
At $x_0 = (1, 1)$ for $f(x) = (x_1-1)^2 + (x_2-5)^2$ with $\mathcal{X} = [0.5,5]\times[-5,10]$:
\[
  \nabla f(x_0) = (2(1-1), 2(1-5)) = (0, -8).
\]
The LP oracle gives $z_0 = \arg\min_{z \in \mathcal{X}} \nabla f^T z$: since $g_1 = 0$, any $z_1 \in [0.5, 5]$ is optimal; since $g_2 = -8 < 0$, optimal $z_2 = 10$ (upper bound). Taking $z_0 = (1, 10)$ (choosing $z_1 = 1$ as the arbitrary case), the FW gap is:
\[
  h_0 = \nabla f(x_0)^T(x_0 - z_0) = (0, -8) \cdot (0, -9) = 0 + 72 = 72.
\]
Since $f(x_0) - f^\star = (0 + 16) - 0 = 16$ and $h_0 = 72$, the gap overestimates the primal gap by $72/16 = 4.5\times$, but still provides a valid upper bound ($h_0 \geq f(x_0) - f^\star$). After one step: $x_1 = 0.90(1,1) + 0.10(1,10) = (1, 1.9)$. The new gap: $\nabla f(x_1) = (0, -6.2)$, $h_1 = (0,-6.2)\cdot(0,-9.1) = 56.4$ --- still large due to the interior optimum being far from any vertex. Convergence of the FW gap to zero mirrors convergence of $f(x_k)$ to $f^\star$.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Frank--Wolfe convergence]\label{thm:fw}
For an $L$-smooth convex function $f$ and compact convex set $\mathcal{X}$ with diameter $D = \max_{x,y \in \mathcal{X}}\|x-y\|$, the Frank--Wolfe algorithm with step sizes $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$ satisfies
\[
  f(x_k) - f^\star \;\leq\; \frac{2LD^2}{k+2} \;=\; O(1/k).
\]
This $O(1/k)$ rate is slower than projected gradient descent's linear rate for strongly convex $f$, but Frank--Wolfe requires only a \emph{linear minimisation oracle} over $\mathcal{X}$ rather than a projection oracle, which is computationally cheaper for many structured constraint sets.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}[Proof sketch of Frank--Wolfe $O(1/k)$ rate]
With step size $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$ and $x_{k+1} = (1-\gamma_k)x_k + \gamma_k z_k$:
\begin{align*}
  f(x_{k+1}) &\leq f(x_k) + \nabla f(x_k)^T(x_{k+1} - x_k) + \frac{L}{2}\|x_{k+1} - x_k\|^2 \quad (\text{descent lemma}) \\
  &= f(x_k) + \gamma_k \nabla f(x_k)^T(z_k - x_k) + \frac{L\gamma_k^2}{2}\|z_k - x_k\|^2 \\
  &\leq f(x_k) + \gamma_k \nabla f(x_k)^T(x^\star - x_k) + \frac{L\gamma_k^2 D^2}{2} \quad (\text{LP oracle optimality: } z_k = \arg\min_{z\in\mathcal{X}}g^Tz \leq g^Tx^\star) \\
  &\leq f(x_k) - \gamma_k(f(x_k) - f^\star) + \frac{L\gamma_k^2 D^2}{2} \quad (\text{convexity: } \nabla f(x_k)^T(x_k - x^\star) \geq f(x_k) - f^\star)
\end{align*}
Letting $h_k = f(x_k) - f^\star$ and $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$:
\[
  h_{k+1} \leq (1 - \gamma_k)h_k + \frac{L\gamma_k^2 D^2}{2}.
\]
By induction with $h_0 = f(x_0) - f^\star$, this telescopes to $h_k \leq \frac{2LD^2}{k+2}$, giving the $O(1/k)$ rate. The key ingredient is the LP oracle bound $\nabla f(x_k)^T z_k \leq \nabla f(x_k)^T x^\star$, which is the defining property of the LP oracle. No projection is required.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Linear convergence for strongly convex objectives]
When $f$ is $\mu$-strongly convex in addition to $L$-smooth, Frank--Wolfe with decaying step sizes achieves a faster rate. Specifically, if the optimal solution $x^\star$ is a vertex of $\mathcal{X}$ (as in our boundary case where $x^\star = (0.5, 0)$ is a vertex), Frank--Wolfe eventually identifies the correct vertex and converges \emph{linearly} at rate $(1 - \mu/L)^k$. This \emph{linear convergence at vertices} is a key theoretical result: the $O(1/k)$ rate applies for interior optima, but for ``vertex-identified'' iterates the rate improves to linear. In our boundary case, once the algorithm identifies the vertex $(0.5, -5)$ as the LP solution for all subsequent iterations, the convergence to $(0.5, 0)$ becomes geometric. The fixed-$\beta$ variant in our implementation prevents this vertex identification in early iterations, explaining the observed slower convergence.
\end{remark}

The \emph{Frank--Wolfe gap} $h_k = \max_{z \in \mathcal{X}} \nabla f(x_k)^T (x_k - z)$ is a primal certificate of optimality: $h_k = 0$ iff $x_k$ is the constrained minimiser. It can be computed for free at each iteration as a byproduct of the LP subproblem.

\subsection{Feasible Set and Linear Programme}

Feasible set: $\mathcal{X} = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^2 : 0.5 \leq x_1 \leq 5,\ -5 \leq x_2 \leq 10\}$, with diameter $D = \sqrt{(5-0.5)^2 + (10-(-5))^2} = \sqrt{20.25 + 225} = \sqrt{245.25} \approx 15.66$.

\textbf{Linear Programme:} For $f(x) = a^T x$ with $a = [1, 2]^T$, the minimum over $\mathcal{X}$ is obtained by independent coordinate-wise optimisation. Since both components of $a$ are positive, the objective decreases as each $x_i$ decreases, so the minimum is at the lower bounds:
\begin{equation}
x^{\star} = (0.5,\, -5), \qquad f^{\star} = 0.5 + 2(-5) = -9.5.
\end{equation}
This illustrates the fundamental theorem of linear programming: for any LP over a bounded polytope, the minimum is attained at a vertex (extreme point). The feasible box has $2^2 = 4$ vertices; $x^\star$ is identified by checking which vertex minimises $a^Tx$.

\begin{remark}[LP duality and sensitivity analysis]
For the LP $\min_{x \in \mathcal{X}} a^Tx$ with box constraints $l \leq x \leq u$ (where $l = (0.5, -5)^T$, $u = (5, 10)^T$, $a = (1, 2)^T$), the dual problem is:
\[
  \max_{\nu_l, \nu_u \geq 0}\; l^T\nu_l - u^T\nu_u \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \nu_u - \nu_l = a.
\]
Since $a = (1,2)^T > 0$, the optimal dual variables are $\nu_u = 0$, $\nu_l = a$, giving dual value $l^T a = 0.5\times1 + (-5)\times2 = -9.5 = f^\star$. Strong LP duality holds (primal = dual), confirming optimality.

\textbf{Sensitivity analysis}: The dual variable $(\nu_l)_i = a_i > 0$ measures the rate of change of the optimal value with respect to the lower bound $l_i$. Specifically, $\partial f^\star/\partial l_i = (\nu_l)_i = a_i$: if we increase $l_1 = 0.5$ to $0.5 + \epsilon$, the optimal value increases by $\epsilon \cdot 1 = \epsilon$. Concretely: $f^\star(l_1 = 0.6) = 0.6 + 2(-5) = -9.4$ vs $f^\star(l_1 = 0.5) = -9.5$: change $= +0.1 = \epsilon \times 1$. ✓

This sensitivity interpretation is the foundation of \emph{shadow prices} in economics (the marginal value of relaxing a constraint) and \emph{parametric LP} (tracing how the optimal solution changes as constraints vary).
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Why LP optima occur at vertices]
\emph{Proof by contradiction.} Suppose $x^\star$ is a minimiser that is not a vertex. Since $\mathcal{X}$ is a polytope, $x^\star$ can be written as a convex combination of two feasible points: $x^\star = \lambda u + (1-\lambda)v$ with $u \neq v$ and $\lambda \in (0,1)$. Then:
\[
  a^T x^\star = \lambda a^T u + (1-\lambda) a^T v.
\]
Since $a^T x^\star = f^\star$ is minimal, we need $a^Tu \geq f^\star$ and $a^Tv \geq f^\star$. But then $a^Tx^\star = \lambda a^Tu + (1-\lambda)a^Tv \geq f^\star$, which requires $a^Tu = a^Tv = f^\star$. So both $u$ and $v$ are also optimal. By induction, we can find a vertex $x^v$ with $a^T x^v = f^\star$. Therefore, every LP has a vertex optimal solution. Computationally, this means we only need to check $2^d$ vertices for a box constraint (trivially), or $\binom{n}{d}$ basic feasible solutions for a general polytope $\{x: Ax \leq b\}$ with $n$ constraints --- motivating the simplex method which moves between adjacent vertices, and Frank--Wolfe which solves the LP subproblem by this argument.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Frank--Wolfe Algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Frank--Wolfe (Conditional Gradient) with Fixed Mixing Parameter}
\label{alg:fw}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial feasible $x_0 \in \mathcal{X}$, mixing parameter $\beta \in (0,1)$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $z_k \gets \arg\min_{z \in \mathcal{X}}\, g_k^T z$ \Comment{Linear minimisation oracle (LP step)}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets \beta\, x_k + (1 - \beta)\, z_k$ \Comment{Fixed-weight convex combination}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{remark}[Implementation note]
The standard Frank--Wolfe algorithm uses decaying step sizes $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$, i.e.\ $x_{k+1} = (1-\gamma_k)x_k + \gamma_k z_k$. Here a fixed mixing parameter $\beta$ (constant $1 - \gamma_k$) is used instead. The fixed-$\beta$ variant does not achieve the $O(1/k)$ rate of Theorem~\ref{thm:fw} but converges to a neighbourhood of the optimum and allows comparison of convergence speed across different values of $\beta$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Away-steps Frank--Wolfe and linear convergence]
The standard Frank--Wolfe algorithm can suffer from slow ``zig-zag'' convergence when the optimal solution $x^\star$ lies in the interior of the current convex combination (the \emph{pyramid phenomenon}). The \emph{away-steps} variant (Lacoste-Julien \& Jaggi, 2015) augments each FW step with an ``away step'' that moves \emph{away} from the worst-performing vertex in the current representation. Specifically, if $x_k = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{S}_k} \alpha^v v$ is a convex combination over active vertices $\mathcal{S}_k$, then the away vertex is $v^{\max} = \arg\max_{v \in \mathcal{S}_k} \nabla f(x_k)^T v$ and the away direction $d_k^{\mathrm{away}} = x_k - v^{\max}$ moves the mass away from $v^{\max}$ toward the other vertices. The algorithm selects whichever of the FW direction $d_k^{\mathrm{FW}} = z_k - x_k$ or the away direction gives more descent. For strongly convex $f$, away-steps FW achieves \emph{linear} convergence (matching projected GD) while using only LP oracle calls, not projections. The \emph{blended pairwise} FW (Tsuji et al., 2022) further improves practical performance by simultaneously moving toward the FW vertex and away from the away vertex in a single step, maintaining a sparse representation throughout.

In our box-constraint setting, $\mathcal{S}_k$ has at most 4 vertices (corners of $\mathcal{X}$), so the away step involves checking 4 candidates --- negligible overhead. The zig-zag behaviour observed in Fig.~\ref{fig:q6_fw_conv} with $\beta = 0.90$ is precisely the pyramid phenomenon that away-steps address: the FW vertex alternates between $(5,10)$ and $(0.5,10)$ because neither is consistently the worst vertex, but the away step would eliminate the worst one faster.
\end{remark}

For box constraints, the LP subproblem decomposes per coordinate with $O(d)$ operations:
\begin{equation}
(z_k)_i = \begin{cases} l_i & \text{if } (\nabla f(x_k))_i > 0 \\[2pt] u_i & \text{if } (\nabla f(x_k))_i \leq 0 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
In contrast, projecting onto a general convex set can require solving a QP. For structured sets like the simplex, spectrahedron, or nuclear norm ball, the LP oracle is often available in closed form at $O(d\log d)$ or $O(d^2)$ cost, while projection is much more expensive.

\begin{remark}[Frank--Wolfe gap as a duality gap and primal certificate]
The Frank--Wolfe gap $h_k = \max_{z \in \mathcal{X}} \nabla f(x_k)^T(x_k - z)$ (equivalently, $h_k = \nabla f(x_k)^T x_k - \min_{z \in \mathcal{X}} \nabla f(x_k)^T z$) has a deep interpretation as a \emph{duality gap} for the constrained problem. For convex $f$ and compact $\mathcal{X}$:
\[
  f(x_k) - f^\star \leq h_k = \nabla f(x_k)^T(x_k - z_k),
\]
where $z_k$ is the LP oracle solution. This upper bound on the primal gap is computable for free at each FW iteration (the LP subproblem value is $\min_{z\in\mathcal{X}}\nabla f(x_k)^Tz = \nabla f(x_k)^Tz_k$, already computed). Moreover, $h_k = 0$ if and only if $x_k$ is the constrained minimiser (by the first-order optimality condition for convex $f$).

Proof of the bound: by convexity of $f$, $f(z) \geq f(x_k) + \nabla f(x_k)^T(z - x_k)$ for all $z$. Setting $z = x^\star$: $f^\star \geq f(x_k) - \nabla f(x_k)^T(x_k - x^\star) \geq f(x_k) - \max_{z \in \mathcal{X}}\nabla f(x_k)^T(x_k - z) = f(x_k) - h_k$.

\textbf{Connection to LP duality}: For a linear objective $f(x) = c^Tx$, the LP dual of $\min_{x \in \mathcal{X}} c^Tx$ (with $\mathcal{X} = \{x: Ax \leq b, x \geq 0\}$) is $\max_{y \geq 0} b^Ty$ s.t.\ $A^Ty \leq c$. The FW gap at a primal feasible point $x_k$ with dual feasible $y_k$ equals $c^Tx_k - b^Ty_k$ (the LP duality gap), which is zero at optimality by LP strong duality (complementary slackness). The FW gap generalises this LP duality gap to non-linear smooth objectives.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Sparsity of Frank--Wolfe iterates and structured optimisation]
A key property of Frank--Wolfe is that iterates maintain a \emph{sparse representation} over vertices. If $x_0$ is a vertex and the algorithm uses decaying step sizes $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$, then after $k$ steps:
\[
  x_k = \sum_{i=0}^{k} \alpha_i z_i, \quad \alpha_i \geq 0, \quad \sum \alpha_i = 1,
\]
i.e.\ $x_k$ is a convex combination of at most $k+1$ vertices of $\mathcal{X}$. This sparsity is beneficial when $\mathcal{X}$ is a high-dimensional structured set:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Simplex}: $\mathcal{X} = \Delta_n = \{x \geq 0, \mathbf{1}^Tx = 1\}$. LP oracle selects the $i^\star = \arg\min_i(\nabla f)_i$ coordinate. FW iterates are sparse probability vectors (at most $k+1$ nonzero entries), which is desirable for sparse learning problems.
  \item \textbf{Nuclear norm ball}: $\mathcal{X} = \{X: \|X\|_* \leq 1\}$ (matrix with bounded nuclear norm). LP oracle selects $-\sigma_{\max}uv^T$ (top singular vectors). FW iterates are sums of rank-1 matrices, building low-rank approximations. This is the basis for online matrix completion algorithms.
  \item \textbf{$\ell_1$ ball}: $\mathcal{X} = \{x: \|x\|_1 \leq r\}$. LP oracle selects a coordinate vector $\pm re_{i^\star}$. FW naturally produces sparse solutions without explicit $\ell_1$ regularisation.
\end{itemize}
For our box constraint, all four vertices are ``visited'' in the first few iterations, so there is no sparsity benefit --- but the LP oracle cost advantage ($O(d)$ vs $O(d^2)$ for projection onto a general convex set) is the primary motivation.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Interior vs Boundary Behaviour}

\begin{remark}[Zig-zag mechanism: gradient sign changes drive vertex alternation]
The zig-zag pattern in Frank--Wolfe arises because for interior optima, the gradient $\nabla f(x_k)$ changes sign in one or more coordinates as the iterate passes through the optimum. For the interior case $f(x) = (x_1-1)^2 + (x_2-5)^2$ with $x^\star = (1,5)$: when $x_k^{(1)} < 1$ (left of optimum), $(\nabla f)_1 = 2(x_1-1) < 0$, so the LP oracle selects the upper bound $z^{(1)} = 5$; after the step, if $x_{k+1}^{(1)} > 1$ (now right of optimum), $(\nabla f)_1 > 0$, and the oracle selects the lower bound $z^{(1)} = 0.5$. This alternation between extremes persists as long as the iterate oscillates around the optimum.

The severity of the zig-zag depends on the step size $1-\beta$: larger steps cause larger overshoots, inducing more extreme oscillations and ultimately a larger converged neighbourhood. Smaller steps (larger $\beta$) damp the oscillations at the cost of slower approach. The decaying schedule $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2) \to 0$ resolves this by shrinking steps to zero, allowing the iterate to settle at the optimum without oscillation --- this is why decaying Frank--Wolfe achieves the $O(1/k)$ rate, while fixed-$\beta$ converges only to a neighbourhood.
\end{remark}

\textbf{Interior optimum:} Frank--Wolfe exhibits the characteristic zig-zag convergence where $z_k$ oscillates between opposite vertices of $\mathcal{X}$. The $O(1/k)$ convergence rate (with decaying step sizes, Theorem~\ref{thm:fw}) is slower than projected gradient descent's linear rate. In practice for interior optima, projected gradient descent is preferred when projection is cheap.

\textbf{Boundary optimum:} Frank--Wolfe is particularly well suited to boundary optima because the LP oracle naturally selects the vertex closest to the direction of the constrained optimum, guiding iterates toward the boundary. For constraint sets where boundary optima are common --- $\ell_1$ balls promoting sparsity, trace-norm balls promoting low rank, or flow polytopes in network optimisation --- Frank--Wolfe is preferred because: (1) the LP oracle is cheap and exploits problem structure; (2) the boundary-vertex attraction provides implicit regularisation (sparse solutions emerge naturally); and (3) no projection is required.

\textbf{Frank--Wolfe gap as a stopping criterion:} The Frank--Wolfe gap $h_k = g_k^T (x_k - z_k)$ is computed for free at each iteration and provides a valid upper bound on the primal gap: $f(x_k) - f^\star \leq h_k$. This gives a practical, anytime stopping criterion --- halt when $h_k \leq \epsilon$ for desired accuracy $\epsilon$. In contrast, projected gradient descent requires an estimate of $f^\star$ or a separate dual certificate for such a guarantee.

\begin{remark}[Frank--Wolfe gap: worked example]
For the interior case $f(x) = (x_1-1)^2 + (x_2-5)^2$ on $\mathcal{X} = [0.5,5]\times[-5,10]$, at $x_0 = (1, 1)$: $\nabla f(x_0) = (0, -8)$. The LP oracle selects $z_0 = (5, 10)$ (upper bounds, since both gradient components are $\leq 0$ for $x_2$). The FW gap is:
\[
  h_0 = \nabla f(x_0)^T(x_0 - z_0) = (0)(1-5) + (-8)(1-10) = 0 + 72 = 72.
\]
This tells us $f(x_0) - f^\star \leq 72$. Indeed $f(x_0) = 0 + 16 = 16$ and $f^\star = 0$, so the true gap is 16 and the FW bound (72) is valid but not tight. After many iterations as $x_k \to (1, 5)$: $\nabla f(x_k) \to 0$, so $h_k \to 0$ --- the gap tightens as we approach the optimum. The gap is tight (equals the true gap) when $x_k = x^\star$; it is loose at early iterations when the gradient is large and the LP oracle selects a far vertex. For the boundary case $f(x) = x_1^2 + x_2^2$ with $x^\star = (0.5, 0)$: the FW gap at convergence measures how far the last LP solution is from the current iterate along the gradient direction, providing a certificate that the boundary minimum has been found.
\end{remark}

\textbf{Practical recommendation:} The decaying step-size schedule $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$ should be preferred over fixed $\beta$ for convergence guarantees. In our boundary case, the fixed $\beta = 0.93$ leaves a gap $f = 0.364$ vs $f^\star = 0.25$ that would be closed by allowing smaller steps in later iterations.

\begin{remark}[Frank--Wolfe vs projected gradient: oracle complexity comparison]
For a box-constrained problem as in Q6, both Frank--Wolfe and projected gradient descent achieve linear convergence to $f^\star$ for strongly convex $f$ (PGD) and $O(1/k)$ for only convex $f$ (FW). However, the \emph{per-iteration cost} differs:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Projected GD}: LP step costs $O(d)$; projection onto box also costs $O(d)$ (per-coordinate clipping). Both are equally cheap for box constraints.
  \item \textbf{Frank--Wolfe}: LP step costs $O(d)$; no projection needed. But for a nuclear norm ball $\{X : \|X\|_* \leq 1\}$ (rank-$r$ matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{p\times q}$), the LP oracle (computing the top singular vectors of $\nabla f$) costs $O(pqr)$ via truncated SVD vs projection (which requires a full SVD at $O(pq\min(p,q))$). This makes FW $O(\min(p,q)/r)$ times cheaper per iteration for low-rank matrices --- a critical advantage in collaborative filtering.
\end{itemize}
For our box constraint, both methods are equally cheap per iteration, and PGD's superior convergence rate (linear vs $O(1/k)$) makes it preferable. Frank--Wolfe's advantage emerges only for structured constraint sets where the LP oracle is substantially cheaper than projection.
\end{remark}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

This report has investigated a broad spectrum of optimisation algorithms across three structurally distinct benchmark functions. The key findings are synthesised below.

\begin{remark}[Stochastic gradient descent for non-convex deep learning: from theory to practice]
The theoretical results derived in this report apply to convex or locally strongly convex objectives. Modern deep learning involves objectives that are:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Massively overparameterised}: $d \sim 10^{11}$ (GPT-4) with $m \sim 10^{13}$ training tokens.
  \item \textbf{Non-convex with exponentially many saddle points}: But empirically, SGD avoids all saddle points and converges to ``good'' minima.
  \item \textbf{Composed}: $f(x) = \ell(\phi_L(\cdots\phi_1(x)\cdots))$ with $L$ layers, backpropagated via chain rule at $O(Ld)$ cost per sample.
\end{itemize}
The practical Adam hyperparameters $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$, $\varepsilon = 10^{-8}$, $\alpha = 3\times10^{-4}$ (from Kingma \& Ba) emerged empirically and remain standard despite years of tuning and theoretical scrutiny. The choice $\beta_2 = 0.999$ (long memory for variance estimation) vs $\beta_1 = 0.9$ (shorter memory for gradient direction) balances stable direction estimation with responsive second-moment estimates. Warm-up schedules (linearly increasing $\alpha$ from 0 to the target over $\approx 4\%$ of training) prevent large initial steps when the bias correction $(1-\beta_2^t)^{-1}$ is still large.

\textbf{Connection to our experiments}: Our Benchmark A result with Adam ($f = 0.4826$, converged in $\approx 30$ iterations) is consistent with Adam's efficiency on quadratic-like objectives. The Benchmark C result ($f = 1.578$ vs Nesterov's $0.479$) reflects Adam's inability to exploit the consistent gradient direction along the Rosenbrock valley --- the $x_2$ and $x_1$ gradients have very different scales during valley traversal, and Adam's per-coordinate normalisation ``flattens'' this difference, losing the consistent directional signal that Nesterov's global momentum preserves.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Optimality of Nesterov's rate]
Nesterov (1983) proved that the $O(1/k^2)$ rate is \emph{information-theoretically optimal} for first-order methods on smooth convex functions: no algorithm using only gradient evaluations can guarantee better than $O(1/k^2)$ convergence for general $L$-smooth convex functions. This means Nesterov's accelerated gradient method simultaneously achieves:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item The optimal convergence rate among first-order methods for smooth convex functions ($O(1/k^2)$).
  \item The optimal rate among first-order methods for smooth strongly convex functions ($O((1-1/\sqrt\kappa)^k)$), matching the Chebyshev polynomial lower bound.
\end{enumerate}
In contrast, gradient descent achieves only $O(1/k)$ (suboptimal for convex) and $O((1-1/\kappa)^k)$ (suboptimal for strongly convex). The gap between GD and Nesterov is not a limitation of the specific implementation but reflects a fundamental information-theoretic lower bound: Nesterov's method cannot be improved upon within the first-order oracle model, without additional structural assumptions.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Sharp convergence inequalities connecting theory to experiments]
The theoretical bounds in Theorem~\ref{thm:gd} and Theorem~\ref{thm:nesterov} can be checked against observations:

\textbf{GD on Benchmark A ($\kappa \approx 1$, $\alpha = 0.08$, 80 iters):} Theory: $f(x_{80}) - f^\star \leq (1-0.08)^{80}(f(x_0) - f^\star) = 0.92^{80} \times (f(x_0) - 0.4826)$. With $f(x_0) = J(0,0) = \frac{1}{2}(0^T H 0) - 0 + c = c \approx 8.52$ (sum of $y^2/2m$), we get $f(x_0) - f^\star \approx 8.52 - 0.4826 = 8.04$ and predicted $f(x_{80}) \leq 0.4826 + 0.92^{80} \times 8.04 \approx 0.4826 + 0.00136 \times 8.04 \approx 0.4935$. Observed: $0.4826$. The bound is off by $\approx 0.01$ above (GD converges faster than the bound predicts because $\kappa \approx 1$ means the exact rate $(1-2/(\kappa+1))^2 = (1-1)^2 = 0$, i.e.\ near one-step convergence).

\textbf{Nesterov on Benchmark C ($\kappa \approx 2504$, $\alpha = 0.0007$, 150 iters):} Theory: $f(x_{150}) - f^\star \leq 2L\|x_0-x^\star\|^2/(150+1)^2 = 2\times1001.6\times((-1-1)^2+(1-1)^2)/(151)^2 = 2\times1001.6\times4/22801 \approx 8003/22801 \approx 0.35$. Observed: $f = 0.479$, which exceeds the bound prediction of $0.35 + f^\star = 0.35$. The discrepancy: the bound uses $\alpha = 1/L = 1/1001.6$ (the theoretically optimal step), but our experiment uses $\alpha = 0.0007 < 1/L$, a smaller step that slows convergence. The ratio $\alpha_{\mathrm{theory}} / \alpha_{\mathrm{expt}} = 1/(1001.6 \times 0.0007) \approx 1.43$, giving an approximate effective convergence factor $\approx 1.43\times$ slower, consistent with the observed $0.479$ vs predicted $0.35$.
\end{remark}

\textbf{Benchmark A (quadratic, $\kappa \approx 1$):} All gradient methods converge to the same floor $J^\star \approx 0.4826$; Newton reaches it in one step. The near-unit condition number makes this benchmark easy for all first-order methods.

\textbf{Benchmark B (mildly ill-conditioned, $\kappa \approx 10$):} Momentum methods (Nesterov, Adam, Heavy Ball) offer modest but noticeable advantages over GD. Newton's curvature information provides the most efficient path in only 20 iterations. The moderate anisotropy distinguishes methods that adapt to the coordinate-wise curvature (Adam, Adagrad) from those that treat all directions equally (GD).

\textbf{Benchmark C (Rosenbrock, $\kappa \approx 2504$):} This is the decisive benchmark. Newton's method dramatically outperforms all first-order methods on a per-iteration basis. Among first-order methods: Polyak (with correct $f^\star = 0$) converges best ($f = 0.009$), followed by Nesterov ($f = 0.479$). GD barely makes progress ($f = 3.39$) due to the stringent stability constraint $\alpha < 2/L \approx 0.002$. This confirms the theoretical prediction: for large $\kappa$, first-order methods converge as $(1-2/(\kappa+1))^k \approx e^{-2k/\kappa}$, requiring $O(\kappa)$ iterations, while Newton is insensitive to $\kappa$ near the optimum.

\textbf{Adaptive step sizes:} The Polyak step size is theoretically optimal when $f^\star$ is known, but practically fragile: misspecification of $f^\star$ leads to divergence (Benchmarks A and B). Adagrad and RMSprop are more robust for practical applications. Adagrad's monotone decay suits sparse gradient settings (e.g.\ NLP); RMSprop's exponential forgetting is better for stationary objectives where long-term gradient history is misleading.

\textbf{Theoretical vs.\ practical advantage of Nesterov over Adam:} On our benchmarks, Nesterov outperforms Adam on Benchmark C ($f = 0.479$ vs $1.578$). However, in large-scale deep learning, Adam is overwhelmingly preferred despite Nesterov's stronger theoretical guarantee. This is because: (1)~real neural networks have non-convex, non-smooth objectives where Nesterov's convergence theory does not apply; (2)~Adam's per-coordinate scaling implicitly handles the extreme gradient scale variation across layers (weight matrices vs.\ bias vectors); (3)~Adam's bias correction prevents the large initial step that Nesterov's rising momentum can produce in early iterations. These results serve as a reminder that theoretical rates on convex benchmarks do not always predict practical performance in non-convex settings.

A key practical reason for Adam's dominance: in a neural network with $L$ layers, the effective Lipschitz constant of gradients in layer $\ell$ scales as $\prod_{l \neq \ell} \|W_l\|_{\mathrm{op}}$ (product of weight norms from all other layers). This means layers deep in the network can have gradients $10^3$--$10^6\times$ larger than shallow layers. Adam's per-coordinate normalisation effectively standardises gradient magnitudes across layers, providing an implicit layer-wise learning rate --- something Nesterov's isotropic momentum cannot do without explicit per-layer hyperparameter tuning.

\textbf{Second-order methods:} Newton's method provides the best convergence on all benchmarks when appropriately damped, achieving accuracy in 20 iterations that first-order methods fail to reach in 150. For small-to-medium scale problems where Hessian computation is feasible, Newton or quasi-Newton (L-BFGS) methods should be the first choice.

\textbf{Derivative-free methods:} Finite differences with small $\delta$ closely match gradient descent performance at $d+1$ function evaluations per step. However, the optimal $\delta^\star = (4\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}/M)^{1/2} \approx 10^{-8}$ (forward FD) or $(3\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}/M_3)^{1/3} \approx 10^{-5}$ (centered FD) is far smaller than our experimental $\delta = 0.05$, which was chosen for visibility rather than optimality. In practice, with $\delta$ near machine-epsilon optimal, FD GD is nearly indistinguishable from exact GD. Nelder--Mead and random search are valuable when analytical gradients are unavailable, at the cost of slower convergence and limited scalability to high dimensions.

\textbf{Constrained optimisation:} Projected gradient descent provides hard constraint satisfaction from the first iteration, at the cost of a projection oracle. Penalty methods are simpler to implement but require careful tuning of $\lambda$: too small yields residual infeasibility; too large creates a poorly conditioned landscape requiring smaller step sizes.

\textbf{Frank--Wolfe:} The projection-free nature of Frank--Wolfe makes it attractive for structured constraint sets (nuclear norm, simplex, flow polytopes) where LP oracles are cheaper than projections. The characteristic $O(1/k)$ rate is sufficient for many practical applications, and the LP vertices naturally attract iterates towards sparse solutions.

\begin{remark}[Non-convex landscape theory: saddle points and loss surfaces]
All three benchmarks are either globally convex (A) or have a single global minimum with no spurious local minima (B, C). Modern deep learning, by contrast, involves non-convex objectives with complex landscapes. The theoretical understanding of deep learning optimisation has advanced significantly:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Saddle point proliferation}: For random matrix models of neural networks, Dauphin et al.\ (2014) showed that local minima are rare at low loss values, and most critical points at high loss are saddle points. The presence of a negative curvature direction allows gradient-based methods (and their noisy SGD variants) to escape saddle points.
  \item \textbf{Strict saddle property}: A function satisfies the \emph{strict saddle property} if every saddle point has at least one direction of strict negative curvature. Under this property, gradient descent with small random perturbations converges to local minima (Jin et al., 2017). Many practically relevant objectives (matrix factorisation, phase retrieval, dictionary learning) satisfy this property.
  \item \textbf{Global convergence via overparameterisation}: For sufficiently wide neural networks (width $\geq$ polynomial in training samples and depth), the training loss landscape has been shown to be ``effectively convex'' near initialisation (Jacot et al., 2018 --- Neural Tangent Kernel theory), with every local minimum being global. This provides a theoretical foundation for why SGD successfully trains deep networks despite non-convexity.
\end{enumerate}
Our Rosenbrock experiment ($\kappa \approx 2504$, unique global minimum) serves as a useful proxy: it captures the difficulty of non-convex optimisation (slow first-order convergence, need for curvature information) without the additional complication of spurious local minima, allowing clean isolation of algorithmic effects.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Implicit regularisation: what SGD finds]
Beyond convergence speed, the \emph{solution found} by an algorithm can matter as much as whether it converges. For overparameterised problems where many global minima exist (e.g.\ any solution in the interpolation regime), different algorithms converge to different solutions:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Gradient descent} with small step size converges to the minimum of $\|x\|^2$ subject to $f(x) = 0$ for linear models (implicit $\ell_2$ regularisation / minimum norm solution).
  \item \textbf{SGD} with small batches introduces noise that can drive iterates toward ``flat'' minima (regions where the Hessian has small eigenvalues), which empirically generalise better.
  \item \textbf{Newton's method} converges to the nearest local minimum from the starting point, regardless of its geometry.
  \item \textbf{Mirror descent} with a non-Euclidean geometry (e.g.\ KL divergence) converges to the minimum $\ell_1$ norm solution, promoting sparsity.
\end{itemize}
This implicit regularisation phenomenon means the optimisation algorithm itself functions as a regulariser, independently of any explicit regularisation term in the objective. For our deterministic small-scale benchmarks this effect is absent (there is a unique minimum), but it is crucial for understanding why SGD-trained neural networks generalise despite being run until training loss is zero.
\end{remark}

\textbf{Why these benchmark functions?} The three benchmarks are not arbitrary: they are canonical functions specifically designed to expose distinct algorithmic weaknesses. Benchmark A (well-conditioned quadratic) serves as a sanity check where all correct algorithms converge. Benchmark B (mildly anisotropic with non-convex perturbation) tests ability to handle coordinate-wise curvature differences without being trapped by local non-convexity. Benchmark C (Rosenbrock) is the ``standard'' hard test for unconstrained optimisation: its narrow curved valley with $\kappa \approx 2504$ exposes gradient descent's dependence on $O(\kappa)$ iterations, and its non-convexity tests robustness of second-order methods outside the local region. These three functions collectively discriminate between all the algorithmic families studied, with each benchmark revealing a different facet of algorithm performance.

\begin{remark}[Problem structure as an algorithmic resource]
A unifying theme across all six questions is that \emph{problem structure} is the key resource that algorithms exploit for faster convergence, cheaper iterations, or better-adapted updates. The structural assumptions and their algorithmic consequences are:

\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule

The art of practical optimisation lies in identifying what structures are present in the problem and choosing the algorithm that exploits them most effectively. For data science practitioners, this means: (1) checking if the objective is quadratic (prefer Newton); (2) estimating the condition number (prefer Nesterov/L-BFGS for large $\kappa$); (3) identifying constraint structure (prefer FW for LP-solvable sets, PGD for simple projection sets); (4) assessing gradient availability (prefer gradient-based if auto-diff is available, FD otherwise); (5) considering whether stochastic gradients are cheaper and their variance (prefer Adam/RMSprop for large datasets with noise, deterministic methods for small problems).
\end{remark}

\subsection{The Role of Condition Number Across Algorithms}

The condition number $\kappa = L/\mu$ is the single most important parameter determining first-order optimisation difficulty. Table~\ref{tab:rates} shows how dramatically convergence rates depend on $\kappa$:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{GD}: rate $(1-1/\kappa)^k$. For $\kappa = 2504$: $(1 - 0.0004)^k \approx e^{-k/2504}$. To reach $10^{-6}$ accuracy: $k \approx 2504 \times \ln(10^6) \approx 34{,}600$ iterations.
  \item \textbf{Nesterov}: rate $(1-1/\sqrt{\kappa})^k$. For $\kappa = 2504$: $(1-0.02)^k$. To reach $10^{-6}$: $k \approx \sqrt{2504} \times \ln(10^6) \approx 690$ iterations.
  \item \textbf{Newton (local)}: double-exponential. To reach $10^{-6}$ from $\|x_0-x^\star\| = 0.1$: about 4 iterations (each halves the exponent).
\end{itemize}
The $\kappa$-dependence hierarchy GD $\gg$ Nesterov $\gg$ Newton (in terms of iterations to fixed accuracy) explains the observed ordering in all our Benchmark C experiments and is the deepest theoretical result in first-order optimisation.

For small-to-medium scientific computing problems where $d \leq 10^4$ and function evaluations are expensive, second-order methods are preferred.

\subsection{Practical Recommendations}

For data science practitioners:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item For well-conditioned, large-scale problems: \textbf{Adam} or \textbf{RMSprop} offer the best robustness without requiring $f^\star$ or second-order information.
\item For small-scale, well-conditioned problems with known Hessian: \textbf{Newton's method} in 5--20 iterations.
\item For well-conditioned problems with known optimal value: \textbf{Polyak step size} achieves optimal convergence.
\item For highly ill-conditioned problems ($\kappa \gg 1$): \textbf{Nesterov momentum} provides the best first-order guarantee; \textbf{Newton} if second-order information is accessible.
\item For black-box objectives: \textbf{Nelder--Mead} (low dimension), \textbf{Nesterov random search} (high dimension, parallel budget).
\item For linearly constrained problems: \textbf{Frank--Wolfe} when the LP oracle is cheap; \textbf{projected gradient descent} otherwise.
\end{itemize}

\begin{remark}[Open problems and frontiers in optimisation theory]
Despite the maturity of convex optimisation theory, several important questions remain open:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Adam's convergence}: While AMSGrad provably converges, the original Adam algorithm's convergence on non-convex objectives (the practically relevant case) is not fully characterised. Empirical evidence suggests Adam converges on smooth objectives, but tight non-convex convergence rates for Adam remain an active research area (Zhang et al., 2022: Adam achieves $O(1/\sqrt{T})$ rate for non-convex objectives under bounded gradient variance).
  \item \textbf{Optimal first-order rate for non-convex objectives}: For $L$-smooth non-convex functions, the best known rate for reaching an $\epsilon$-stationary point ($\|\nabla f(x)\| \leq \epsilon$) is $O(1/\epsilon^2)$ gradient evaluations, achieved by GD with appropriate step. Whether this is optimal or whether acceleration is possible for non-convex problems is open. Carmon et al.\ (2020) proved $\Omega(1/\epsilon^{12/7})$ lower bounds for specific classes, suggesting the $O(1/\epsilon^2)$ rate may not be tight.
  \item \textbf{Sharp oracle complexity for zeroth-order optimisation}: The gap between the $\Omega(d/\epsilon)$ lower bound and Nesterov random search's $O(d^2/\epsilon)$ rate for smooth convex functions suggests room for improvement. Whether an $O(d/\epsilon)$-optimal zeroth-order algorithm exists for all smooth convex functions (not just specific geometries) remains open.
  \item \textbf{Frank--Wolfe: away-steps optimal rate}: The away-steps variant achieves linear convergence for strongly convex objectives at vertices, but the exact constant is not tight. Whether the blended pairwise FW or other variants can match projected gradient descent's rate while using only LP oracles is an open question.
  \item \textbf{Practical acceleration in non-convex settings}: Nesterov's $O(1/k^2)$ rate relies critically on convexity. The ``online-to-batch conversion'' and ``parameter-free'' algorithms represent attempts to achieve acceleration-like behaviour without convexity, but a clean analogue of the $O(1/k^2)$ rate for non-convex problems has not been established.
\end{enumerate}
These open questions represent active research fronts at the intersection of theoretical computer science, mathematics, and machine learning --- motivated by practical systems of enormous scale.
\end{remark}

def gradient_descent(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        x = x - alpha * grad_fn(x)
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Polyak Step Size}]
def polyak_step(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, f_star, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        alpha_k = (loss_fn(x) - f_star) / (np.dot(g, g) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(alpha_k)
        x = x - alpha_k * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Adagrad}]
def adagrad(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha0, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    G = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        G += g**2
        eff_alpha = alpha0 / (np.sqrt(G) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(np.mean(eff_alpha))
        x = x - eff_alpha * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={RMSprop}]
def rmsprop(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha0, beta, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    v = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        v = beta * v + (1 - beta) * g**2
        eff_alpha = alpha0 / (np.sqrt(v) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(np.mean(eff_alpha))
        x = x - eff_alpha * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Heavy Ball / Polyak Momentum}]
def heavy_ball(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    z = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        z = beta * z + alpha * g
        x = x - z
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Nesterov Momentum}]
def nesterov_momentum(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta_max, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    z = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for k in range(1, n_iters + 1):
        beta_k = min((k - 1) / (k + 2), beta_max)
        lookahead = x + beta_k * z
        g = grad_fn(lookahead)
        z = beta_k * z - alpha * g
        x = x + z
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Adam Optimiser}]
def adam_optimiser(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta1, beta2, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    m_vec, v_vec = np.zeros_like(x), np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for t in range(1, n_iters + 1):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        m_vec = beta1 * m_vec + (1 - beta1) * g
        v_vec = beta2 * v_vec + (1 - beta2) * g**2
        m_hat = m_vec / (1 - beta1**t)
        v_hat = v_vec / (1 - beta2**t)
        x = x - alpha * m_hat / (np.sqrt(v_hat) + eps)
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Damped Newton's Method}]
def newtons_method(grad_fn, hess_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, n_iters, damping=1e-8):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, update_norms = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        H = hess_fn(x) if callable(hess_fn) else hess_fn
        H_reg = H + damping * np.eye(len(x))
        try:
            p = np.linalg.solve(H_reg, g)
        except np.linalg.LinAlgError:
            p = g
        update_norms.append(np.linalg.norm(alpha * p))
        x = x - alpha * p
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(update_norms)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Nelder--Mead Simplex}]
def nelder_mead(loss_fn, x0, step, n_iters, alpha_r=1.0, gamma=2.0, rho=0.5, sigma=0.5):
    n = len(x0)
    simplex = np.zeros((n + 1, n))
    simplex[0] = x0.copy()
    for i in range(n):
        simplex[i + 1] = x0.copy()
        simplex[i + 1][i] += step
    f_vals = np.array([loss_fn(v) for v in simplex])
    centroid_hist = [np.mean(simplex, axis=0).copy()]
    f_best_hist = [np.min(f_vals)]
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        order = np.argsort(f_vals)
        simplex = simplex[order]; f_vals = f_vals[order]
        centroid = np.mean(simplex[:-1], axis=0)
        x_r = centroid + alpha_r * (centroid - simplex[-1])
        f_r = loss_fn(x_r)
        if f_vals[0] <= f_r < f_vals[-2]:
            simplex[-1] = x_r; f_vals[-1] = f_r
        elif f_r < f_vals[0]:
            x_e = centroid + gamma * (centroid - simplex[-1])
            f_e = loss_fn(x_e)
            if f_e < f_r: simplex[-1] = x_e; f_vals[-1] = f_e
            else: simplex[-1] = x_r; f_vals[-1] = f_r
        else:
            if f_r < f_vals[-1]:
                x_c = centroid + rho * (x_r - centroid)
                f_c = loss_fn(x_c)
                if f_c <= f_r: simplex[-1] = x_c; f_vals[-1] = f_c
                else:
                    for i in range(1, n+1):
                        simplex[i] = simplex[0]+sigma*(simplex[i]-simplex[0])
                        f_vals[i] = loss_fn(simplex[i])
            else:
                x_c = centroid + rho * (simplex[-1] - centroid)
                f_c = loss_fn(x_c)
                if f_c < f_vals[-1]: simplex[-1] = x_c; f_vals[-1] = f_c
                else:
                    for i in range(1, n+1):
                        simplex[i] = simplex[0]+sigma*(simplex[i]-simplex[0])
                        f_vals[i] = loss_fn(simplex[i])
        centroid_hist.append(np.mean(simplex, axis=0).copy())
        f_best_hist.append(np.min(f_vals))
    return np.array(centroid_hist), np.array(f_best_hist), simplex
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Frank--Wolfe Algorithm}]
def frank_wolfe(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, bounds, beta, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, z_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        z = np.array([bounds[i][0] if g[i] > 0 else bounds[i][1]
                       for i in range(len(g))])
        z_hist.append(z.copy())
        x = beta * x + (1 - beta) * z
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(z_hist)
\end{lstlisting}
