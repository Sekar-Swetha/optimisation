\begin{abstract}
This report presents a comprehensive theoretical and empirical study of optimisation algorithms
across six method families, evaluated on three benchmark problems of increasing difficulty: a
linear-regression quadratic loss ($\kappa \approx 1.3$, Benchmark A), a sinusoidally perturbed
quadratic ($\kappa \approx 5$, Benchmark B), and the ill-conditioned Rosenbrock function
($\kappa \approx 500$, Benchmark C).  The methods investigated span: (Q1) adaptive step-size
methods --- Polyak, Adagrad, RMSprop, and Heavy Ball --- with formal convergence guarantees
and AMSGrad as a convergence-corrected variant; (Q2) accelerated and stochastic methods ---
Nesterov momentum (optimal $O(1/k^2)$), Adam, and mini-batch SGD with variance-reduction
extensions; (Q3) Newton's method with Hessian regularisation and L-BFGS as a quasi-Newton
alternative; (Q4) derivative-free approaches including forward/central finite differences,
Nesterov random search ($O(d/k)$ convergence), Nelder--Mead simplex, and grid search; (Q5)
constrained optimisation via projected gradient descent, quadratic penalty methods, and
augmented Lagrangian; and (Q6) the Frank--Wolfe (conditional gradient) algorithm with formal
duality gap certificate.
Key theoretical contributions include: (i) an irreducible SGD noise floor derivation ($J^{\star}
\approx \sigma^2(m-d)/(2m)$) quantitatively explaining the observed 36-fold increase under
noise $\sigma = 6$; (ii) a proof that the Frank--Wolfe duality gap is an efficiently computable
certificate of sub-optimality; (iii) quantitative convergence rate predictions (Nesterov
achieves $\sim 48\times$ fewer iterations than GD on $\kappa = 500$); (iv) formal Augmented
Lagrangian convergence guarantees showing why large-$\lambda$ penalty methods suffer
$O(\lambda)$ ill-conditioning; (v) a proximal gradient / ISTA interpretation of PGD; (vi)
Nesterov's first-order oracle lower bound proving $O(1/k^2)$ is information-theoretically
optimal; (vii) a mirror-descent unification showing PGD, Frank--Wolfe, and exponentiated
gradient as special cases of Bregman proximal descent; (viii) Adam as a diagonal approximation
of natural gradient (Fisher information matrix); and (ix) a continuous-time ODE interpretation
of Heavy Ball (damped harmonic oscillator) and Nesterov (time-varying $3/t$ damping).
Key empirical findings include: Polyak step diverges when $f^{\star}$ is misspecified; Newton
converges in one iteration on the quadratic benchmark (condition-number-independent); FD
gradient quality depends critically on $\delta$ (optimal $\delta \approx \sqrt{\varepsilon_{\rm
mach}}$ for forward FD); and the Frank--Wolfe duality gap decays at the $O(1/k)$ rate.
All experiments use a fixed random seed (\texttt{np.random.seed(42)}) for reproducibility.
\end{abstract}

\subsection{Benchmark A: Linear Regression Quadratic Loss}
A synthetic dataset with $m = 1000$ samples and two parameters is generated:
\begin{equation}
\theta^{\star} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
x^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_2), \quad
y^{(i)} = {\theta^{\star}}^T x^{(i)} + \varepsilon^{(i)}, \quad
\varepsilon^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1).
\end{equation}
The loss function is the mean squared error:
\begin{equation}
J(\theta) = \frac{1}{2m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \bigl(\theta^T x^{(i)} - y^{(i)}\bigr)^2
           = \frac{1}{2m}\|X\theta - y\|^2.
\label{eq:lossA}
\end{equation}
This is a convex quadratic with Hessian $H = \frac{1}{m}X^T X$ (constant and positive
semi-definite).  The gradient is $\nabla J(\theta) = \frac{1}{m}X^T(X\theta - y)$.  Due to
irreducible observation noise, the minimum loss is $J^{\star} \approx 0.4826 > 0$; the
closed-form minimiser is the ordinary least-squares estimator
$\theta_{\mathrm{OLS}} = (X^TX)^{-1}X^Ty \approx (3.028,\,4.017)$.  The condition number
of $H$ is $\kappa(H) \approx 1.3$, so Benchmark A is well-conditioned.
Starting point: $\theta_0 = [0, 0]^T$.

\subsection{Benchmark B: Toy Neural Network Loss}
\begin{equation}
f(x_1, x_2) = (x_1 - 1)^2 + 5(x_2 - 2)^2 + \sin(x_1).
\label{eq:lossB}
\end{equation}
The gradient and Hessian are
\begin{align}
\nabla f(x) &= \bigl[2(x_1 - 1) + \cos(x_1),\; 10(x_2 - 2)\bigr]^T, \\
\nabla^2 f(x) &= \operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(2 - \sin(x_1),\; 10\bigr).
\end{align}
The Hessian eigenvalue ratio varies between approximately $1.8:10$ (when $\sin x_1 = -1$)
and $3:10$ (when $\sin x_1 = 1$), creating moderate anisotropy with condition number
$\kappa \in [3.3, 5.6]$.  The unique minimum is at approximately
$(x_1^{\star}, x_2^{\star}) \approx (0.582, 2.000)$ with $f^{\star} \approx 0.7244$.
Starting point: $x_0 = [-1, 4]^T$.

\subsection{Benchmark C: Rosenbrock Function}
\begin{equation}
f(x_1, x_2) = (1 - x_1)^2 + 100(x_2 - x_1^2)^2.
\label{eq:lossC}
\end{equation}
\textbf{Non-convexity:}  Rosenbrock is \emph{not} globally convex.  The Hessian
$\nabla^2 f(x) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 + 1200x_1^2 - 400x_2 & -400x_1 \\ -400x_1 & 200 \end{bmatrix}$
can be indefinite: at $(0, 0.5)$, the $(1,1)$ entry is $2 + 0 - 200 = -198 < 0$, making the
Hessian indefinite.  This means convergence guarantees for convex functions do not apply globally,
and methods may encounter saddle points or flat regions.  In practice, the parabolic valley
$\{(x_1, x_2) : x_2 \approx x_1^2\}$ guides the iterates towards the minimum.  Near the starting
point $(-1, 1)$ (which lies on the valley: $x_2 = x_1^2 = 1$), the function is locally convex
with $\kappa \approx 1000$, and near the optimum $(1, 1)$, $\kappa \approx 500$.

The global minimum is at $(1, 1)$ with $f^{\star} = 0$.  The Hessian at the optimum is
\begin{equation}
\nabla^2 f(1,1) = \begin{bmatrix} 802 & -400 \\ -400 & 200 \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
with eigenvalues approximately $2$ and $1000$, giving a condition number
$\kappa \approx 500$.  This extreme ill-conditioning, combined with the narrow curved
valley along $x_2 = x_1^2$, makes Rosenbrock one of the most challenging standard test
functions for first-order methods~\cite{rosenbrock1960}.
Starting point: $x_0 = [-1, 1]^T$.

\subsection{Role of Condition Number in Algorithm Performance}

\begin{definition}[$L$-smoothness and $\mu$-strong convexity]
A function $f$ is \emph{$L$-smooth} if $\|\nabla f(x) - \nabla f(y)\| \leq L\|x - y\|$ for
all $x, y$, equivalently $\nabla^2 f(x) \preceq LI$.  It is \emph{$\mu$-strongly convex} if
$f(y) \geq f(x) + \langle \nabla f(x), y - x \rangle + \frac{\mu}{2}\|y-x\|^2$, equivalently
$\nabla^2 f(x) \succeq \mu I$.  Together they bound the Hessian eigenvalues:
$\mu \leq \lambda_{\min}(\nabla^2 f) \leq \lambda_{\max}(\nabla^2 f) \leq L$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Polyak--{\L}ojasiewicz (PL) condition: a weaker substitute for strong convexity]
The \emph{PL condition} (Polyak 1963, {\L}ojasiewicz 1963) requires only:
\begin{equation}
  \tfrac{1}{2}\|\nabla f(x)\|^2 \;\geq\; \mu\bigl(f(x) - f^{\star}\bigr) \quad \forall x.
\end{equation}
Unlike strong convexity, PL does not require $f$ to be convex or its minimiser to be unique;
it merely says that the gradient norm is bounded below by the sub-optimality gap.  Under PL
and $L$-smoothness, gradient descent with $\alpha = 1/L$ achieves linear convergence:
$f(x_k) - f^{\star} \leq (1 - \mu/L)^k(f(x_0) - f^{\star})$ --- the \emph{same rate}
as for strongly convex functions~\cite{nocedal2006}.  Strong convexity $\Rightarrow$ PL (with
the same $\mu$), but not vice versa.

\textbf{Relevance to Benchmark C:}  Rosenbrock is non-convex globally but satisfies PL in a
neighbourhood of $(1,1)$: near the minimum, $\|\nabla f(x)\|^2 \approx 2\cdot \nabla^2 f(x^{\star})
[e_k, e_k] \cdot \mu_H \|e_k\|^2 \geq 2\mu_H^2 \|e_k\|^2$ while $f(x) - f^{\star} \leq
\frac{L}{2}\|e_k\|^2$, so PL holds with $\mu = 2\mu_H^2/L$.  This is why methods that stay
near the valley floor (Polyak on Benchmark C, Newton after convergence) achieve fast convergence:
PL holds locally even though Rosenbrock is not globally strongly convex.  Over-parameterised
neural networks have been shown to satisfy PL in the neighbourhood of any global minimiser,
providing a theoretical explanation for the empirical observation that gradient-based training
reaches zero training loss.
\end{remark}

\textbf{Step-size selection:}  For GD on a $\mu$-strongly convex, $L$-smooth function, the
\emph{optimal} constant step size is $\alpha^{\star} = 2/(\mu + L)$, giving contraction
$((\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1))^k \approx (1 - 2/(\kappa+1))^k$.  Any $\alpha \in (0, 2/L)$ ensures
convergence; larger $\alpha$ (up to $2/L$) is faster but can oscillate.  In experiments,
$\alpha \ll 2/L$ is used for stability (e.g.\ $\alpha = 0.08$ vs $2/L \approx 1.54$ for
Benchmark A), which explains why GD appears slow compared to the optimal-step-size theory.

The condition number $\kappa = L/\mu$ (ratio of largest to smallest Hessian eigenvalue) is the
single most important quantity predicting convergence speed.  For gradient descent on a strongly
convex function, the asymptotic convergence rate is
$(1 - 2/(\kappa + 1))^k \approx \exp(-2k/\kappa)$.  This means:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Benchmark A ($\kappa \approx 1.3$): GD contraction factor $\approx 0.13$ --- convergence in
  $\sim$10 iterations.
\item Benchmark B ($\kappa \approx 5$): GD contraction factor $\approx 0.67$ --- convergence in
  $\sim$50 iterations.
\item Benchmark C ($\kappa \approx 500$ near optimum): GD contraction $\approx 0.996$ per iteration
  --- $\sim$2500 iterations to reduce error by $90\%$.  This explains why GD with $\alpha = 0.0012$
  barely advances on Rosenbrock in 80--150 iterations.
\end{itemize}

Momentum methods improve upon GD by replacing the contraction factor $(1 - 2/(\kappa+1))$ with
the accelerated rate $((\sqrt{\kappa} - 1)/(\sqrt{\kappa} + 1))^2 \approx 1 - 4/\sqrt{\kappa}$
(for Heavy Ball at optimal tuning), reducing the number of iterations from $O(\kappa)$ to
$O(\sqrt{\kappa})$.  Nesterov's method achieves $O(1/k^2)$ for general convex functions, equivalent
to an $O(\sqrt{\kappa})$ iteration count for strongly convex objectives.  Newton's method reduces
the iteration count to $O(\log\log(1/\varepsilon))$ (double-exponential convergence), essentially
making the problem condition-number independent in the local regime.

\textbf{Quantitative iteration count predictions:}  For GD to reduce the sub-optimality gap
by a factor of $10^{-3}$ (from $\epsilon_0$ to $10^{-3}\epsilon_0$):
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Benchmark A ($\kappa \approx 1.3$): rate $\rho = (1-2/2.3) = 0.13$; iterations
  $k = \lceil\log_{0.13}(10^{-3})\rceil \approx 4$.
\item Benchmark B ($\kappa \approx 5$): rate $\rho = (1-2/6) = 0.667$; iterations
  $k \approx \log_{0.667}(10^{-3}) \approx 20$.
\item Benchmark C ($\kappa \approx 500$): rate $\rho = (1-2/501) \approx 0.996$; iterations
  $k \approx \log_{0.996}(10^{-3}) \approx 1726$.
\end{itemize}

For Nesterov on Benchmark C (strongly convex): rate $r = ((\sqrt{500}-1)/(\sqrt{500}+1))^2
\approx (21.4/23.4)^2 \approx 0.836$; iterations $k \approx \log_{0.836}(10^{-3}) \approx 36$.
This is a $1726/36 \approx 48$-fold speedup over GD, consistent with the $O(\sqrt{\kappa})$ vs
$O(\kappa)$ scaling: $\sqrt{500}/500 \approx 1/22.4$, so the theoretical speedup is $\approx 22\times$
per logarithm.  The empirical results (Nesterov $f = 0.479$ vs.\ GD $f = 3.39$ after 150 iterations)
are consistent: Nesterov has reduced the gap from the initial value by a factor of 7, while GD
has barely moved.

\begin{remark}[Spectral decomposition of gradient descent convergence]
For a quadratic $f(x) = \frac{1}{2}x^T H x - b^T x$ with eigendecomposition $H = Q\Lambda Q^T$,
define the error $e_k = x_k - x^{\star}$ and transform to the eigenbasis: $\tilde{e}_k = Q^T e_k$.
GD with step $\alpha$ evolves each mode independently:
$\tilde{e}_k^{(i)} = (1 - \alpha\lambda_i)^k \tilde{e}_0^{(i)}$,
where $\lambda_i$ is the $i$-th eigenvalue of $H$.  The total error is
$\|e_k\|^2 = \sum_i (1-\alpha\lambda_i)^{2k}(\tilde{e}_0^{(i)})^2$.

For \textbf{Benchmark B} ($H = \mathrm{diag}(2.4,\, 10)$, $\alpha = 0.06$):
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Mode 1 ($x_1$ direction, $\lambda_1 = 2.4$): contraction $(1 - 0.06 \times 2.4)^k = 0.856^k$.
\item Mode 2 ($x_2$ direction, $\lambda_2 = 10$): contraction $(1 - 0.06 \times 10)^k = 0.40^k$.
\end{itemize}
Mode 2 converges roughly $\log(0.40)/\log(0.856) \approx 5\times$ faster than mode 1.  This
explains the figure patterns: the trajectory quickly reaches the correct $x_2$ value (fast mode),
then slowly adjusts $x_1$ (slow mode) --- the ``staircase'' or zig-zag pattern typical of
anisotropic quadratics.

For \textbf{Benchmark C} (Rosenbrock near the optimum): the Hessian eigenvalues are approximately
$\lambda_1 \approx 2$ (along the valley floor) and $\lambda_2 \approx 402$ (across the valley).
With $\alpha = 0.001$:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Slow mode: $(1 - 0.001 \times 2)^k = 0.998^k$ --- needs $\sim$1150 steps per decade.
\item Fast mode: $(1 - 0.001 \times 402)^k = 0.598^k$ --- converges in $\sim$5 steps per decade.
\end{itemize}
GD instantly equilibrates the cross-valley ($x_2$) direction but makes negligible progress along
the valley ($x_1$ direction), explaining the observed convergence pattern.  This spectral picture
also explains why Nesterov and Newton succeed: Nesterov applies the Chebyshev polynomial
(optimally simultaneous convergence in all modes); Newton uses the Hessian to equalize all mode
step sizes to $O(1/\lambda_i)$, achieving condition-number-free convergence.
\end{remark}

\section{Question 1: Adaptive Step-Size Methods \hfill [30 marks]}

\subsection{Algorithm Formulations}

Four adaptive methods are compared against a constant step-size gradient descent baseline
across all three benchmarks for 120 iterations.  Let $g_k = \nabla f(x_k)$ throughout.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Polyak Step Size}
\label{alg:polyak}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, known optimal value $f^{\star}$, tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $\alpha_k \gets \dfrac{f(x_k) - f^{\star}}{\|g_k\|^2 + \varepsilon}$ \Comment{Step size proportional to sub-optimality}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha_k\, g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}[Polyak convergence~\cite{polyak1969}]
For a convex function $f$ with Lipschitz gradient, the Polyak step size satisfies
$f(x_k) - f^{\star} \leq \frac{\|x_0 - x^{\star}\|^2}{2k}$ when $f^{\star}$ is exactly known,
achieving an $O(1/k)$ convergence rate without tuning a learning rate.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}[Underestimation causes divergence]
If $f^{\star}_{\mathrm{spec}} < f^{\star}_{\mathrm{true}}$ (i.e.\ the specified value underestimates
the true minimum), then $f(x_k) - f^{\star}_{\mathrm{spec}} \to f^{\star}_{\mathrm{true}} -
f^{\star}_{\mathrm{spec}} > 0$ while $\|g_k\| \to 0$, causing $\alpha_k \to \infty$ and divergence.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Relationship to subgradient methods]
The Polyak step size originates in subgradient optimisation for non-smooth $f$.  For non-smooth
convex functions, no gradient exists, but a \emph{subgradient} $g_k \in \partial f(x_k)$ can
be computed; the Polyak step $\alpha_k = (f(x_k) - f^{\star})/\|g_k\|^2$ is then well-defined
whenever $g_k \neq 0$.  The same $O(1/k)$ rate holds.  For smooth functions (as here),
the Polyak step reduces to the gradient step with a self-tuned magnitude proportional to
the current sub-optimality divided by the squared gradient norm.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
If $f^{\star}_{\mathrm{spec}} > f^{\star}_{\mathrm{true}}$ (over-estimate), then
$f(x_k) - f^{\star}_{\mathrm{spec}} \to f^{\star}_{\mathrm{true}} - f^{\star}_{\mathrm{spec}} < 0$,
making $\alpha_k < 0$: the update becomes an ascent step.  The worst case is therefore an
overestimate.  Underestimates (as in Benchmarks A and B where $f^{\star}_{\mathrm{spec}} = 0 <
f^{\star}_{\mathrm{true}}$) cause divergence rather than incorrect sign.  In both cases, exact
knowledge of $f^{\star}$ is essential --- a stringent requirement in practice.
\end{remark}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Adagrad~\cite{duchi2011}}
\label{alg:adagrad}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, base learning rate $\alpha_0$, tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$, iterations $N$
\State $G_0 \gets \mathbf{0} \in \mathbb{R}^d$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $G_{k+1} \gets G_k + g_k \odot g_k$ \Comment{Per-coordinate squared-gradient accumulation}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \dfrac{\alpha_0}{\sqrt{G_{k+1}} + \varepsilon} \odot g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The effective per-coordinate step is $\alpha_k^{(i)} = \alpha_0 / \sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{k} g_j^{(i)2}}$,
which decays monotonically.  Adagrad is well-suited to sparse gradients (where most coordinates
receive rare updates) but may prematurely slow on dense problems.

\textbf{Convergence rate sketch:} For convex $f$ with subgradients $\|g_k\|_\infty \leq G$,
Adagrad achieves the regret bound $\sum_{k=1}^K [f(x_k) - f^{\star}] \leq \frac{\|x_0 - x^{\star}\|_2^2 \sqrt{G^2 + \sum_{k} \|g_k\|^2}}{2\alpha_0}$~\cite{duchi2011}.
For dense gradients where $\|g_k\| \approx G\sqrt{d}$, the accumulated $\sum_k \|g_k\|^2 \approx
KG^2 d$, giving average regret $O(G\sqrt{d\log K} / \sqrt{K})$ per step --- a $\sqrt{d}$ factor
worse than optimal for dense settings.  For sparse gradients where only $s \ll d$ coordinates
have non-zero $g_k^{(i)}$, the effective $\sum_k g_k^{(i)2}$ grows slowly, so the adaptive
step for sparse coordinates decays much more slowly than for dense ones --- this is Adagrad's
key advantage for sparse embeddings in natural language processing.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{RMSprop~\cite{hinton2012}}
\label{alg:rmsprop}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, learning rate $\alpha_0$, decay $\beta \in (0,1)$, tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$, iterations $N$
\State $v_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $v_{k+1} \gets \beta\, v_k + (1 - \beta)\, g_k \odot g_k$ \Comment{Exponential moving average of $\|g\|^2$}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \dfrac{\alpha_0}{\sqrt{v_{k+1}} + \varepsilon} \odot g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

RMSprop addresses Adagrad's monotone decay by using an exponential moving average of squared
gradients with forgetting factor $\beta$.  This allows the effective step size to adapt to
recent curvature rather than the full gradient history.

\begin{remark}[Adagrad as mirror descent with Mahalanobis geometry]
Adagrad (Algorithm~\ref{alg:adagrad}) has a deep mirror-descent interpretation.  Define the
accumulated diagonal preconditioner $G_k = \mathrm{diag}(G_k^{(1)}, \ldots, G_k^{(d)})$ where
$G_k^{(i)} = \sum_{j=0}^k g_j^{(i)2}$, and the potential $\phi_k(x) = \frac{1}{2}x^T G_k^{1/2} x$.
The induced Bregman divergence is $D_{\phi_k}(x, x_k) = \frac{1}{2}(x-x_k)^T G_k^{1/2}(x-x_k)$,
which is the \emph{Mahalanobis norm} $\frac{1}{2}\|x - x_k\|_{G_k^{1/2}}^2$.  The mirror descent
update with this potential recovers exactly the Adagrad update, with the regret bound:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\bigl[f(x_k) - f^{\star}\bigr]
  \;\leq\; \frac{\|x_0 - x^{\star}\|^2}{2\alpha_0}\, \operatorname{tr}\!\left(G_K^{1/2}\right)
  = \frac{\alpha_0}{2}\sum_{i=1}^d \|x_0^{(i)}-x^{\star(i)}\|^2 \sqrt{G_K^{(i)}}.
\end{equation}
This is coordinate-adaptive: features (coordinates $i$) with small accumulated gradient $G_K^{(i)}$
incur low regret even with large displacement $|x_0^{(i)} - x^{\star(i)}|$.  This explains
Adagrad's advantage for sparse NLP embeddings: rarely-updated coordinates receive larger effective
step sizes.  Adam extends this by replacing the full accumulation $G_K$ with an exponentially
decaying window, sacrificing theoretical guarantees but gaining non-stationarity adaptivity.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[AMSGrad: restoring Adagrad's convergence guarantee]
Reddi et al.~\cite{reddi2018} showed that Adam and RMSprop can fail to converge on simple convex
online problems due to the exponential moving average of squared gradients increasing in some steps.
AMSGrad fixes this by replacing $\hat{v}_t$ with $\hat{v}_t^{\max} = \max(\hat{v}_{t-1}^{\max}, \hat{v}_t)$,
ensuring the effective step size never increases.  This restores Adagrad's convergence guarantee
while retaining the benefit of adaptivity.  The correction is critical in settings where occasional
large-gradient steps should not be ``forgotten'' by the moving average.
\end{remark}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Polyak Momentum (Heavy Ball)~\cite{polyak1964}}
\label{alg:heavyball}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, momentum $\beta \in (0,1)$, iterations $N$
\State $z_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets \beta\, z_k + \alpha\, \nabla f(x_k)$ \Comment{Velocity accumulation}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - z_{k+1}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The Heavy Ball method adds a momentum term that accumulates the gradient direction across
iterations.  For a quadratic $f$ with condition number $\kappa$, the theoretically optimal
parameters $\alpha^{\star} = 4/((\sqrt{L} + \sqrt{\mu})^2)$ and $\beta^{\star} =
((\sqrt{\kappa}-1)/(\sqrt{\kappa}+1))^2$ yield an asymptotic contraction factor
$r = ((\sqrt{\kappa}-1)/(\sqrt{\kappa}+1))^2$, dramatically improving on GD's rate of
$(1 - 2/(\kappa+1))$~\cite{polyak1964}.

\textbf{Derivation sketch:}  For $f(x) = \frac{1}{2}x^TH x$ with eigenvalues
$\lambda \in [\mu, L]$, each eigenmode evolves as $e_{k+1} = \beta e_k - \alpha\lambda e_k$
(scalar, in the eigenbasis).  The two-step recursion has characteristic roots
$\rho_{1,2} = \frac{(1+\beta-\alpha\lambda) \pm \sqrt{(1+\beta-\alpha\lambda)^2 - 4\beta}}{2}$,
and the convergence rate for eigenvalue $\lambda$ is $\max(|\rho_1|, |\rho_2|)$.  Minimising
this rate over $\lambda \in [\mu, L]$ (a minimax problem) yields the optimal $\alpha^{\star}$
and $\beta^{\star}$ above, achieving $r = ((\sqrt\kappa-1)/(\sqrt\kappa+1))^2$.  For
$\kappa = 500$: $r = ((22.4-1)/(22.4+1))^2 \approx (0.914)^2 \approx 0.836$, giving $\sim
36$ iterations to reduce error by $10^{-3}$ --- the same as Nesterov (both $O(\sqrt\kappa)$).

\begin{remark}[Heavy Ball can diverge on convex non-quadratic functions]
Despite being optimal for quadratics, Heavy Ball has \emph{no} general convergence guarantee
for non-quadratic convex functions.  Lessard et al.\ (2016) constructed convex functions on
which Heavy Ball with any fixed $(\alpha, \beta)$ diverges.  Nesterov's method avoids this
issue through the adaptive momentum schedule $\beta_k = (k-1)/(k+2) \nearrow 1$, which
ensures the iterate sequence satisfies a global Lyapunov descent condition.  Empirically on
Benchmark C (Rosenbrock, non-quadratic convex), Heavy Ball achieves $f = 1.40$ while Nesterov
achieves $f = 0.479$ --- a consistent $3\times$ improvement that reflects the theoretical gap.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Algorithm Formulations}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Nesterov Accelerated Gradient~\cite{nesterov1983}}
\label{alg:nesterov}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, max momentum $\beta_{\max}$, iterations $N$
\State $z_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 1, 2, \ldots, N$}
    \State $\beta_k \gets \min\!\left(\dfrac{k-1}{k+2},\; \beta_{\max}\right)$ \Comment{Adaptive momentum schedule}
    \State $\tilde{x}_k \gets x_k + \beta_k\, z_k$ \Comment{Lookahead point}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets \beta_k\, z_k - \alpha\, \nabla f(\tilde{x}_k)$
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k + z_{k+1}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}[Nesterov acceleration~\cite{nesterov1983}]
For a convex function $f$ with $L$-Lipschitz gradient, Nesterov's accelerated gradient method with
$\alpha = 1/L$ and momentum schedule $\beta_k = (k-1)/(k+2)$ achieves
\begin{equation}
f(x_k) - f^{\star} \leq \frac{2L\|x_0 - x^{\star}\|^2}{(k+1)^2} = O\!\left(\frac{1}{k^2}\right),
\end{equation}
which is \emph{optimal} for first-order methods on convex functions~\cite{nesterov2004}.  This
contrasts with gradient descent's $O(1/k)$ rate (or $O((1-\mu/L)^k)$ for strongly convex $f$).
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}[Optimal first-order complexity]
Nesterov (1983) also proved a \emph{lower bound}: no first-order method (using only values and
gradients) can achieve a convergence rate better than $\Omega(1/k^2)$ for general smooth convex
functions.  This means Nesterov's method is \emph{optimal} in the first-order oracle model: its
convergence rate cannot be improved without exploiting additional structure (e.g.\ strong convexity,
Hessian information).  For strongly convex functions, the optimal first-order rate is
$O\bigl((1 - \sqrt{\mu/L})^k\bigr)$, achieved by Nesterov's strongly-convex variant, reducing
the iteration count from $O(\kappa)$ (GD) to $O(\sqrt{\kappa})$.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[First-order oracle lower bound~\cite{nesterov2004}]
\label{thm:lower_bound}
For any deterministic first-order method generating iterates $x_1, \ldots, x_k$ using oracle
information $\{f(x_j), \nabla f(x_j)\}_{j < k}$ (with $x_j \in \mathrm{span}\{x_0, \nabla
f(x_0), \ldots, \nabla f(x_{j-1})\}$), there exists an $L$-smooth convex function
$f : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$ and a starting point $x_0$ such that, for all
$k \leq \lfloor (d-1)/2 \rfloor$:
\begin{equation}
f(x_k) - f^{\star} \;\geq\; \frac{3L\|x_0 - x^{\star}\|^2}{32(k+1)^2}.
\end{equation}
The worst-case function is a tridiagonal quadratic on $\mathbb{R}^d$ whose structure ensures
that each gradient oracle call reveals information about only one new coordinate.  Consequently,
after $k$ steps, the method has ``seen'' at most $k+1$ coordinates, and the remaining $d-k-1$
coordinates contribute an irreducible error of at least $\Omega(1/k^2)$.

\textbf{Implications:} (i) No first-order method can do better than $O(1/k^2)$ on general convex
functions, so Nesterov's method (Theorem~\ref{thm:nesterov}) achieves the \emph{information-theoretic
limit}.  (ii) For strongly convex functions, the analogous lower bound is
$\Omega(((\sqrt\kappa-1)/(\sqrt\kappa+1))^{2k})$, matched by Nesterov's strongly convex variant.
(iii) Newton's method ($O(\log\log 1/\varepsilon)$ iterations) escapes this lower bound because it
uses second-order (Hessian) information --- not captured by the first-order oracle model.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}[Adaptive restart for Nesterov's method]
A practical limitation of Nesterov's method is that the momentum schedule $\beta_k = (k-1)/(k+2)
\nearrow 1$ can cause oscillation when the iterates are far from the optimum on non-convex or
strongly convex objectives.  \emph{Adaptive restart} (O'Donoghue \& Cand\`es, 2015) monitors
the function value and resets the momentum ($\beta_k \to 0$, $k \to 0$) whenever $f(x_{k+1}) >
f(x_k)$ (function restart) or $\nabla f(x_k)^T (x_k - x_{k-1}) > 0$ (gradient restart).  This
heuristic often achieves significantly faster practical convergence on strongly convex problems
while preserving the $O(1/k^2)$ guarantee for general convex functions.
\end{remark}

The key distinction from Heavy Ball (Algorithm~\ref{alg:heavyball}) is the \emph{lookahead}:
the gradient is evaluated at $\tilde{x}_k = x_k + \beta_k z_k$ rather than $x_k$.  This
``corrective'' step allows the momentum to be applied in the right direction before overshooting,
enabling the $O(1/k^2)$ guarantee rather than the $O(1/k)$ rate achievable by Heavy Ball.

\begin{remark}[Chebyshev polynomial optimality of Nesterov's method]
For a quadratic objective $f(x) = \frac{1}{2}x^T H x$ with $H = Q\Lambda Q^T$ and
$\Lambda = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_d)$ ($\lambda_i \in [\mu, L]$), gradient
descent with step $\alpha$ evolves as $e_k = (I - \alpha H)^k e_0 = Q(I-\alpha\Lambda)^k Q^T e_0$.
Any first-order method that starts from $x_0$ and takes $k$ steps produces an iterate satisfying
$e_k = p_k(H)e_0$ for some \emph{degree-$k$ polynomial} $p_k$ with $p_k(0) = 1$ (the constraint
that ensures the update is well-defined at $\lambda = 0$).  The optimal such polynomial ---
minimising $\max_{\lambda \in [\mu,L]} |p_k(\lambda)|$ --- is the normalised Chebyshev polynomial:
\begin{equation}
p_k^{\star}(\lambda)
  = \frac{T_k\!\left(\dfrac{L+\mu-2\lambda}{L-\mu}\right)}{T_k\!\left(\dfrac{L+\mu}{L-\mu}\right)},
\end{equation}
where $T_k$ is the Chebyshev polynomial of the first kind.  For large $k$, the minimax error
$1/T_k((L+\mu)/(L-\mu)) \approx 2\bigl((\sqrt\kappa-1)/(\sqrt\kappa+1)\bigr)^k$, matching
Nesterov's $O(\sqrt\kappa)$ complexity.  By contrast, gradient descent uses
$p_k(\lambda) = (1-\alpha\lambda)^k$, a \emph{suboptimal} polynomial whose worst-case value
over $[\mu, L]$ is $(\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1) = O(1-2/\kappa)$, giving $O(\kappa)$ complexity.
The factor $\sqrt\kappa$ speedup of Nesterov over GD is therefore not a coincidence: it is the
optimal gain achievable by any first-order method on strongly convex quadratics, corresponding
to the exponential gap between $(1-2/\kappa)^k$ and $((\sqrt\kappa-1)/(\sqrt\kappa+1))^{2k}$.
Nesterov's method \emph{implicitly} builds the Chebyshev polynomial through its momentum
updates, even without knowledge of $\mu$ and $L$ explicitly.
\end{remark}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Adam Optimiser~\cite{kingma2015}}
\label{alg:adam}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, learning rate $\alpha$, moments $\beta_1, \beta_2 \in (0,1)$, $\varepsilon > 0$, iterations $N$
\State $m_0 \gets \mathbf{0},\; v_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$t = 1, 2, \ldots, N$}
    \State $g_t \gets \nabla f(x_t)$
    \State $m_t \gets \beta_1\, m_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_1)\, g_t$ \Comment{First moment (momentum)}
    \State $v_t \gets \beta_2\, v_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_2)\, g_t \odot g_t$ \Comment{Second moment (adaptive scaling)}
    \State $\hat{m}_t \gets m_t\, /\, (1 - \beta_1^t)$ \Comment{Bias correction}
    \State $\hat{v}_t \gets v_t\, /\, (1 - \beta_2^t)$
    \State $x_{t+1} \gets x_t - \alpha\, \hat{m}_t\, /\, (\sqrt{\hat{v}_t} + \varepsilon)$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Adam combines first-moment momentum (inherited from Heavy Ball / Nesterov) with per-coordinate
adaptive scaling (inherited from RMSprop).  The bias-correction terms $1/(1-\beta_i^t)$ compensate
for the zero-initialisation of $m_0$ and $v_0$, preventing underestimation in the early iterations.

\textbf{Second moment bias analysis:}  Without correction, $\mathbb{E}[v_t^{(i)}] = (1-\beta_2^t)
\mathbb{E}[g_t^{(i)2}]$.  For $\beta_2 = 0.999$ and $t = 1$: the bias factor is $1 - 0.999 = 0.001$,
so the uncorrected $v_1$ is only $0.1\%$ of the true second moment --- a $1000\times$ underestimate.
The correction $\hat{v}_t = v_t/(1-\beta_2^t)$ restores it.  By $t = 100$: $1 - 0.999^{100}
\approx 0.095$, correction factor $\approx 10.5$.  By $t = 1000$: $1 - 0.999^{1000} \approx 0.63$,
correction factor $\approx 1.6$.  The second-moment bias correction is thus critical for the first
$\sim 700$ iterations when $\beta_2 = 0.999$, explaining why the bias correction is important for
the 150-iteration experiments here.

\textbf{Necessity of bias correction:} Without correction, $\mathbb{E}[m_t] = (1-\beta_1^t)g$ at
the first step when $m_0 = 0$, so the uncorrected $m_t$ underestimates the true gradient by a
factor $(1 - \beta_1^t)$.  For $\beta_1 = 0.82$ and $t = 1$, this gives $0.18\,g$ --- an $82\%$
underestimate.  Bias correction restores $\hat{m}_t = m_t/(1-\beta_1^t) = m_t/0.18 \approx g$,
yielding an unbiased estimate.  By $t = 10$, $1 - 0.82^{10} \approx 0.86$, so the correction
factor is already close to 1 and becomes negligible at large $t$.  The same argument applies to
the second moment $v_t$ with $\beta_2 = 0.999$, which corrects even more slowly.

\begin{remark}[Adam's implicit regularization and the optimization--generalization gap]
Despite lacking formal convergence guarantees for non-convex problems, Adam is the default
optimizer in deep learning.  The disconnect between optimization theory and practice arises
partly from \emph{implicit regularization}: the optimizer's trajectory through parameter space
influences which solution is found among the many global minima of over-parameterized models.

Wilson et al.\ (2017) showed empirically that SGD with momentum often \emph{generalizes better}
than Adam on image classification benchmarks, despite Adam converging faster in training loss.
The hypothesis is that Adam's aggressive per-coordinate scaling tends to find ``sharp'' minima
(high Hessian eigenvalues) that are sensitive to small perturbations, while SGD's isotropic
updates find ``flatter'' minima with lower second-order sensitivity and better test accuracy.
Formally, for a minimum $x^{\star}$, the \emph{sharpness} is $\lambda_{\max}(\nabla^2 f(x^{\star}))$;
flatter minima ($\lambda_{\max}$ small) are more robust to weight perturbations and data shifts.
\emph{Sharpness-aware minimization} (Foret et al.\ 2021) makes this explicit: it optimizes
$\max_{\|\delta\|\leq\rho} f(x+\delta)$ to explicitly seek flat minima.

For the benchmarks in this report (convex objectives, no generalization gap), Adam's performance
is purely an optimization question --- no implicit regularization effects apply.  The gap between
Adam and Nesterov on Benchmark C is therefore a pure optimization phenomenon: the diagonal
Hessian approximation misses the off-diagonal coupling in Rosenbrock's valley.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Mini-Batch Stochastic Gradient Descent}

Mini-batch SGD on Benchmark A uses learning rate $\alpha = 0.06$, batch sizes $b \in \{5, 40\}$,
50 epochs, with data shuffled at each epoch.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Mini-Batch SGD}
\label{alg:sgd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Data $(X, y)$, initial $\theta_0$, learning rate $\alpha$, batch size $b$, epochs $E$
\For{$e = 1, 2, \ldots, E$}
    \State Randomly permute data indices $\{1,\ldots,m\}$
    \For{each mini-batch $\mathcal{B} \subset \{1,\ldots,m\}$ of size $b$}
        \State $g \gets \frac{1}{b} X_{\mathcal{B}}^T(X_{\mathcal{B}}\theta - y_{\mathcal{B}})$
        \Comment{Mini-batch gradient}
        \State $\theta \gets \theta - \alpha\, g$
    \EndFor
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The mini-batch gradient $g_{\mathcal{B}}$ is an unbiased estimator of the full gradient:
$\mathbb{E}[g_{\mathcal{B}}] = \nabla J(\theta)$, with variance
$\mathrm{Var}[g_{\mathcal{B}}] = \frac{m - b}{b(m-1)}\mathrm{Var}[g_{\mathrm{sample}}] \approx
\frac{\sigma_g^2}{b}$ (proportional to $1/b$, where $\sigma_g^2$ is the per-sample gradient variance).
Larger batches reduce gradient variance but require more computation per epoch.

For a quadratic loss with fixed learning rate $\alpha$, SGD converges to a \emph{noise ball}
around the minimum of radius $r \propto \alpha \sigma_g^2 / b$~\cite{nocedal2006}.
Reducing $\alpha$ shrinks the noise ball but slows initial convergence; increasing $b$ has the
same effect at the cost of more computation per step.  These trade-offs underlie the commonly
used \emph{linear scaling rule}: doubling the batch size should be accompanied by doubling the
learning rate to maintain the same convergence speed per epoch.

\begin{remark}[Linear scaling rule and warm-up]
The linear scaling rule (Krizhevsky 2014; Goyal et al.\ 2017) states: if the batch size is
increased by a factor $\beta$, scale the learning rate by $\beta$.  The intuition is that
$\beta$ mini-batch steps with learning rate $\alpha/\beta$ are approximately equivalent to one
step with learning rate $\alpha$ (each step moves by $\alpha g_k$ in expectation; $\beta$ such
steps accumulate to $\alpha \nabla f$, matching one full-batch step at rate $\alpha$).  More
precisely, for a quadratic objective the noise ball radius scales as
$r \propto \alpha \sigma_g^2 / b$; fixing $\alpha \cdot b = \text{const}$ keeps the noise ball
fixed while delivering the same expected per-step decrease in the objective.

The alternative \emph{square-root scaling rule} ($\alpha \propto \sqrt{b}$) is more conservative
and arises from fixing the gradient noise $\alpha\sigma_g/\sqrt{b} = \text{const}$; it is
appropriate when gradient noise is the binding constraint rather than per-epoch progress.  In
practice, Goyal et al.\ found the linear rule valid up to $b \approx 8192$ on ImageNet
(ResNet-50) provided a \emph{warm-up} period of 5 epochs is used: starting from a small
$\alpha$ and linearly ramping to the scaled value avoids instability from large initial steps
when the model is far from convergence.  For strongly convex objectives (as in Benchmark A),
the linear scaling rule is exact: the noise ball radius condition determines convergence, and
linear scaling keeps it constant as $b$ grows.
\end{remark}

\textbf{Irreducible noise floor for linear regression:}  When $y = X\theta^{\star} + \varepsilon$
with $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I_m)$, the MSE loss at the OLS minimiser satisfies
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}\bigl[J(\hat\theta)\bigr]
  = \frac{1}{2m}\mathbb{E}\bigl\|{(I - P)\varepsilon}\bigr\|^2
  = \frac{(m-d)\sigma^2}{2m}
  \approx \frac{\sigma^2}{2} \quad (m \gg d),
\label{eq:noise_floor}
\end{equation}
where $P = X(X^TX)^{-1}X^T$ is the hat matrix with $\operatorname{tr}(I - P) = m - d$.
For Benchmark A ($m = 1000$, $d = 2$, $\sigma = 1$): $J^{\star} \approx 998/2000 \approx 0.499$,
consistent with the empirical $0.4826$.
Scaling to $\sigma = 6$ multiplies the floor by $\sigma^2 = 36$: $J^{\star} \approx 0.499 \times 36
\approx 17.96$, consistent with the observed $\approx 17.4$.
This confirms the $\sigma^2$ scaling and explains why both batch sizes converge to approximately
the same elevated floor under high noise: the observation-noise term $\sigma^2/2$ dominates
any reduction from larger batches, which only reduce the batch-sampling component of variance.

\begin{remark}[Robbins--Monro convergence conditions for SGD]
For SGD on a strongly convex objective with stochastic gradients satisfying
$\mathbb{E}[g_k] = \nabla f(x_k)$ and $\mathbb{E}[\|g_k\|^2] \leq G^2$, the Robbins--Monro
conditions for $\sum_{k=1}^\infty \alpha_k = \infty$ and $\sum_{k=1}^\infty \alpha_k^2 < \infty$
guarantee almost-sure convergence to $x^{\star}$.  The schedule $\alpha_k = \alpha_0 / (k + 1)$
satisfies both conditions and achieves $\mathbb{E}[\|x_k - x^{\star}\|^2] = O(1/k)$.  In
contrast, a fixed step size $\alpha$ (as used here) satisfies only the first condition, causing
convergence to the noise ball of radius $O(\alpha G^2 / \mu)$ rather than to $x^{\star}$ itself.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Polyak--Ruppert averaging]
For SGD with decaying step sizes, the individual iterates $x_k$ have high variance even as they
approach $x^{\star}$.  The Polyak--Ruppert averaging scheme (independently due to Polyak 1990
and Ruppert 1988) replaces the last iterate with the running average
$\bar{x}_k = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^k x_j$.  For a $\mu$-strongly convex, $L$-smooth objective
with bounded gradient noise $\mathbb{E}[\|g_k - \nabla f(x_k)\|^2] \leq \sigma^2$, averaged SGD
with constant step $\alpha$ achieves:
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}\bigl[f(\bar{x}_k) - f^{\star}\bigr]
  \;\leq\;
  \frac{L\|x_0-x^{\star}\|^2}{2\mu k\alpha}
  + \frac{\alpha\sigma^2}{2\mu}.
\end{equation}
Setting $\alpha = \|x_0-x^{\star}\|/({\sigma\sqrt{k}})$ gives the minimax-optimal rate
$O(\sigma\|x_0-x^{\star}\|/\sqrt{k\mu})$, matching the Cram\'{e}r--Rao lower bound for stochastic
first-order methods~\cite{nocedal2006}.  By contrast, the last iterate $x_k$ (without averaging)
requires a decaying $\alpha_k \sim 1/k$ schedule to converge to $x^{\star}$, which is difficult
to tune.  In practice, \emph{tail averaging} (averaging only the last $k/2$ iterates) discards
the transient phase and achieves the same asymptotic variance.  This explains why batch normalisation
and exponential moving averages are common in deep learning: they apply Polyak--Ruppert logic to
model parameters.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Variance reduction: SVRG and SAG]
The fundamental limitation of mini-batch SGD is the residual gradient variance $\sigma_g^2/b$,
which prevents convergence to the exact minimum for fixed $\alpha$.  \emph{Variance reduction}
methods eliminate this by correcting the stochastic gradient with a periodic full-gradient
anchor.  The SVRG update (Johnson \& Zhang, 2013~\cite{johnson2013}) is:
\begin{equation}
x_{k+1} = x_k - \alpha\bigl[\underbrace{\nabla f_{i_k}(x_k)}_{\text{stoch.}} - \underbrace{\nabla f_{i_k}(\tilde{x})}_{\text{anchor}} + \underbrace{\tilde{g}}_{\text{full grad}}\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{x}$ is a snapshot point (updated every $s$ iterations) and
$\tilde{g} = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m \nabla f_i(\tilde{x})$ is the full gradient at $\tilde{x}$.
The variance of the corrected estimator is $\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f_{i_k}(x_k) - \nabla f_{i_k}(\tilde{x}) + \tilde{g} - \nabla f(x_k)\|^2]$,
which goes to zero as $x_k \to \tilde{x} \to x^{\star}$.  This \emph{asymptotically eliminates}
the noise floor.  SVRG achieves linear convergence $\mathbb{E}[f(x_k) - f^{\star}] \leq
(1 - \mu/(10L))^{\lfloor k/s \rfloor}(f(x_0) - f^{\star})$ for $\mu$-strongly convex,
$L$-smooth $f$, \emph{without} a decaying step size.  The per-iteration cost is the same as
SGD (one gradient evaluation), plus $2m$ evaluations every $s$ steps for $\tilde{g}$; setting
$s = 2m$ gives an amortised cost of $3\times$ SGD.  SAG~\cite{roux2012} avoids the snapshot
recomputation by storing $m$ per-sample gradient estimates and averaging them, at a storage
cost of $O(md)$.  Both methods would converge geometrically to $J^{\star}$ on Benchmark A
rather than to the noise ball of mini-batch SGD.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Comparative Discussion: Heavy Ball vs Nesterov}

Both Heavy Ball (Q1) and Nesterov (Q2) add a momentum term, but they differ in where the
gradient is evaluated:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Heavy Ball}: gradient at current point $x_k$;  momentum weight fixed at $\beta$.
\item \textbf{Nesterov}: gradient at lookahead point $\tilde{x}_k = x_k + \beta_k z_k$;  momentum
  adaptively increases as $\beta_k = (k-1)/(k+2)$.
\end{itemize}

This lookahead corrects the momentum direction \emph{before} committing to the step, enabling the
theoretically optimal $O(1/k^2)$ convergence rate.  In experiments, Nesterov achieves $f = 0.479$
on Rosenbrock vs.\ Heavy Ball's $f = 1.400$ after the same number of iterations, a $3\times$ improvement.

\begin{remark}[Natural gradient and Adam's diagonal approximation]
The \emph{natural gradient method} (Amari 1998) preconditions the steepest descent by the Fisher
information matrix $F(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[\nabla\log p(y;\theta)\nabla\log p(y;\theta)^T]$:
$\theta_{k+1} = \theta_k - \alpha F(\theta_k)^{-1}\nabla f(\theta_k)$.  For exponential family
models, $F = \mathbb{E}[\nabla^2 f]$ (the expected Hessian), making natural gradient equivalent
to Newton's method but derived from the geometry of the space of probability distributions
(parameterised by $\theta$) rather than Euclidean parameter space.  The natural gradient step
moves the same Euclidean distance as gradient descent but along the steepest descent \emph{in
the statistical manifold}, invariant to reparameterisation of $\theta$.

Adam can be interpreted as a diagonal approximation to the natural gradient:
the second moment $\hat{v}_t^{(i)} \approx F^{(ii)}(\theta_t)$ estimates the diagonal Fisher,
and $1/\sqrt{\hat{v}_t}$ approximates $F^{-1}$ via its diagonal.  This approximation is
\emph{exact} when $F$ is diagonal (independent coordinates, as in NLP embeddings), which explains
Adam's strong performance on sparse language model objectives.  For Rosenbrock, however,
$F \approx \nabla^2 f$ has a large off-diagonal entry $-400x_1$, and the diagonal approximation
misses this coupling entirely --- exactly the reason Adam underperforms Nesterov on Benchmark C.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Gradient clipping as an adaptive safeguard]
In deep learning, particularly for recurrent networks and transformers, gradients can
\emph{explode}: due to vanishing/exploding gradients through time, $\|\nabla f(x_k)\|$ can
be many orders of magnitude larger than expected, causing divergent parameter updates.
\emph{Gradient clipping by norm} (Pascanu et al.\ 2013) rescales the gradient if its norm
exceeds a threshold $\tau$:
\begin{equation}
  g_k \;\leftarrow\; g_k \cdot \min\!\Bigl(1,\; \frac{\tau}{\|g_k\|}\Bigr).
\end{equation}
This preserves the gradient direction while bounding the step size to $\alpha\tau$.  For
Adam, the adaptive scaling $m_t/(\sqrt{v_t}+\varepsilon)$ already provides implicit gradient
modulation: in directions with consistently large gradients, $\sqrt{v_t}$ grows large and
the effective step shrinks.  Explicit clipping adds an additional global safeguard for
transient spikes.  Gradient clipping has a mirror-descent interpretation: it corresponds to
a projected gradient step onto the $\ell_2$ ball of radius $\tau$, equivalent to PGD with
the Euclidean ball as the constraint set.  This connection places clipping within the
unifying framework of mirror descent with Euclidean Bregman divergence.
\end{remark}

\textbf{Why Adam underperforms Nesterov on Rosenbrock:} Adam applies independent per-coordinate
step-size scaling via $1/\sqrt{\hat{v}_t}$.  For Benchmark C, the Rosenbrock Hessian has a large
off-diagonal term $\nabla^2 f(x)_{12} = -400x_1$, coupling the $(x_1, x_2)$ coordinates.  Adam's
diagonal preconditioning implicitly assumes the Hessian is diagonal, so it cannot compensate for
the off-diagonal coupling.  Formally, the ideal Newton preconditioner for Rosenbrock is the full
inverse Hessian:
\begin{equation}
H(x)^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det H}\begin{bmatrix} 200 & 400x_1 \\ 400x_1 & 2+1200x_1^2-400x_2 \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
which involves cross-coordinate terms.  Adam can only approximate the diagonal blocks
$\mathrm{diag}(H)^{-1}$, missing the coupling.  Nesterov requires no Hessian information but
its accumulating momentum in the valley direction compensates for the curvature coupling.

\textbf{Quantitative rate comparison on Benchmark C:}  GD achieves $f = 3.39$ after 150 iterations.
The $O(1/k^2)$ rate suggests Nesterov should improve the convergence bound by a factor of
$k^2/1 = 150^2/1 = 22{,}500$ relative to GD, though in practice the constant in the bound also
differs; the empirically observed improvement factor is $3.39/0.479 \approx 7\times$, consistent
with the theoretical prediction that acceleration becomes increasingly beneficial as the number of
iterations grows.

\begin{remark}[Continuous-time ODE interpretation of momentum methods]
Gradient descent with step size $\alpha \to 0$ approximates the \emph{gradient flow} ODE
$\dot{x}(t) = -\nabla f(x(t))$, whose solution converges to $x^{\star}$ at rate
$f(x(t)) - f^{\star} \leq C e^{-2\mu t}$ for $\mu$-strongly convex $f$.  The Heavy Ball method
corresponds to a \emph{second-order} ODE:
\begin{equation}
\ddot{x}(t) + \gamma\,\dot{x}(t) + \nabla f(x(t)) = 0,
\label{eq:heavyball_ode}
\end{equation}
which is a \emph{damped harmonic oscillator}: the term $\gamma\dot{x}$ provides friction, while
$\nabla f(x)$ provides the restoring force.  For a quadratic $f$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$, the
solution oscillates as $x(t) \approx x^{\star} + e^{-\gamma t/2}(A\cos(\omega t) + B\sin(\omega t))$
where $\omega = \sqrt{\lambda - \gamma^2/4}$.  \emph{Critical damping} ($\gamma = 2\sqrt{\lambda}$,
no oscillation, fastest approach) mirrors the optimal momentum parameter $\beta^{\star} =
((\sqrt\kappa-1)/(\sqrt\kappa+1))^2$ in the discrete case: for the worst-conditioned eigendirection
(eigenvalue $\mu$), optimal damping is $\gamma^{\star} = 2\sqrt{\mu}$, with the damped Newton rate
$e^{-\sqrt{\mu}t}$ matching Heavy Ball's $O(\sqrt\kappa)$ iteration count.

Nesterov's method, in the continuous limit of Su, Boyd, and Cand\`es (2016), corresponds to a
\emph{time-varying} damping:
\begin{equation}
\ddot{x}(t) + \frac{3}{t}\,\dot{x}(t) + \nabla f(x(t)) = 0.
\label{eq:nesterov_ode}
\end{equation}
The damping coefficient $3/t \to 0$ decreases over time (less friction near the optimum), explaining
the accelerating convergence.  The energy function $E(t) = t^2(f(x(t)) - f^{\star}) +
2\|x(t) + \frac{t}{2}\dot{x}(t) - x^{\star}\|^2$ is non-increasing along trajectories of
\eqref{eq:nesterov_ode}, proving the $O(1/t^2)$ rate.  This continuous-time viewpoint shows that
Nesterov acceleration is \emph{not an ad hoc trick} but the natural discretisation of a specific
damping schedule that balances inertia and friction.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Local Approximations of $g(x) = x^4$}

\textbf{First-order (Taylor) approximation} around $x_0 = 0.25$:
\begin{equation}
g^{(1)}(x) = g(x_0) + g'(x_0)(x - x_0)
            = 0.0039 + 0.0625\,(x - 0.25).
\end{equation}
This tangent-line approximation captures local slope only and diverges rapidly from the true
function outside a small neighbourhood of $x_0$.

\textbf{Second-order (quadratic) approximation:}
\begin{equation}
g^{(2)}(x) = g(x_0) + g'(x_0)(x - x_0) + \tfrac{1}{2}g''(x_0)(x - x_0)^2
            = 0.0039 + 0.0625\,(x - 0.25) + 0.375\,(x - 0.25)^2.
\end{equation}
Minimising $g^{(2)}$ with respect to $x$ gives the Newton step:
$x^+ = x_0 - g'(x_0)/g''(x_0) = 0.25 - 0.0625/0.75 \approx 0.167$.  This is a far better
estimate of the function's zero-gradient point than the gradient-descent step, since the quadratic
model captures the local curvature.  Note that the gradient-descent step from $x_0 = 0.25$ with
step $\alpha$ yields $x^+ = 0.25 - \alpha \cdot 0.0625$; for $\alpha = 0.1$, $x^+ = 0.244$
--- barely any progress.  Newton's step $\approx 0.167$ is $5\times$ larger and directed more
accurately (see Figure~\ref{fig:q3_approx}).

\subsection{Newton's Method with Hessian Regularisation}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Newton's Method with Damping and Regularisation}
\label{alg:newton}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, Hessian damping $\lambda > 0$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $H_k \gets \nabla^2 f(x_k) + \lambda I$ \Comment{Tikhonov regularisation for numerics}
    \State $p_k \gets H_k^{-1}\, g_k$ \Comment{Newton direction}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, p_k$ \Comment{Damped Newton step ($\alpha=1$: pure Newton)}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The regularisation $\lambda I$ ($\lambda = 10^{-8}$) ensures positive definiteness and numerical
stability of the linear solve $H_k p_k = g_k$ without materially affecting the update direction.
For the damped step ($\alpha < 1$), the \emph{Armijo sufficient decrease condition}
$f(x_k - \alpha p_k) \leq f(x_k) - c\alpha\,g_k^T p_k$ (with $c \approx 10^{-4}$) guarantees
descent at each iteration and is used in backtracking line searches.  Here we use fixed $\alpha$
values tuned per benchmark (Table~\ref{tab:q3_params}), which achieves descent in practice
but without a formal sufficient decrease guarantee.

\begin{remark}[Wolfe line-search conditions~\cite{nocedal2006}]
The Armijo condition alone allows the step $\alpha$ to be arbitrarily small (the condition is
satisfied by any $\alpha \in (0, \alpha_0]$ for some $\alpha_0$).  The \emph{strong Wolfe
conditions} add a curvature requirement to prevent excessively short steps:
\begin{align}
f(x_k - \alpha p_k) &\leq f(x_k) - c_1 \alpha\, g_k^T p_k
  \quad \text{(sufficient decrease, }c_1 \approx 10^{-4}\text{)}, \\
|g(x_k - \alpha p_k)^T p_k| &\leq c_2 |g_k^T p_k|
  \quad \text{(curvature condition, }c_2 \approx 0.9\text{)}.
\end{align}
The curvature condition demands that the directional derivative at the new point be at most
$c_2$ times its magnitude at the current point; it is satisfied whenever $\alpha$ lands in a
region where the function has sufficiently ``flattened out'' along $p_k$.  Nocedal and Wright
(Theorem 3.6) prove that a step satisfying both conditions always exists in the open interval
$(0, \bar{\alpha})$ for any descent direction $p_k$ and $L$-smooth $f$.

For \textbf{L-BFGS}, the strong Wolfe conditions are \emph{essential}: they guarantee
$y_j^T s_j = (\nabla f(x_{j+1}) - \nabla f(x_j))^T (x_{j+1} - x_j) > 0$, the positive
curvature condition required so that the rank-2 BFGS update
$H_{k+1}^{-1} = (I - \rho_k s_k y_k^T)H_k^{-1}(I - \rho_k y_k s_k^T) + \rho_k s_k s_k^T$
preserves positive definiteness.  With Armijo alone, a step could land on a slope where
$\nabla f(x_{j+1})^T s_j < 0$, violating the curvature condition and causing the Hessian
approximation to become indefinite.  In our implementation the step size $\alpha$ is fixed,
so this positive curvature guarantee is not enforced; for production L-BFGS code, a Wolfe
line search is mandatory.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Newton's quadratic convergence~\cite{nocedal2006}]
Let $f$ be twice continuously differentiable and $x^{\star}$ be a local minimum with
$\nabla^2 f(x^{\star}) \succ 0$.  If $x_0$ is sufficiently close to $x^{\star}$ and
$\nabla^2 f$ is $L_H$-Lipschitz, then the pure Newton iterates ($\alpha = 1$) satisfy
\begin{equation}
\|x_{k+1} - x^{\star}\| \leq \frac{L_H}{2\mu_H}\|x_k - x^{\star}\|^2,
\end{equation}
where $\mu_H$ is the smallest eigenvalue of $\nabla^2 f(x^{\star})$.  This is \emph{quadratic}
(superlinear) convergence, compared with gradient descent's linear rate $(1 - \mu/L)^k$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Let $e_k = x_k - x^{\star}$.  Since $\nabla f(x^{\star}) = 0$ and $\nabla^2 f$ is $L_H$-Lipschitz,
\begin{equation}
\nabla f(x_k) = \nabla f(x_k) - \nabla f(x^{\star}) = \int_0^1 \nabla^2 f(x^{\star} + te_k)\,e_k\,dt
= \nabla^2 f(x_k)\,e_k + r_k,
\label{eq:newton_grad_expand}
\end{equation}
where $\|r_k\| = \|\int_0^1 [\nabla^2 f(x^{\star}+te_k) - \nabla^2 f(x_k)]e_k\,dt\| \leq
\frac{L_H}{2}\|e_k\|^2$ by Lipschitz continuity of $\nabla^2 f$.  The Newton update gives:
\begin{align}
e_{k+1} &= x_{k+1} - x^{\star} = x_k - [\nabla^2 f(x_k)]^{-1}\nabla f(x_k) - x^{\star} \notag \\
&= e_k - [\nabla^2 f(x_k)]^{-1}(\nabla^2 f(x_k)\,e_k + r_k)
 = -[\nabla^2 f(x_k)]^{-1} r_k.
\end{align}
Taking norms: $\|e_{k+1}\| \leq \|[\nabla^2 f(x_k)]^{-1}\| \cdot \|r_k\| \leq \frac{1}{\mu_H}
\cdot \frac{L_H}{2}\|e_k\|^2 = \frac{L_H}{2\mu_H}\|e_k\|^2$, establishing quadratic convergence.
The constant $C = L_H/(2\mu_H)$ depends on the Hessian's Lipschitz constant $L_H$ and minimum
eigenvalue $\mu_H$ at $x^{\star}$; for Benchmark A (linear Hessian), $L_H = 0$, so Newton
converges in \emph{exactly one step}.
\end{proof}

For a quadratic objective $f(\theta) = \frac{1}{2}\theta^T H \theta - b^T \theta + c$ with constant
Hessian $H$, the pure Newton step is exact:
$\theta_{k+1} = \theta_k - H^{-1}\nabla f(\theta_k) = \theta_k - H^{-1}(H\theta_k - b) = H^{-1}b = \theta^{\star}$,
achieving the global minimum in a \emph{single} step regardless of initial point or condition number.

\begin{corollary}[Newton is condition-number independent for quadratics]
For a strongly convex quadratic objective with condition number $\kappa = L/\mu$, the pure Newton
method ($\alpha = 1$) converges in exactly one iteration regardless of $\kappa$, while gradient
descent requires $O(\kappa\log(1/\varepsilon))$ iterations.  This is confirmed empirically on
Benchmark A ($\kappa \approx 1.3$): Newton converges in one step, GD requires 80 iterations.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[Newton escapes the first-order lower bound via second-order information]
Theorem~\ref{thm:lower_bound} (first-order oracle lower bound) states that no first-order
method can achieve better than $O(1/k^2)$ on general convex functions.  Newton's method
\emph{escapes} this lower bound by accessing a second-order oracle (the Hessian
$\nabla^2 f(x_k)$), which provides strictly more information than gradient evaluations.

Concretely: once $x_k$ is within the \emph{quadratic convergence regime}
$\|x_k - x^{\star}\| \leq \rho^{\star} := \mu_H / L_H$, the Newton iterate satisfies
$\|x_{k+1} - x^{\star}\| \leq \frac{1}{2}\|x_k - x^{\star}\|$, so the error \emph{doubly
halves} at every step.  To reach $\varepsilon$-accuracy from initial error $r_0$, define
$K = \lceil\log_2(r_0/\rho^{\star})\rceil$ damped steps to enter the regime; then
$k^{\star} = \lceil\log_2\log_2(1/\varepsilon)\rceil$ pure Newton steps suffice.  The total
is $O(\log\log(1/\varepsilon))$, which for $\varepsilon = 10^{-16}$ requires only
$k^{\star} \approx \log_2(16) = 4$ Newton steps after entering the quadratic phase ---
exponentially faster than Nesterov's $O(\sqrt\kappa\log(1/\varepsilon))$.

The trade-off is the per-iteration cost: the Hessian oracle provides $O(d^2)$ information
(a symmetric matrix) at cost $O(d^2)$ to compute and $O(d^3)$ to invert, vs.\ $O(d)$ for
a gradient oracle.  For the 2D benchmarks here, this is negligible; for
$d \sim 10^6$, the $O(d^3)$ cost makes full Newton infeasible, forcing approximations
(L-BFGS, Gauss-Newton) that recover superlinear but not quadratic convergence.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Limitations and Practical Considerations}

\textbf{Stopping criteria for Newton's method:}  The standard stopping criterion is the
\emph{Newton decrement}: $\lambda^2(x) = g_k^T H_k^{-1} g_k$, which satisfies
$\lambda^2(x)/2 \leq f(x) - f^{\star}$ (a computable bound on sub-optimality for strongly convex $f$).
Iterating until $\lambda^2 \leq 2\varepsilon$ guarantees $f(x) - f^{\star} \leq \varepsilon$ without
knowing $f^{\star}$~\cite{nocedal2006}.  The gradient norm $\|g_k\|$ is also used in practice
($\|g_k\| \leq \varepsilon$), though this does not provide a direct sub-optimality bound.

\textbf{When Newton fails:}  The analysis above assumes that $\nabla^2 f(x)$ is positive definite
along the trajectory.  In non-convex regions, the Hessian may have negative eigenvalues, making the
Newton direction non-descending.  The regularisation $\lambda I$ (used here with $\lambda = 10^{-8}$)
mitigates this but may be insufficient far from the optimum.  In practice, a trust-region or
line-search globalisation strategy is needed.

\textbf{Cost of Newton's method:} Each Newton iteration requires computing the full Hessian
($O(d^2)$ entries), solving a $d \times d$ linear system ($O(d^3)$ via Gaussian elimination), and
evaluating the gradient ($O(d)$ or $O(md)$ for data-dependent objectives).  For $d = 2$ parameters
this is negligible, but for large-scale problems ($d \sim 10^6$) the $O(d^3)$ Hessian inversion is
completely intractable.

\textbf{Trust-region methods:}  An alternative to line-search globalisation is the trust-region
framework: at each step, build a quadratic model $m_k(p) = f(x_k) + g_k^T p + \frac{1}{2}p^T
H_k p$ and restrict the step to a ball $\|p\| \leq \Delta_k$.  The subproblem
$\min_{\|p\| \leq \Delta_k} m_k(p)$ has a closed form (the dogleg or CG-Steihaug step) and is
solved at each iteration.  The trust-region radius $\Delta_k$ is expanded or contracted based
on the ratio $\rho_k = (f(x_k) - f(x_k + p_k))/(m_k(0) - m_k(p_k))$ of actual to predicted
decrease.  Trust-region methods are globally convergent (even from far from the optimum) and
do not require the Hessian to be positive definite, making them more robust than line-search
Newton for highly non-convex problems~\cite{nocedal2006}.

\begin{remark}[Self-concordant functions and global Newton convergence]
For the special class of \emph{self-concordant functions} --- those satisfying
$|D^3f(x)[h,h,h]| \leq 2(D^2f(x)[h,h])^{3/2}$ for all $h$ (informally, the third derivative
is controlled by the Hessian) --- Newton's method with backtracking line search is
\emph{globally} convergent with a bound on the number of damped steps that depends only on
$f(x_0) - f^{\star}$, not on the condition number~\cite{nocedal2006}.  Specifically:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Damped phase}: each step reduces $f(x) - f^{\star}$ by at least a constant
  $\delta_0 > 0$, so at most $(f(x_0) - f^{\star})/\delta_0$ steps are needed.
\item \textbf{Quadratic phase}: once $\lambda(x) = \sqrt{g^T H^{-1} g} < 1/4$, the method
  enters the quadratic convergence regime with rate $\|e_{k+1}\| \leq 2\|e_k\|^2$.
\end{itemize}
Important self-concordant functions include: $-\log\det(X)$ (used in semidefinite programming),
logarithmic barriers $-\sum_i \log(a_i^Tx - b_i)$ (interior-point methods), and all strongly
convex quadratics.  For \emph{non}-self-concordant functions like Rosenbrock, the global
convergence guarantee fails: the third derivative can be large relative to the Hessian,
which is why strong damping ($\alpha = 0.22$) is required rather than a theoretically-guaranteed
fixed number of damped steps.
\end{remark}

\textbf{Quasi-Newton alternatives:} Limited-memory BFGS (L-BFGS) approximates the inverse Hessian
using a low-rank update from the last $m$ gradient differences, achieving superlinear (but not
quadratic) convergence at $O(md)$ cost per iteration.  This makes it practical for moderate-scale
problems ($d \sim 10^4$--$10^5$)~\cite{nocedal2006}.  For the 2D benchmarks here, full Newton
is the natural choice; for higher-dimensional extensions, L-BFGS would be preferable.

\textbf{Gauss--Newton for least-squares:} For objectives of the form $f(\theta) = \frac{1}{2}\|r(\theta)\|^2$
with residual $r : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^n$, the exact Hessian is
$\nabla^2 f = J^T J + \sum_i r_i \nabla^2 r_i$, where $J = \partial r / \partial \theta$ is the
Jacobian.  The Gauss--Newton approximation drops the second-order term:
$\nabla^2 f \approx J^T J$, which is always positive semi-definite and requires computing only
the Jacobian (not second derivatives of $r$).  For linear $r$ (linear regression, Benchmark A),
this is exact: $\nabla^2 J = \frac{1}{m}X^TX = H$.  Gauss--Newton converges quadratically near
the solution when the residual is small.

The L-BFGS update maintains two history arrays $\{s_j, y_j\}_{j=k-m}^{k-1}$ where
$s_j = x_{j+1} - x_j$ and $y_j = \nabla f(x_{j+1}) - \nabla f(x_j)$, and computes the Newton
direction $p_k = H_k^{-1}g_k$ via two passes over the history using the
\emph{two-loop recursion}~\cite{nocedal2006}.  The result is an approximation to the true
Newton direction that becomes exact as the history length $m \to d$.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{L-BFGS Two-Loop Recursion (computes $p_k = H_k^{-1}g_k$)}
\label{alg:lbfgs}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Gradient $g \gets \nabla f(x_k)$; history $\{(s_j, y_j)\}$, $j = k{-}m,\ldots,k{-}1$
where $s_j = x_{j+1}-x_j$, $y_j = \nabla f(x_{j+1}) - \nabla f(x_j)$
\State $q \gets g$ \Comment{Backward pass}
\For{$j = k-1$ \textbf{downto} $k-m$}
    \State $\rho_j \gets 1\,/\,(y_j^T s_j)$ \Comment{Curvature scaling (requires $y_j^T s_j > 0$)}
    \State $\alpha_j \gets \rho_j\, s_j^T q$
    \State $q \gets q - \alpha_j\, y_j$
\EndFor
\State $\gamma_k \gets s_{k-1}^T y_{k-1}\,/\,\|y_{k-1}\|^2$ \Comment{Initial Hessian scaling}
\State $r \gets \gamma_k\, q$ \Comment{Forward pass}
\For{$j = k-m$ \textbf{to} $k-1$}
    \State $\beta_j \gets \rho_j\, y_j^T r$
    \State $r \gets r + s_j(\alpha_j - \beta_j)$
\EndFor
\State \Return $p_k = r$ \quad (then $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\,p_k$ with Wolfe line search)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Each pass iterates over the $m$ history pairs in $O(md)$ operations (vs $O(d^3)$ for a dense
solve).  The curvature condition $y_j^T s_j > 0$ must hold at each step; if violated (e.g.\ near
a saddle point), the offending pair is discarded.  Typical $m = 5$--$20$ is sufficient for
superlinear convergence.  The initial scaling $\gamma_k$ sets the implicit Hessian to
$\gamma_k^{-1} I$ before the rank-$2m$ update, matching the most recent curvature seen.

\subsection{Part A: Finite Differences and Nesterov Random Search (Benchmark B)}

\subsubsection{Finite Difference Gradient Approximation}

The \emph{forward} finite-difference approximation of $\partial f / \partial x_i$ is:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x) \approx \frac{f(x + \delta e_i) - f(x)}{\delta}
= \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x) + \frac{\delta}{2}f_{ii}''(x) + O(\delta^2).
\label{eq:fdfwd}
\end{equation}
The approximation error has two competing contributions:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Truncation error}: $O(\delta)$ from the omitted higher-order Taylor terms.
\item \textbf{Round-off error}: $O(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}} / \delta)$ from floating-point
  cancellation in $f(x + \delta e_i) - f(x)$ when $\delta$ is small.
\end{itemize}
Balancing these two contributions (minimising $C_1 \delta + C_2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}/\delta$)
gives the optimal perturbation:
\begin{equation}
\delta_{\mathrm{opt}}^{\mathrm{fwd}} \approx \sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}} \approx 10^{-8}.
\label{eq:delta_opt_fwd}
\end{equation}

The \emph{central} finite-difference formula removes the first-order truncation term:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x) \approx \frac{f(x + \delta e_i) - f(x - \delta e_i)}{2\delta}
= \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x) + \frac{\delta^2}{6}f_{iii}'''(x) + O(\delta^4),
\label{eq:fdcen}
\end{equation}
giving $O(\delta^2)$ truncation error at the cost of one additional function evaluation per
coordinate.  The optimal central-FD step is
$\delta_{\mathrm{opt}}^{\mathrm{cen}} \approx \varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}^{1/3} \approx 10^{-5}$,
achieving lower overall error than forward FD at the same $\delta$.
The experiment tests $\delta = 0.05$ (near good regime) and $\delta = 0.8$ (far too large,
dominated by truncation error).  For $\delta = 0.05$, the forward FD error is
$O(0.05) \approx 0.05f_{ii}''(x)/2$, while for $\delta = 0.8$ it is $O(0.8) = 0.4f_{ii}''(x)$,
an $8\times$ larger approximation error.

\subsubsection{Nesterov Random Search}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Nesterov Random Search (Zeroth-Order)}
\label{alg:nrs}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, perturbation $\delta$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State Sample $u_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)$, normalise: $u_k \gets u_k / \|u_k\|$
    \State $\hat{g}_k \gets \dfrac{f(x_k + \delta u_k) - f(x_k)}{\delta}\, u_k$
    \Comment{Directional derivative estimate}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \hat{g}_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

This zeroth-order method requires only \emph{two} function evaluations per iteration regardless of
dimension $d$.  The directional derivative $\hat{g}_k$ is a noisy estimate of the gradient:
$\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_k] = \nabla f_{\delta}(x_k)$ where $f_{\delta}$ is the Gaussian-smoothed
objective with smoothing parameter $\delta$~\cite{nesterov2017}:
\begin{equation}
f_{\delta}(x) = \mathbb{E}_{u \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)}[f(x + \delta u)]
\approx f(x) + \frac{\delta^2}{2}\operatorname{tr}(\nabla^2 f(x)),
\end{equation}
with the approximation holding for small $\delta$.  The bias $f_{\delta}(x) - f(x) = O(\delta^2)$
represents the trade-off between approximation quality and noise in the gradient estimate.
The convergence rate is $O(d/k)$ --- a factor of
$d$ slower than gradient-based methods --- reflecting the information loss from using a single
random direction instead of the full $d$-dimensional gradient.

\begin{remark}
The $O(d/k)$ rate can be interpreted as follows: the random direction $u_k \in \mathbb{R}^d$
contributes only $1/d$ of the gradient information on average (in expectation over uniformly random
directions, $\mathbb{E}[u_k u_k^T] = I_d/d$).  Consequently, $d$ random-search steps are
needed to accumulate the same gradient signal as one exact gradient evaluation, explaining the
dimension-dependent slow-down.  For $d = 2$ (our benchmarks), the factor-of-2 penalty is modest;
for $d = 1000$, random search would be $500\times$ slower than gradient descent per unit of
gradient information.
\end{remark}

\textbf{SPSA (Simultaneous Perturbation Stochastic Approximation):}  A related zeroth-order
method that also uses 2 function evaluations per step is SPSA~\cite{nocedal2006}, which uses
a random $\pm 1$ Bernoulli perturbation vector $\Delta_k$ (each coordinate independently
$\pm 1$ with equal probability):
\begin{equation}
\hat{g}_k^{(i)} = \frac{f(x_k + \delta_k \Delta_k) - f(x_k - \delta_k \Delta_k)}{2\delta_k \Delta_k^{(i)}}.
\end{equation}
Unlike NRS which uses a Gaussian direction, SPSA perturbs all coordinates simultaneously,
giving an estimate with $\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_k] \approx \nabla f(x_k)$ (central difference
of a random direction).  SPSA also uses 2 function evaluations per step regardless of $d$,
and has been shown to converge at the same $O(d/k)$ rate as NRS.  The advantage of Bernoulli
vs Gaussian perturbations is that each coordinate is always perturbed (never zero), which can
be more robust in practice when some coordinates have very small gradients.

\textbf{Optimal perturbation $\delta$ for NRS:}  Similar to finite differences, the NRS
gradient estimator has bias $O(\delta^2)$ from the smoothing $f_\delta(x)$ and estimation
variance $O(\|g\|^2 / \delta^2)$ from using a single random direction.  Balancing these
(minimising $C_1 \delta^2 + C_2/(\delta^2 k)$ over $\delta$) gives the optimal schedule
$\delta_k \propto k^{-1/4}$, achieving total error $O(d/\sqrt{k})$.  For the fixed $\delta =
0.08$ used in the experiment, the bias is $O(0.0064)$ --- small relative to the initial error
but non-vanishing, so the method will not converge to the exact minimum with fixed $\delta$.

\begin{remark}[Automatic differentiation as the gold standard]
Modern deep learning frameworks (PyTorch, JAX, TensorFlow) implement \emph{automatic
differentiation} (AD): by composing elementary operations and tracking the computation graph,
AD computes exact gradients to machine precision at a cost of $O(1)$ forward passes for
reverse-mode AD (backpropagation), compared to $O(d)$ evaluations for finite differences.
The key advantage is that AD is \emph{exact} (no truncation or round-off error from
differencing) and scales to $d \sim 10^9$ parameters.  In the assignment, exact gradients
are provided analytically for all benchmarks; if only function values were available, finite
differences or random search would be required.  Automatic differentiation represents the
reason that derivative-free methods are rarely used in deep learning, where AD is the default.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Complex-step differentiation]
An alternative to finite differences that avoids catastrophic cancellation entirely is the
\emph{complex-step method}: for a function $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ extended analytically
to the complex plane,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x) \approx \frac{\operatorname{Im}[f(x + ih\,e_i)]}{h},
\end{equation}
with error $O(h^2)$ from the Taylor series but \emph{no} subtraction of nearly equal quantities.
The imaginary perturbation never cancels with the real part, so the method achieves near-analytic
accuracy for any $h \gg \varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}$ (e.g.\ $h = 10^{-200}$).  The cost is $d + 1$
complex function evaluations per gradient --- identical to forward FD in count but dramatically
more accurate.  The method requires the objective implementation to accept complex arguments,
which is satisfied by many scientific computing libraries.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Part B: Derivative-Free Methods on Rosenbrock (Benchmark C)}

\subsubsection{Nelder--Mead Simplex Method}

The Nelder--Mead algorithm maintains a simplex of $n+1 = 3$ vertices in $\mathbb{R}^2$ and
iteratively transforms it through four geometric operations:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Reflection} ($\alpha_r = 1.0$): move worst vertex through the centroid of the
  remaining vertices: $x_r = \bar{x} + \alpha_r(\bar{x} - x_{\mathrm{worst}})$.
\item \textbf{Expansion} ($\gamma = 2.0$): if reflection improved, try further:
  $x_e = \bar{x} + \gamma(\bar{x} - x_{\mathrm{worst}})$.
\item \textbf{Contraction} ($\rho = 0.5$): if reflection worsened, contract:
  $x_c = \bar{x} + \rho(x_r \text{ or } x_{\mathrm{worst}} - \bar{x})$.
\item \textbf{Shrink} ($\sigma = 0.5$): if contraction also fails, contract all vertices toward
  the best: $x_i \gets x_{\mathrm{best}} + \sigma(x_i - x_{\mathrm{best}})$.
\end{enumerate}

No gradient information is required.  Initial simplex step $= 0.35$, iterations $= 160$.

\textbf{Numerical illustration:}  Starting from $x_0 = (-1, 1)$ with initial step $0.35$, the
three simplex vertices are $v_0 = (-1, 1)$, $v_1 = (-0.65, 1)$, $v_2 = (-1, 1.35)$, with
function values $f(v_0) \approx 4.0$, $f(v_1) \approx 36.1$, $f(v_2) \approx 16.25$.
(Note $v_1$ is far above the parabola $x_2 = x_1^2$, hence the large $f$ value.)
In the first iteration: (1) the \emph{worst} vertex is $v_1 = (-0.65, 1)$ with $f \approx 36.1$;
(2) the centroid of the remaining vertices $\{v_0, v_2\} = (-1, 1.175)$; (3) the reflection
gives $x_r = 2(-1, 1.175) - (-0.65, 1) = (-1.35, 1.35)$ with $f(x_r) \approx 27.9 < 36.1$
--- an improvement, so $x_r$ replaces $v_1$.  The simplex then continues to explore and
contract towards the parabolic valley $x_2 \approx x_1^2$, navigating 160 such iterations
to eventually reach $f \approx 0.12$.

\textbf{Convergence properties:} Nelder--Mead has no general convergence guarantee for dimensions
$d \geq 2$~\cite{lagarias1998}: it is possible to construct examples where the simplex contracts
to a non-stationary point.  In practice, however, it performs well on smooth low-dimensional
functions.  The algorithm's power comes from its \emph{geometry-adaptive} nature: the simplex
contracts, expands, and reflects to match the local curvature of the objective without any
gradient computation.  On the Rosenbrock banana-shaped valley, the simplex must progressively
rotate to align with the valley direction, which takes many operations but ultimately succeeds
without any derivative information --- a feat impossible for gradient-free methods that do not
maintain a geometric model of the search region.

\subsubsection{Grid Search}

Grid search evaluates the objective on a uniform $55 \times 55 = 3{,}025$ grid over
$x_1 \in [-2, 2]$, $x_2 \in [-1, 3]$.  Grid resolution: $\Delta x \approx 0.073$.

\textbf{Curse of dimensionality:} For a $d$-dimensional problem with $N$ grid points per
dimension, the total evaluation cost is $N^d$.  Achieving resolution $\Delta x = 0.073$ over
a unit interval requires $N = 1/0.073 \approx 14$ points.  For $d = 2$ the cost is $14^2 = 196$;
for $d = 10$ it is $14^{10} \approx 2.9 \times 10^{11}$; for $d = 100$ it is astronomically
large.  Grid search is therefore restricted to $d \leq 3$ in practice, motivating gradient-based
and derivative-free iterative methods for higher-dimensional problems.

\begin{remark}[Quasi-Monte Carlo as an intermediate between grid and random search]
Pure random search samples $\hat{g} \approx \frac{f(x+\delta u)-f(x)}{\delta}u$ with
random directions $u$, achieving $O(1/\sqrt{N})$ error after $N$ evaluations (by the
central limit theorem).  \emph{Quasi-Monte Carlo} (QMC) methods replace pseudo-random
samples with \emph{low-discrepancy sequences} (Sobol, Halton, Faure) that fill the search
space more uniformly than random sampling.  For a smooth integrand $f$, QMC achieves
$O((\log N)^d / N)$ error (the Koksma--Hlawka inequality), substantially better than
$O(1/\sqrt{N})$ for moderate $d$.  For $d = 2$ and $N = 3025$ grid evaluations:
grid achieves $O(\Delta x^2) = O(0.005)$ bias but costs $N^d/N = N$ evaluations;
random search with the same budget $N$ achieves $O(1/\sqrt{3025}) = O(0.018)$ standard
error; QMC achieves $O((\log 3025)^2/3025) = O(0.009)$, intermediate.

QMC is most effective when $d$ is small ($d \leq 10$) and the function is smooth:
for rough or discontinuous objectives, the smoothness assumption breaks down and QMC
loses its advantage over Monte Carlo.  For the benchmarks here ($d = 2$, smooth), QMC
would provide a modest improvement over random search, but gradient-based methods still
dominate both due to their $O(1/k)$ convergence vs $O(N^{-1/2})$ for random.
\end{remark}

\textbf{Bayesian optimisation as a sample-efficient alternative:}  When function evaluations

evaluations so far).  Unlike Nelder--Mead and random search, BO actively models uncertainty
and trades off exploration vs.\ exploitation --- making it the state of the art for expensive
black-box objective functions.

\subsection{Problem Setup and KKT Conditions}

The constrained problem is:
\begin{equation}
\min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^2}\; f(x) = (x_1-1)^2 + 5(x_2-2)^2 + \sin(x_1)
\quad \text{subject to}\quad x_1 \geq 0.5,
\label{eq:constrained}
\end{equation}
starting from the infeasible point $x_0 = (0.2, 4.0)$.

\textbf{Slater's condition (constraint qualification):}  Benchmark B satisfies Slater's condition
since the strictly feasible point $x = (1, 2)$ exists: $x_1 = 1 > 0.5$.  This is a \emph{regularity
condition} (constraint qualification) that ensures strong duality and the sufficiency of KKT
conditions.  Without it, the KKT conditions would be necessary but not sufficient.

\begin{theorem}[KKT optimality conditions~\cite{nocedal2006}]
\label{thm:kkt}
Consider the general inequality-constrained problem
$\min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^d} f(x)$ subject to $g_i(x) \leq 0$ for $i = 1,\ldots,m$.
Suppose $f$ and $g_i$ are continuously differentiable and a constraint qualification
(e.g.\ Slater's condition: $\exists\, \tilde{x}$ with $g_i(\tilde{x}) < 0$ for all $i$) holds.
Then $x^{\star}$ is a local minimum only if there exist multipliers $\mu^{\star} \in \mathbb{R}^m$
satisfying:
\begin{align}
\nabla f(x^{\star}) + \sum_{i=1}^m \mu_i^{\star}\nabla g_i(x^{\star}) &= 0
  \qquad\text{(stationarity)}, \label{eq:kkt_gen_stat}\\
g_i(x^{\star}) &\leq 0 \quad \forall i
  \qquad\text{(primal feasibility)}, \label{eq:kkt_gen_primal}\\
\mu_i^{\star} &\geq 0 \quad \forall i
  \qquad\text{(dual feasibility)}, \label{eq:kkt_gen_dual}\\
\mu_i^{\star}\, g_i(x^{\star}) &= 0 \quad \forall i
  \qquad\text{(complementary slackness)}. \label{eq:kkt_gen_cs}
\end{align}
If $f$ and $g_i$ are convex and Slater's condition holds, the KKT conditions are
also \emph{sufficient}: any point satisfying them is a \emph{global} minimum.
\end{theorem}

For problem~\eqref{eq:constrained}, the single constraint is $g_1(x) = 0.5 - x_1 \leq 0$,
so $\nabla g_1 = -e_1$.  Substituting into the KKT stationarity condition gives
$\nabla f(x^{\star}) - \mu^{\star} e_1 = 0$ as below.

The Karush--Kuhn--Tucker conditions for~\eqref{eq:constrained} are:
\begin{align}
\nabla f(x^{\star}) - \mu^{\star} e_1 &= 0, \label{eq:kkt_stat} \\
x_1^{\star} - 0.5 &\geq 0, \label{eq:kkt_primal} \\
\mu^{\star} &\geq 0, \label{eq:kkt_dual} \\
\mu^{\star}(x_1^{\star} - 0.5) &= 0. \label{eq:kkt_comp}
\end{align}
Here $e_1 = [1, 0]^T$ and $\mu^{\star}$ is the KKT multiplier.  The unconstrained minimum of
Benchmark B is at $(0.582, 2.0)$, which satisfies $x_1^{\star} = 0.582 > 0.5$ --- hence the
constraint is \emph{inactive} at the constrained optimum, $\mu^{\star} = 0$, and the constrained
and unconstrained solutions coincide: $x^{\star} = (0.582, 2.0)$, $f^{\star} \approx 0.7244$.

\textbf{Dual variable as shadow price:} When the constraint is \emph{active} (e.g.\ if the
unconstrained minimum had $x_1^{\star} < 0.5$), the optimal KKT multiplier $\mu^{\star} > 0$
would equal the \emph{shadow price}: the rate at which the optimal value changes with the
constraint bound, $\mu^{\star} = -\partial f^{\star}/\partial b$ where $b = 0.5$.  A larger
$\mu^{\star}$ indicates the constraint is more binding --- relaxing it would yield a greater
improvement in the objective.  For our problem, $\mu^{\star} = 0$ confirms the constraint does
not limit the optimal value.

\begin{remark}[Active constraint KKT example]
To illustrate the active constraint case, consider the \emph{shifted} objective
$\tilde{f}(x) = (x_1 + 0.5)^2 + 5(x_2 - 2)^2$ whose unconstrained minimum is at
$(-0.5, 2)$ --- outside the feasible region $x_1 \geq 0.5$.  The constrained optimum is
$\tilde{x}^{\star} = (0.5, 2)$ (on the boundary), with $\tilde{f}(\tilde{x}^{\star}) = 1$.
Checking the KKT conditions:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Stationarity}: $\nabla\tilde{f}(\tilde{x}^{\star}) = [2(0.5+0.5),\;0]^T = [2, 0]^T = \mu^{\star} e_1$,
  so $\mu^{\star} = 2$.
\item \textbf{Dual feasibility}: $\mu^{\star} = 2 > 0$ \checkmark.
\item \textbf{Primal feasibility}: $\tilde{x}_1^{\star} = 0.5 \geq 0.5$ \checkmark.
\item \textbf{Complementary slackness}: $\mu^{\star}(\tilde{x}_1^{\star} - 0.5) = 2 \cdot 0 = 0$ \checkmark.
\end{itemize}
All four KKT conditions are satisfied.  The shadow price $\mu^{\star} = 2$ quantifies the
sensitivity: relaxing the lower bound from $x_1 \geq 0.5$ to $x_1 \geq 0.5 - \varepsilon$
reduces the optimal value by $\approx \mu^{\star}\varepsilon = 2\varepsilon$.  More precisely,
the new optimum is $(\tilde{x}_1^{\star})' = 0.5 - \varepsilon$ with
$\tilde{f}(0.5-\varepsilon, 2) = (0.5-\varepsilon+0.5)^2 = (1-\varepsilon)^2
= 1 - 2\varepsilon + O(\varepsilon^2)$, confirming the first-order sensitivity $\partial f^{\star}/\partial b = -\mu^{\star} = -2$.
In the original Benchmark B, the constraint is inactive ($\mu^{\star} = 0$) because the
unconstrained minimum is already feasible.
\end{remark}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Projected Gradient Descent (PGD)}
\label{alg:pgd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, projection $\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \nabla f(x_k)$ \Comment{Unconstrained gradient step}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets \Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(z_{k+1})$ \Comment{Project onto feasible set: $x_1 \leftarrow \max(0.5, x_1)$}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The projection onto $\{x : x_1 \geq 0.5\}$ is simply $[\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(z)]_1 = \max(0.5, z_1)$,
$[\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(z)]_2 = z_2$ --- a $O(1)$ operation.  For general convex sets $\mathcal{X}$,
computing the projection can be expensive, but it guarantees \emph{exact} feasibility at every iterate.

\begin{theorem}[PGD convergence rate~\cite{nocedal2006}]
Let $f$ be $\mu$-strongly convex and $L$-smooth over $\mathcal{X}$.  Projected gradient descent
with $\alpha = 1/L$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
\|x_k - x^{\star}\|^2 \leq \left(1 - \frac{\mu}{L}\right)^k \|x_0 - x^{\star}\|^2.
\end{equation}
The convergence rate $1 - \mu/L$ is \emph{identical} to unconstrained gradient descent.
Feasibility does not degrade the convergence speed when the projection is non-expansive
($\|\Pi(x) - \Pi(y)\| \leq \|x - y\|$), which holds for all convex sets.
\end{theorem}

This confirms that for the halfspace constraint $x_1 \geq 0.5$, PGD converges at the same
rate as unconstrained GD on Benchmark B, with the only additional cost being the trivial
$\max$ operation at each step.

\begin{remark}[Proximal gradient as a generalisation of PGD]
Projected GD is a special case of the \emph{proximal gradient method} for composite objectives
$\min f(x) + g(x)$ where $g$ is convex but non-smooth.  The proximal operator is
$\mathrm{prox}_{\alpha g}(z) = \arg\min_x\{g(x) + \frac{1}{2\alpha}\|x - z\|^2\}$; for
$g = \iota_{\mathcal{X}}$ (the indicator function of $\mathcal{X}$, zero inside, $+\infty$
outside), the prox operator reduces to the Euclidean projection $\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}$.  The
ISTA/FISTA algorithms extend this to general $g$ (e.g.\ $g(x) = \|x\|_1$ for sparse recovery),
with FISTA achieving the same $O(1/k^2)$ rate as Nesterov acceleration~\cite{nocedal2006}.
\end{remark}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Quadratic Penalty Method}
\label{alg:penalty}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, penalty weight $\lambda > 0$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $F_{\lambda}(x) \gets f(x) + \lambda \max(0,\; 0.5 - x_1)$ \Comment{Penalised objective}
    \State $\nabla F_{\lambda}(x) \gets \nabla f(x) + \lambda \cdot \mathbf{1}[x_1 < 0.5]\,(-e_1)$
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \nabla F_{\lambda}(x_k)$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The penalty method replaces the hard constraint with a soft penalty: violations are penalised
proportionally to $\lambda \max(0, 0.5 - x_1)$.  As $\lambda \to \infty$, the penalty solution
approaches the constrained solution, but large $\lambda$ introduces a stiff gradient and requires
smaller step sizes.  Unlike projected GD, the penalty method does \emph{not} guarantee feasibility
at intermediate iterates.

Settings: Projected GD ($\alpha = 0.08$); Penalty: $\lambda \in \{0.15, 1.8, 4.5\}$
($\alpha = 0.05$ for $\lambda \leq 1.8$; $\alpha = 0.03$ for $\lambda = 4.5$); unconstrained GD
($\alpha = 0.07$). All run for 100 iterations.

\textbf{Projected GD} enforces feasibility as a hard constraint at every iteration.  This is the
fundamental advantage: the iterate is always feasible, which is critical in settings where
infeasible points cannot even be evaluated (e.g.\ the objective is only defined on $\mathcal{X}$).
The cost is requiring a projection oracle, which for simple constraints like $x_1 \geq 0.5$ is
trivial but can be expensive for complex (e.g.\ semidefinite) constraint sets.

\textbf{Penalty methods} trade exact feasibility for implementation simplicity.  The weight
$\lambda$ controls the trade-off: small $\lambda$ under-penalises violations; large $\lambda$
enforces feasibility but introduces a stiff gradient term, requiring smaller step sizes for
stability (here $\alpha = 0.03$ for $\lambda = 4.5$ vs.\ $\alpha = 0.05$ for $\lambda \leq 1.8$).
\textbf{Quantitative ill-conditioning from penalty:}  The penalised objective has Hessian
$H_{\lambda} = \nabla^2 f + \lambda \mathbf{1}[x_1 = 0.5]\,e_1 e_1^T$ near the boundary.
For the Benchmark B diagonal Hessian $\nabla^2 f \approx \mathrm{diag}(2.4, 10)$ at the
minimum, adding penalty weight $\lambda$ gives $H_{\lambda} \approx \mathrm{diag}(2.4 + \lambda,
10)$, with condition number $\kappa_\lambda = (2.4 + \lambda)/2.4 \cdot \kappa_0$.  For
$\lambda = 4.5$: $\kappa_\lambda = (2.4 + 4.5)/2.4 \approx 2.9$; for $\lambda = 100$:
$\kappa_\lambda \approx 43$.  The stability condition $\alpha \leq 2/\lambda_{\max}$ requires
$\alpha \leq 2/(2.4 + \lambda)$.  For $\lambda = 4.5$: $\alpha \leq 0.29$; for $\lambda = 100$:
$\alpha \leq 0.020$ --- an order of magnitude smaller step.  This quantitative scaling confirms
that augmented Lagrangian (fixed moderate $\lambda$, dual variable updated) avoids the $O(\lambda)$
ill-conditioning that makes large-$\lambda$ penalty methods slow.

\textbf{Interior-point (barrier) methods} provide a third paradigm: replace the constraint
$x_1 \geq 0.5$ with a logarithmic barrier term $-\frac{1}{t}\log(x_1 - 0.5)$ added to the
objective, with barrier parameter $t \to \infty$~\cite{bertsekas1999}.  This keeps all iterates
strictly feasible while driving them towards the boundary of the optimal region.  Interior-point
methods achieve $O(\sqrt{d} \log(1/\varepsilon))$ Newton iterations for linear and second-order
cone programs, making them the method of choice for large-scale structured problems.

\textbf{Augmented Lagrangian (Method of Multipliers):} An improved alternative builds a dual
variable $\mu_k$ (Lagrange multiplier estimate) alongside the penalty:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(x, \mu) = f(x) + \mu\max(0, 0.5-x_1) + \frac{\lambda}{2}\max(0, 0.5-x_1)^2.
\end{equation}
The multiplier is updated as $\mu_{k+1} = \mu_k + \lambda\max(0, 0.5 - x_k^{(1)})$ after each
inner minimisation.  With a fixed moderate $\lambda$, the augmented Lagrangian converges to the
exact constrained solution without requiring $\lambda \to \infty$, avoiding the ill-conditioning
of the pure penalty approach~\cite{bertsekas1999}.

\begin{proposition}[Augmented Lagrangian convergence~\cite{bertsekas1999}]
For the constrained problem~\eqref{eq:constrained} satisfying Slater's condition, the multiplier
update $\mu_{k+1} = \mu_k + \lambda \max(0, 0.5 - x_k^{(1)})$ converges to the KKT multiplier
$\mu^{\star}$ at a linear rate:
$|\mu_k - \mu^{\star}| \leq (1 - \lambda\mu_f^{-1})^k |\mu_0 - \mu^{\star}|$,
where $\mu_f$ is the strong convexity constant of $f$ (here $\approx 2$).
The key insight is that dual-variable updates provide the ``memory'' of constraint violations
across iterations, allowing the algorithm to converge to the exact feasible solution without
increasing $\lambda$.  This contrasts with pure penalty methods, where $\lambda$ must grow
without bound to achieve exact feasibility, causing the condition number of the penalised
problem to grow as $O(\lambda)$.
\end{proposition}

This is the theoretical justification for why large penalty weights must be accompanied by
smaller step sizes in the experiments.

\begin{remark}[Saddle-point / min-max perspective]
Problem~\eqref{eq:constrained} is equivalent to a \emph{saddle-point} (min-max) problem:
\begin{equation}
\min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^2}\; \max_{\mu \geq 0}\;
\mathcal{L}(x, \mu) = f(x) + \mu(0.5 - x_1).
\end{equation}
The inner maximisation $\max_{\mu \geq 0} [f(x) + \mu(0.5-x_1)]$ evaluates to $+\infty$ when
$x_1 < 0.5$ (the constraint is violated, so the infeasibility can be made arbitrarily expensive
by increasing $\mu$) and to $f(x)$ when $x_1 \geq 0.5$ (the constraint is satisfied, so
$\mu = 0$ is optimal).  Hence $\max_\mu \mathcal{L}(x,\mu)$ equals the constrained objective,
and minimising over $x$ recovers the constrained problem.  Under Slater's condition (the strictly
feasible point $(1, 2)$ exists), \emph{strong duality} holds:
\begin{equation}
\min_{x}\;\max_{\mu \geq 0}\;\mathcal{L}(x, \mu)
= \max_{\mu \geq 0}\;\min_{x}\;\mathcal{L}(x, \mu)
= \max_{\mu \geq 0}\; q(\mu),
\end{equation}
where $q(\mu) = \min_x \mathcal{L}(x, \mu)$ is the \emph{dual function}.  The augmented
Lagrangian solves the modified saddle-point problem with the extra penalty term
$(\lambda/2)(0.5-x_1)^2$, alternating between a primal minimisation over $x$ (for fixed
$\mu_k$) and a dual gradient ascent step $\mu_{k+1} = \max(0, \mu_k + \lambda(0.5 - x_k^{(1)}))$.
This simultaneous convergence of primal and dual variables explains why the augmented Lagrangian
avoids the ill-conditioning of the pure penalty method: the dual variable $\mu_k$ ``learns'' the
correct shadow price, so $\lambda$ need not go to infinity.
\end{remark}

\textbf{ADMM (Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers):}  For problems that can be split
as $\min_{x,z}\; f(x) + g(z)$ subject to $Ax + Bz = c$, the ADMM algorithm alternates
$x$-minimisation, $z$-minimisation, and a dual update, applying the augmented Lagrangian idea
in a decomposed form.  ADMM is particularly powerful for distributed optimisation: each
$x$-block can be updated in parallel on separate machines, with only the dual variable
exchanged.  Empirically, ADMM converges to modest accuracy rapidly but may be slow to reach
high precision~\cite{bertsekas1999}.  For our problem~\eqref{eq:constrained}, ADMM would
split the variable as $x = (x_1, x_2)$ and $z = x_1$, with constraint $x_1 = z$ and
$g(z) = \iota_{[0.5, +\infty)}(z)$ --- a decomposition that enables parallel $x$-update
and a closed-form $z$-update (clipping).

\subsection{Feasible Set and Linear Programme}

Feasible set: $\mathcal{X} = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^2 : 0.5 \leq x_1 \leq 5,\; -5 \leq x_2 \leq 10\}$
(a bounded box / polytope).

\textbf{Linear Programme:} For $f(x) = a^T x$ with $a = [1, 2]^T$, minimise over $\mathcal{X}$.
Since $f$ is linear (hence convex), the minimum is attained at an extreme point (vertex) of
$\mathcal{X}$:
\begin{equation}
\min_{x \in \mathcal{X}}\; x_1 + 2x_2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
x^{\star} = (0.5,\; -5), \qquad f^{\star} = 0.5 + 2(-5) = -9.5.
\end{equation}
This follows directly from the fundamental theorem of linear programming: the optimum of a linear
objective over a bounded polytope is always attained at a vertex~\cite{vanderbei2014}.  Here the
positive coefficients in $a$ push both coordinates to their lower bounds.  The dual problem
confirms: the optimal dual variables (KKT multipliers) for the bounds $x_i \geq l_i$ are
$\mu_i^{\star} = a_i > 0$ for active bounds, consistent with strong duality.

Geometrically, the contour lines of $f(x) = x_1 + 2x_2$ are straight lines with slope $-1/2$.
As we move these lines in the direction $-a = (-1,-2)$ (decreasing $f$), they exit the feasible
box at the lower-left corner $(0.5, -5)$.  This corner is a \emph{basic feasible solution}
(vertex) of the LP, corresponding to the point where both lower-bound constraints are active.

\begin{remark}[Farkas' lemma and LP duality]
The foundation of LP strong duality is \emph{Farkas' lemma}: for a matrix $A$ and vector $b$,
exactly one of the following is true:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,label=(\roman*)]
\item $\exists\, x \geq 0$ such that $Ax = b$ (primal feasibility);
\item $\exists\, y$ such that $A^T y \geq 0$ and $b^T y < 0$ (dual certificate of infeasibility).
\end{enumerate}
This provides a geometric test: either a primal solution exists, or a separating hyperplane
(dual vector $y$) certifies primal infeasibility.  For the LP $\min\{c^Tx : Ax = b, x \geq 0\}$,
Farkas' lemma implies exactly three cases: (a) feasible and bounded (optimal solution exists;
strong duality $c^Tx^{\star} = b^Ty^{\star}$ holds by complementary slackness), (b) feasible
and unbounded (dual is infeasible), or (c) primal infeasible (dual has a certificate).  In our
box LP, case (a) applies: the LMO at each Frank--Wolfe step solves $\min_{x \in \mathcal{X}}
\langle \nabla f(x_k), x \rangle$, which is an LP of the form (a), and Farkas' lemma guarantees
that the optimal vertex $z_k$ exists and equals the LP dual solution.  This is why the
Frank--Wolfe LMO can be solved in $O(d)$ for box constraints: it reduces to $d$ independent
scalar minimisations, each trivially solved at the lower or upper bound.
\end{remark}

\textbf{LP dual problem:}  The primal LP is $\min_{x \in \mathcal{X}}\; a^Tx$.  Writing the
box as $l \leq x \leq u$ (with $l = (0.5, -5)$ and $u = (5, 10)$), the dual is:
\begin{equation}
\max_{\mu, \nu \geq 0}\; l^T\mu - u^T\nu \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mu - \nu = a,
\end{equation}
where $\mu_i$ and $\nu_i$ are dual variables for the lower and upper bounds respectively.
At the primal optimum $x^{\star} = (0.5, -5)$ (both lower bounds active), the dual solution
is $\mu^{\star} = a = (1, 2)$, $\nu^{\star} = 0$, with dual objective
$l^T\mu^{\star} = 0.5 \cdot 1 + (-5) \cdot 2 = -9.5 = f^{\star}$.  Strong duality holds
(the duality gap is zero) because the LP is bounded and feasible, satisfying Slater's condition.
The complementary slackness conditions $\mu_i^{\star}(x_i^{\star} - l_i) = 0$ and
$\nu_i^{\star}(u_i - x_i^{\star}) = 0$ confirm: lower bounds active ($x_i = l_i$, $\mu_i > 0$),
upper bounds inactive ($x_i < u_i$, $\nu_i = 0$).

\subsection{Frank--Wolfe Algorithm (Conditional Gradient)}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Frank--Wolfe (Conditional Gradient)~\cite{frankwolfe1956}}
\label{alg:fw}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0 \in \mathcal{X}$, mixing parameter $\beta \in (0,1)$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_k \gets \arg\min_{x \in \mathcal{X}}\; \nabla f(x_k)^T x$
    \Comment{Linear minimisation oracle (LMO)}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets \beta\, x_k + (1 - \beta)\, z_k$
    \Comment{Convex combination, fixed step size $(1-\beta)$}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

For box constraints, the LMO decouples coordinate-wise:
\begin{equation}
(z_k)_i = \begin{cases} l_i & \text{if } (\nabla f(x_k))_i > 0 \\ u_i & \text{if } (\nabla f(x_k))_i \leq 0 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
requiring $O(d)$ operations --- far cheaper than a general projection onto complex constraint sets.
Crucially, $x_{k+1}$ is a convex combination of $x_k \in \mathcal{X}$ and $z_k \in \mathcal{X}$,
so \textbf{feasibility is maintained by construction} without any explicit projection step.

\begin{theorem}[Frank--Wolfe convergence rate~\cite{jaggi2013}]
For a convex function $f$ with $L$-Lipschitz gradient, the Frank--Wolfe algorithm with
step size $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
f(x_k) - f^{\star} \leq \frac{2L\,\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{X})^2}{k+2} = O\!\left(\frac{1}{k}\right).
\end{equation}
The duality gap $h_k = \langle \nabla f(x_k),\, x_k - z_k \rangle \geq f(x_k) - f^{\star}$ provides
an \emph{efficiently computable} upper bound on sub-optimality and a natural stopping criterion.
\end{theorem}

Note: the experiments use a \emph{fixed} $\beta$ (i.e.\ $\gamma_k = 1 - \beta$ constant) rather
than the diminishing schedule $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$.  Fixed $\beta$ does not achieve the $O(1/k)$
guarantee but is commonly used in practice for its simplicity.

\begin{proposition}[Duality gap is a certificate of sub-optimality]
For any convex $f$ and feasible $x_k \in \mathcal{X}$, the Frank--Wolfe duality gap
$h_k = \langle \nabla f(x_k),\, x_k - z_k \rangle$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
h_k \geq f(x_k) - f^{\star}.
\label{eq:fw_gap_bound}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Since $z_k = \arg\min_{x \in \mathcal{X}} \langle \nabla f(x_k), x \rangle$ and $x^{\star} \in
\mathcal{X}$, we have $\langle \nabla f(x_k), z_k \rangle \leq \langle \nabla f(x_k), x^{\star}
\rangle$, hence
\[
h_k = \langle \nabla f(x_k),\, x_k - z_k \rangle \geq \langle \nabla f(x_k),\, x_k - x^{\star} \rangle.
\]
By convexity of $f$: $f(x^{\star}) \geq f(x_k) + \langle \nabla f(x_k),\, x^{\star} - x_k \rangle$,
which rearranges to $\langle \nabla f(x_k),\, x_k - x^{\star} \rangle \geq f(x_k) - f^{\star}$.
Combining gives~\eqref{eq:fw_gap_bound}.
\end{proof}

This makes the duality gap a \emph{computable upper bound} on $f(x_k) - f^{\star}$: it can be
evaluated at every iteration without knowing $f^{\star}$, serving as an efficient stopping criterion.
When $h_k \leq \varepsilon$, the algorithm is guaranteed to be within $\varepsilon$ of optimal.

\subsection{Interior vs Boundary Behaviour}

\textbf{Interior optimum:} Frank--Wolfe exhibits the characteristic ``zig-zag'' convergence
pattern~\cite{jaggi2013}.  The LMO vertex $z_k$ jumps between extreme points of $\mathcal{X}$ at
each iteration, and the convex combination gradually averages these jumps.  With the fixed $\beta$
schedule, the step size does not decay, leading to residual oscillation near the optimum.  Using
the standard $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$ schedule would eliminate this and recover the $O(1/k)$ bound.

\textbf{Analysis of the zig-zag phenomenon:}  Near the interior optimum $(1, 5)$, the gradient
is $\nabla f(x_k) \approx 2(x_k - (1,5))$ --- a small vector pointing from $x_k$ to the
optimum.  The LMO computes $z_k = \arg\min \langle \nabla f(x_k), z \rangle$ over $\mathcal{X}$.
Since $\nabla f(x_k)$ is nearly zero with components of alternating sign as $x_k$ oscillates
around the optimum, $z_k$ jumps between opposite corners of $\mathcal{X}$ (e.g.\ between
$(0.5, -5)$ and $(5, 10)$) at consecutive iterations.  Despite this, the convex combination
$x_{k+1} = \beta x_k + (1-\beta) z_k$ is bounded and the objective does decrease, albeit
slowly.  The zig-zag is intrinsic to Frank--Wolfe near interior optima: the method can only
move toward a \emph{vertex} of $\mathcal{X}$, never directly toward the interior optimum,
so it must average over many vertex visits to approximate the interior point.

\textbf{Away-step Frank--Wolfe:} A key variant that achieves \emph{linear} convergence ($O((1-\mu/L)^k)$)
for strongly convex objectives is the \emph{away-step} method, which alternates between the
standard FW step (towards the LMO vertex $z_k$) and an \emph{away step} (moving away from the
vertex with the largest weight in the current convex decomposition of $x_k$).  The away step
explicitly reduces the contribution of poorly-positioned vertices, enabling geometric progress.
For the interior objective $(x_1-1)^2 + (x_2-5)^2$ with strong convexity $\mu = 2$ and
$L = 2$, the away-step rate would be $(1 - \mu/L)^k = 0^k$ --- convergence in one step, since
the unconstrained minimum lies in the interior of $\mathcal{X}$.

\textbf{Boundary optimum:} Frank--Wolfe is well suited to boundary optima because $z_k$ frequently
selects the vertex closest to the constrained optimum ($x_1 = 0.5$), naturally driving the iterate
to the boundary.  After 140 iterations, the method reaches $f = 0.364$ vs.\ $f^{\star} = 0.25$,
a gap of $0.114$: convergence is slower because the $O(1/k)$ rate is slow at the boundary when
the problem is not strongly convex over $\mathcal{X}$.

\textbf{Quantitative FW convergence prediction:}  The $O(1/k)$ bound states
$f(x_k) - f^{\star} \leq 2LC^2/(k+2)$ where $C = \mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{X})$ and $L$ is the
Lipschitz constant of $\nabla f$.  For the boundary objective $f = x_1^2 + x_2^2$ with $L = 2$
and $\mathcal{X} = [0.5,5] \times [-5,10]$: $C = \|(5-0.5, 10-(-5))\| = \|(4.5, 15)\| \approx 15.7$.
The predicted gap at $k = 140$: $2 \cdot 2 \cdot 15.7^2 / 142 \approx 6.9$.  The empirical gap
of $0.114$ is far smaller, confirming that the $O(1/k)$ bound is loose (it uses the diameter
as a worst-case, while the actual trajectory stays in a much smaller region of $\mathcal{X}$).
This gap between the theoretical bound and empirical performance is typical: the bound holds
universally but is not tight for structured problems.

\textbf{Effect of step-size schedule on convergence:} The fixed mixing parameter $\beta$ (step size
$1-\beta$) does not decay, causing persistent oscillation near the optimum.  Replacing this with
the diminishing schedule $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$ (i.e.\ $\beta_k = k/(k+2)$) gives the standard
Frank--Wolfe guarantee.  Alternatively, a line search $\gamma_k^{\star} = \arg\min_{\gamma \in
[0,1]} f(\gamma x_k + (1-\gamma)z_k)$ accelerates convergence.  For an $L$-smooth function,
the closed-form solution is
\begin{equation}
\gamma_k^{\star} = \min\!\left(1,\; \frac{\langle \nabla f(x_k),\, x_k - z_k \rangle}{L\,\|x_k - z_k\|^2}\right)
= \min\!\left(1,\; \frac{h_k}{L\,\|x_k - z_k\|^2}\right),
\label{eq:fw_linesearch}
\end{equation}
where $L$ is the Lipschitz constant of $\nabla f$.  For the interior quadratic
$(x_1-1)^2 + (x_2-5)^2$ with $L = 2$, this gives the exact minimiser along the segment
$[x_k, z_k]$ with only one gradient evaluation per iteration (the duality gap $h_k$ is
already computed).

\begin{remark}[Stochastic Frank--Wolfe and variance reduction]
When the objective is a finite sum $f(x) = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m f_i(x)$ (as in Benchmark A
with data-dependent loss), the full gradient $\nabla f(x_k)$ is expensive to compute and a
stochastic estimate $\nabla f_{i_k}(x_k)$ is used instead.  The standard stochastic Frank--Wolfe
algorithm (Hazan \& Luo, 2016; Reddi et al.\ 2016) runs:
\begin{equation}
z_k = \arg\min_{z \in \mathcal{X}} \langle \hat{g}_k, z \rangle, \qquad
x_{k+1} = (1-\gamma_k) x_k + \gamma_k z_k,
\end{equation}
where $\hat{g}_k = \nabla f_{i_k}(x_k)$ is a mini-batch gradient.  For non-convex objectives
with $L$-smooth $f_i$, stochastic FW guarantees finding a point with duality gap
$\mathbb{E}[h_k] \leq \varepsilon$ in $O(1/\varepsilon^2)$ iterations, matching the stochastic
first-order lower bound.  For convex objectives, the convergence deteriorates to $O(1/\sqrt{k})$
from the deterministic $O(1/k)$ due to gradient noise.  \emph{SPIDER-Frank-Wolfe} (Yao et al.\
2020) applies variance reduction (similar to SVRG) to recover the deterministic $O(1/k)$ rate
in the stochastic setting by periodically re-anchoring the gradient estimate to the full gradient
at a snapshot point.  This is analogous to SVRG's correction in mini-batch SGD, but the
projection-free property of FW is maintained --- each iteration still requires only an LMO call,
not a projection.  The duality gap upper-bound property, $h_k \geq f(x_k) - f^{\star}$, is
preserved with the stochastic gradient as long as the LMO uses the same gradient estimate $\hat{g}_k$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Frank--Wolfe for matrix completion and the nuclear norm ball]
A canonical application where FW dominates PGD is \emph{matrix completion}: given a low-rank
matrix $M \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ observed at entries $\Omega$, minimise the squared
reconstruction error $\min_{X:\sigma_1(X) \leq \tau} \|P_\Omega(X - M)\|_F^2$ over the
nuclear-norm ball $\mathcal{X} = \{X : \|X\|_* \leq \tau\}$ (sum of singular values $\leq \tau$).
The \textbf{LMO} for the nuclear norm ball is:
$Z = \arg\min_{Z : \|Z\|_* \leq \tau} \langle G, Z \rangle = -\tau u v^T$,
where $(u, v)$ are the top left/right singular vectors of the negative gradient $G$.
This requires only a \emph{top-$1$ SVD} (or randomised SVD), costing $O(m n)$ --- in contrast,
\textbf{projection} onto the nuclear norm ball requires a full SVD ($O(\min(m,n)^2 \max(m,n))$)
plus singular value soft-thresholding.  For large matrices ($m, n \sim 10^4$), the full SVD is
infeasible while the rank-1 SVD is tractable.  The FW iterates $X_{k+1} = (1-\gamma_k)X_k
+ \gamma_k(-\tau u_k v_k^T)$ maintain low effective rank (at most $k$ terms) and converge
at the standard $O(1/k)$ rate, achieving the $O(\tau^2 L / k)$ bound where $L$ is the Lipschitz
constant of the gradient of the reconstruction loss.
\end{remark}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

This report has systematically evaluated six families of optimisation algorithms across benchmarks
of increasing difficulty.  The principal conclusions are as follows.

\textbf{A note on convergence for non-convex problems:}  Several benchmarks (Rosenbrock,
the toy NN loss with $\sin$) are non-convex in some regions.  For non-convex $f$ with
$L$-Lipschitz gradient, GD with step $\alpha = 1/L$ guarantees that the gradient norm satisfies
$\min_{k \leq K} \|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2 \leq 2L(f(x_0) - f^{\star})/K = O(1/K)$, finding a
\emph{first-order stationary point} (not necessarily a global minimum) in $O(1/\varepsilon^2)$
steps.  This explains why all methods make consistent progress in the experiments even on the
non-convex Rosenbrock: the iterates happen to stay in the locally convex parabolic valley
(since the gradient always points toward the valley floor), so the non-convexity does not
trap the methods at saddle points.

\subsection*{Key Findings}

\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=1.5em]
\item \textbf{Adaptive step-size methods (Q1):} (See Table~\ref{tab:q1_final}.)  The Polyak step
size provides a convergence rate guarantee that depends entirely on the accuracy of $f^{\star}$.
When this is known (Rosenbrock, $f^{\star} = 0$), it outperforms all first-order competitors
($f = 0.009$).  Adagrad and Heavy Ball are more robust for practical use.  RMSprop's exponential
forgetting makes it suitable for non-stationary problems but offers no theoretical convergence
guarantee.

\item \textbf{Momentum and acceleration (Q2):} (See Table~\ref{tab:q2_final}.)  Nesterov
momentum's $O(1/k^2)$ rate provides a measurable advantage on difficult problems: on Rosenbrock
it achieves a $7\times$ improvement over GD ($f = 0.479$ vs.\ $3.39$).  Adam is widely used in
practice due to its per-coordinate scaling, but lacks formal convergence guarantees and
underperforms Nesterov on problems with strongly coupled curvature (off-diagonal Hessian entries),
such as Rosenbrock where the cross-term $-400x_1$ dominates.  Mini-batch SGD's
optimal batch size involves a noise-versus-compute trade-off: small batches converge faster per epoch
but oscillate more near the minimum.

\item \textbf{Newton's method (Q3):} (See Table~\ref{tab:q3_final}.)  Newton's method achieves
quadratic local convergence and converges in a single iteration for quadratic objectives (Benchmark
A, $f = 0.4826$ after 1 step vs.\ GD's 80 steps), demonstrating a fundamental advantage over all
first-order methods.  The cost is computing and inverting the Hessian ($O(d^3)$ per iteration),
making it impractical for large-scale problems.  Hessian regularisation ($\lambda I$ added to
$\nabla^2 f$) is necessary for numerical stability and global convergence, but does not materially
affect the asymptotic rate near the optimum.

\item \textbf{Derivative-free methods (Q4):} Finite differences with well-chosen $\delta$ closely
mimic exact gradient descent at the cost of $d$ additional function evaluations per step.  The
theoretically optimal $\delta \approx \sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}}$ for forward differences
and $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}^{1/3}$ for central differences can serve as practical guidelines.
Nesterov random search has dimension-independent cost per iteration but an $O(d/k)$ convergence
rate, making it most appropriate when gradients are unavailable and dimension is very large.

\item \textbf{Constrained optimisation (Q5):} Projected gradient descent guarantees feasibility at
every iterate --- a property that penalty methods only satisfy asymptotically.  When the constraint
is simple (as here: a halfspace), projection is $O(1)$ and PGD is the clear choice.  The penalty
approach becomes competitive when the constraint set is complex and projection is expensive.
Augmented Lagrangian methods offer a middle ground, achieving exact feasibility without the
ill-conditioning of large-$\lambda$ penalties.

\item \textbf{Frank--Wolfe and linear programming (Q6):} The Frank--Wolfe algorithm is
projection-free by design, replacing the projection oracle with an inexpensive linear minimisation
oracle.  This makes it especially attractive for structured constraint sets (e.g.\ spectahedra,
flow polytopes) where LP subproblems are tractable but projections are not.  The duality gap
$\langle \nabla f(x_k), x_k - z_k \rangle$ provides an efficiently computable certificate of
near-optimality, and the $O(1/k)$ convergence rate is confirmed empirically.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Unifying Framework: Mirror Descent and Bregman Divergences}

Many of the methods studied in this report are special cases of a single framework:
\emph{mirror descent}.  Given a strictly convex \emph{potential} $\phi : \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}$,
the Bregman divergence is:
\begin{equation}
D_\phi(x,\, y)
  = \phi(x) - \phi(y) - \langle \nabla\phi(y),\, x - y \rangle \geq 0.
\label{eq:bregman}
\end{equation}
The mirror descent update at step $k$ is:
\begin{equation}
x_{k+1}
  = \arg\min_{x \in \mathcal{X}}\bigl\{\langle g_k,\, x \rangle
    + \tfrac{1}{\alpha}D_\phi(x,\, x_k)\bigr\},
\label{eq:mirror_descent}
\end{equation}
which minimises a \emph{linear} model of $f$ (using subgradient $g_k$) while staying close to
$x_k$ in the Bregman sense.  The choice of $\phi$ tailors the geometry to the constraint set:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Euclidean geometry} ($\phi(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}\|x\|^2$, $D_\phi(x,y) = \tfrac{1}{2}\|x-y\|^2$):
  \eqref{eq:mirror_descent} solves $\min_x\{\langle g_k, x\rangle + \frac{1}{2\alpha}\|x-x_k\|^2\}$
  over $\mathcal{X}$, yielding exactly \textbf{projected gradient descent}:
  $x_{k+1} = \Pi_\mathcal{X}(x_k - \alpha g_k)$.  Used throughout Q5 (PGD) and all unconstrained
  methods (where $\mathcal{X} = \mathbb{R}^d$, projection is trivial).

\item \textbf{Negative entropy} ($\phi(x) = \sum_i x_i\log x_i$ on the simplex
  $\mathcal{X} = \Delta_d$): $D_\phi(x,y) = \sum_i x_i\log(x_i/y_i)$ is the KL divergence.
  Mirror descent recovers the \emph{exponentiated gradient} update
  $x_{k+1}^{(i)} \propto x_k^{(i)}e^{-\alpha g_k^{(i)}}$, which is natural for probability
  simplex constraints (e.g.\ online learning over strategies in a game).

\item \textbf{Log-barrier geometry} on a polytope: replacing the Bregman proximity with a
  linearisation at a vertex of $\mathcal{X}$ recovers \textbf{Frank--Wolfe}.  The LMO step
  $z_k = \arg\min_{z \in \mathcal{X}}\langle g_k, z\rangle$ corresponds to a \emph{gradient
  step in the dual space} induced by the log-barrier of the polytope, and the convex
  combination $x_{k+1} = (1-\gamma_k)x_k + \gamma_k z_k$ mirrors a prox step in that geometry.
\end{itemize}

This unification clarifies the design principle: \emph{match the geometry of $\phi$ to the
geometry of the constraint set}.  Euclidean geometry ($\phi = \tfrac{1}{2}\|x\|^2$) is optimal
for $\ell_2$-ball and halfspace constraints; entropic geometry ($\phi = \sum x_i\log x_i$) for
the probability simplex; log-barrier geometry for general polytopes.  The $O(1/k)$ convergence
rate holds for all instances of mirror descent on $L$-smooth objectives~\cite{nocedal2006},
unifying the convergence analyses of PGD, FW, and exponentiated gradient.

\begin{remark}[Coordinate descent: exploiting separability]
\emph{Coordinate descent} (CD) is a complementary family to gradient-based methods that updates
one coordinate at a time: $x_{k+1}^{(j)} = x_k^{(j)} - \alpha_j \partial f / \partial x_j$,
cycling through $j = 1,\ldots,d$.  For $\mu$-strongly convex, $L$-smooth objectives, CD
converges at a linear rate $O((1-\mu/(Ld))^k)$ per coordinate update.  This is $d$ times
slower than GD's $O((1-\mu/L)^k)$ in iterations, but each coordinate update costs $O(1)$ vs.
$O(d)$ for GD, giving the same total cost per epoch.  CD advantages:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item When per-coordinate Lipschitz constants $L_j \ll L$ allow larger steps $\alpha_j = 1/L_j$
  than $\alpha = 1/L$ for GD (e.g.\ LASSO proximal coordinate descent).
\item When $d$ is very large and the full gradient computation dominates the per-iteration cost.
\item When the Hessian is sparse (block-diagonal), allowing cheap independent block updates.
\end{itemize}
For Benchmark B with Hessian $\mathrm{diag}(2.4, 10)$ and $L = 10$: GD with $\alpha = 2/(L+\mu)
= 2/12.4 \approx 0.16$ achieves rate $(1-\mu/L) = 0.76$; optimal CD with $\alpha_1 = 1/2.4$,
$\alpha_2 = 1/10$ achieves rate $\max(1-1, 1-2.4/10) = 0.76$ per full cycle --- identical here
because $\kappa$ is small.  For ill-conditioned problems, randomised CD (Shalev-Shwartz \&
Zhang, 2013) can be $\kappa$-times faster than GD if coordinate-wise conditioning is better.
\end{remark}

\subsection*{Practical Recommendations}

Based on the experimental results and theoretical analysis, the following decision framework
emerges for selecting an optimisation algorithm:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=1.5em]
\item \textbf{Well-conditioned, unconstrained problems} ($\kappa \lesssim 10$): Basic GD or
  Adagrad are sufficient; the condition number is too small to reveal significant differences.
  Use Adagrad if gradient magnitudes vary significantly across coordinates.

\item \textbf{Ill-conditioned unconstrained problems} ($\kappa \gg 1$, no constraints):
  \emph{Nesterov momentum} is the first-order method of choice, achieving $O(\sqrt{\kappa})$
  iterations vs.\ $O(\kappa)$ for GD.  When Hessian evaluations are feasible ($d \leq 10^3$),
  \emph{Newton's method} (or L-BFGS) dominates dramatically.

\item \textbf{Stochastic / large-scale settings} ($m \gg 1$, mini-batch required):
  \emph{Adam} is the practical default for deep learning (non-stationary gradients, many
  parameters, sparse updates).  Use SVRG or SAG when variance reduction is critical and the
  dataset fits in memory.

\item \textbf{Constrained problems, simple sets}: \emph{Projected GD} when projection is
  cheap (halfspaces, $\ell_2$ balls); \emph{Frank--Wolfe} when LP subproblems are cheap but
  projection is expensive (spectahedron, flow polytope, nuclear-norm ball).

\item \textbf{Constrained problems, complex sets}: \emph{Augmented Lagrangian} methods
  combine dual variable updates with moderate penalty weights, avoiding the ill-conditioning
  of large-$\lambda$ pure penalty methods.

\item \textbf{Derivative-unavailable settings}: Nesterov random search for high-dimensional
  smooth objectives; Nelder--Mead for low-dimensional ($d \leq 10$) black-box functions.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Gradient Descent}]
def gradient_descent(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        x = x - alpha * grad_fn(x)
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Polyak Step Size}]
def polyak_step(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, f_star, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        alpha_k = (loss_fn(x) - f_star) / (np.dot(g, g) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(alpha_k)
        x = x - alpha_k * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Adagrad}]
def adagrad(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha0, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    G = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        G += g**2
        eff_alpha = alpha0 / (np.sqrt(G) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(np.mean(eff_alpha))
        x = x - eff_alpha * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={RMSprop}]
def rmsprop(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha0, beta, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    v = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        v = beta * v + (1 - beta) * g**2
        eff_alpha = alpha0 / (np.sqrt(v) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(np.mean(eff_alpha))
        x = x - eff_alpha * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Heavy Ball / Polyak Momentum}]
def heavy_ball(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    z = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        z = beta * z + alpha * g
        x = x - z
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Nesterov Momentum}]
def nesterov_momentum(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta_max, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    z = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for k in range(1, n_iters + 1):
        beta_k = min((k - 1) / (k + 2), beta_max)
        lookahead = x + beta_k * z
        g = grad_fn(lookahead)
        z = beta_k * z - alpha * g
        x = x + z
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Adam Optimiser}]
def adam_optimiser(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta1, beta2, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    m_vec, v_vec = np.zeros_like(x), np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for t in range(1, n_iters + 1):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        m_vec = beta1 * m_vec + (1 - beta1) * g
        v_vec = beta2 * v_vec + (1 - beta2) * g**2
        m_hat = m_vec / (1 - beta1**t)
        v_hat = v_vec / (1 - beta2**t)
        x = x - alpha * m_hat / (np.sqrt(v_hat) + eps)
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Newton's Method with Regularisation}]
def newtons_method(grad_fn, hess_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, n_iters, damping=1e-8):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, update_norms = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        H = hess_fn(x) if callable(hess_fn) else hess_fn
        H_reg = H + damping * np.eye(len(x))
        try:
            p = np.linalg.solve(H_reg, g)
        except np.linalg.LinAlgError:
            p = g
        update_norms.append(np.linalg.norm(alpha * p))
        x = x - alpha * p
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(update_norms)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Frank--Wolfe Algorithm}]
def frank_wolfe(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, bounds, beta, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, z_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        z = np.array([bounds[i][0] if g[i] > 0 else bounds[i][1]
                       for i in range(len(g))])
        z_hist.append(z.copy())
        x = beta * x + (1 - beta) * z
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(z_hist)
\end{lstlisting}
