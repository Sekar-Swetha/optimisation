\begin{abstract}
This report presents a systematic comparative study of twelve optimisation algorithms across six categories, evaluated on three benchmark problems: a linear regression quadratic loss (Benchmark~A), a toy neural-network loss with sinusoidal perturbation (Benchmark~B), and the highly ill-conditioned Rosenbrock function (Benchmark~C).

The key findings are as follows. \textbf{Adaptive step-size methods} (Question~1): The Polyak step size achieves the best convergence when $f^\star$ is known exactly (Benchmark~C: $f=0.009$) but diverges catastrophically when $f^\star$ is misspecified; Adagrad and Heavy Ball match the gradient descent baseline with added stability. \textbf{Momentum methods} (Question~2): Nesterov acceleration achieves $f=0.479$ on Rosenbrock vs.\ GD's $f=3.388$, demonstrating the practical benefit of its $O(1/k^2)$ convergence rate; Adam is robust across benchmarks but does not outperform Nesterov on highly non-convex problems. \textbf{Newton's method} (Question~3): Converges to the quadratic minimum in a single step and reaches $f=0.686$ on Rosenbrock in only 20 iterations, versus $f=3.732$ for GD in 80 iterations, illustrating the power of second-order information. \textbf{Derivative-free methods} (Question~4): Forward finite differences with small $\delta=0.05$ closely approximate exact gradients; Nelder--Mead navigates the Rosenbrock valley without gradients; grid search provides global coverage but scales as $O(N^d)$. \textbf{Constrained optimisation} (Question~5): Projected gradient descent enforces the constraint $x_1\geq0.5$ exactly from the first iteration; penalty methods require large $\lambda$ to achieve feasibility but introduce step-size constraints. \textbf{Frank--Wolfe} (Question~6): Naturally handles box constraints through linear subproblems with $O(1/k)$ convergence, and exhibits different behaviours for interior versus boundary optima.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================

Optimisation lies at the heart of machine learning: training a model reduces to minimising a loss function, and the choice of optimisation algorithm profoundly affects convergence speed, solution quality, and computational cost. This report investigates a broad spectrum of first-order, second-order, derivative-free, and constrained optimisation algorithms, providing a principled comparison across benchmark functions of varying difficulty.

The benchmarks are chosen to stress-test complementary aspects of each algorithm. Benchmark~A (quadratic linear regression) has constant, well-conditioned curvature and admits a closed-form solution, making it ideal for verifying correctness. Benchmark~B (toy neural network with sinusoidal perturbation) introduces moderate anisotropy and non-constant curvature, representative of shallow neural networks. Benchmark~C (Rosenbrock) has condition number $\kappa\approx2000$ near its minimum and a narrow curved valley, representing the worst-case difficulty of gradient-based methods. All experiments use \texttt{np.random.seed(42)} for reproducibility.

\section{Benchmark Problems and Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:benchmarks}

\subsection{Benchmark A: Linear Regression Quadratic Loss}
A synthetic dataset with $m = 1000$ samples and $d=2$ parameters is generated:
\begin{equation}
\theta^{\star} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad x^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I_2), \quad y^{(i)} = \theta^{\star T} x^{(i)} + \varepsilon^{(i)}, \quad \varepsilon^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1).
\end{equation}
The mean squared error loss is:
\begin{equation}
J(\theta) = \frac{1}{2m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \left(\theta^T x^{(i)} - y^{(i)}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{2m}\|X\theta - y\|^2.
\end{equation}
The Hessian $H = \frac{1}{m}X^T X$ is constant, positive definite, with eigenvalues $\lambda_{\min}(H)\approx0.98$ and $\lambda_{\max}(H)\approx1.02$ (nearly isotropic). The gradient is $\nabla J(\theta) = \frac{1}{m}X^T(X\theta - y)$. Due to observation noise, the minimum loss is $J^{\star} = \frac{1}{2m}\|y - X\theta_{\text{OLS}}\|^2 \approx 0.4826$, not zero. Starting point: $\theta_0 = [0, 0]^T$.

\subsection{Benchmark B: Toy Neural Network Loss}
\begin{equation}
f(x_1, x_2) = (x_1 - 1)^2 + 5(x_2 - 2)^2 + \sin(x_1).
\end{equation}
The gradient is $\nabla f = [2(x_1 - 1) + \cos(x_1),\ 10(x_2 - 2)]^T$ and the Hessian is $H = \text{diag}(2 - \sin(x_1),\ 10)$. The Hessian eigenvalue ratio $\lambda_2/\lambda_1 = 10/(2-\sin(x_1))$ varies between approximately $3.3:1$ and $\infty$ (when $\sin(x_1)=2$, impossible) and in practice gives a condition number of roughly $5$--$7$ near the minimum, creating moderate anisotropy. The minimum lies at approximately $(0.582, 2.0)$ where $2(x_1-1)+\cos(x_1)=0$ and $x_2=2$, giving $f^{\star} \approx 0.7244$. Starting point: $x_0 = [-1, 4]^T$.

\subsection{Benchmark C: Rosenbrock Function}
\begin{equation}
f(x_1, x_2) = (1 - x_1)^2 + 100(x_2 - x_1^2)^2.
\end{equation}
The global minimum is at $(1, 1)$ with $f^{\star} = 0$. The Hessian at the optimum is:
\begin{equation}
H(1,1) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 + 400\cdot100\cdot1^2 - 400\cdot1 & -400\cdot1 \\ -400\cdot1 & 200 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 802 & -400 \\ -400 & 200 \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
with eigenvalues approximately $1002$ and $0.5$, giving condition number $\kappa \approx 2004$. This extreme ill-conditioning, combined with a narrow curved valley along $x_2 = x_1^2$, makes Rosenbrock one of the most challenging standard test functions in optimisation. A gradient step of size $\alpha > 2/\lambda_{\max} \approx 0.002$ will overshoot in the steep curvature direction. Starting point: $x_0 = [-1, 1]^T$.

\subsection{Benchmark D: Constrained Feasible Set}
For constrained problems (Questions 5--6):
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{X} = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^2 : 0.5 \leq x_1 \leq 5,\ -5 \leq x_2 \leq 10\}.
\end{equation}
This is a compact convex polytope (box constraint). The diameter $\text{diam}(\mathcal{X}) = \sqrt{4.5^2 + 15^2} \approx 15.66$.

\section{Question 1: Adaptive Step-Size Methods \hfill [30 marks]}
\label{sec:q1}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Theoretical Background}
\label{sec:q1_theory}

For a differentiable function that is $L$-smooth (i.e.\ $\|\nabla f(x) - \nabla f(y)\| \leq L\|x-y\|$ for all $x,y$) and $\mu$-strongly convex, constant-step gradient descent with $\alpha = 1/L$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}
f(x_k) - f^\star \leq \left(1 - \frac{\mu}{L}\right)^k (f(x_0) - f^\star).
\end{equation}
The ratio $\kappa = L/\mu$ is the condition number. For Benchmark~A, $\kappa\approx1.04$, explaining rapid convergence. For Benchmark~C, $\kappa\approx2004$, forcing extremely small steps.

The \textbf{Polyak step size} $\alpha_k = (f(x_k)-f^\star)/(\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2 + \varepsilon)$ achieves convergence without knowing $L$: when $f^\star$ is exact, $\alpha_k \to 0$ as $x_k \to x^\star$ since both numerator and denominator vanish at comparable rates. When $f^\star$ is misspecified (set to $0$ when true minimum is $f^\star_{\rm true}>0$), the numerator $f(x_k)-0 \to f^\star_{\rm true}$ while the denominator $\|\nabla f\|^2 \to 0$, so $\alpha_k \to \infty$ --- producing divergence.

\textbf{Adagrad} accumulates squared gradients $G_k = G_{k-1} + g_k \odot g_k$ (monotonically non-decreasing), giving effective step $\tilde{\alpha}_k = \alpha_0/(\sqrt{G_k}+\varepsilon)$ that decays as $O(1/\sqrt{k})$. This guarantees stability but may cause premature stagnation. \textbf{RMSprop} replaces the accumulation with an exponential moving average $v_k = \beta v_{k-1} + (1-\beta)g_k^2$, allowing the effective step to recover after gradients diminish --- suitable for non-stationary objectives.

\subsection{Algorithm Formulations}

Four adaptive methods are compared against a constant step-size gradient descent baseline across all three benchmarks for 120 iterations.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Polyak Step Size}
\label{alg:polyak}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, optimal value $f^{\star}$, tolerance $\varepsilon$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $\alpha_k \gets \dfrac{f(x_k) - f^{\star}}{\|g_k\|^2 + \varepsilon}$
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha_k\, g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Adagrad}
\label{alg:adagrad}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, base learning rate $\alpha_0$, tolerance $\varepsilon$, iterations $N$
\State $G_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $G_{k+1} \gets G_k + g_k \odot g_k$ \Comment{Per-coordinate cumulative sum of squared gradients}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \dfrac{\alpha_0}{\sqrt{G_{k+1}} + \varepsilon} \odot g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{RMSprop}
\label{alg:rmsprop}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, learning rate $\alpha_0$, decay $\beta$, tolerance $\varepsilon$, iterations $N$
\State $v_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $v_{k+1} \gets \beta\, v_k + (1 - \beta)\, g_k \odot g_k$ \Comment{Exponential moving average of squared gradients}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \dfrac{\alpha_0}{\sqrt{v_{k+1}} + \varepsilon} \odot g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Polyak Momentum / Heavy Ball}
\label{alg:heavyball}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, momentum $\beta$, iterations $N$
\State $z_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets \beta\, z_k + \alpha\, \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - z_{k+1}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The Heavy Ball method can be interpreted as a damped harmonic oscillator: $z_k$ accumulates gradient momentum with exponential decay factor $\beta$. For a quadratic with Hessian eigenvalue $\lambda$, optimal parameters $\alpha^\star = 4/(\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}}+\sqrt{\lambda_{\min}})^2$ and $\beta^\star = (\sqrt{\kappa}-1)^2/(\sqrt{\kappa}+1)^2$ achieve convergence rate $(1-2/(\sqrt{\kappa}+1))^k$, superior to GD's $(1-2/(\kappa+1))^k$ for large $\kappa$.

\subsection{Comparative Analysis}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Convergence speed:} When $f^{\star}$ is known exactly (Benchmark~C), Polyak converges fastest ($f=0.009$ in 120 iterations). For misspecified $f^\star$, Adagrad and Heavy Ball match GD on well-conditioned problems (Benchmarks~A, B) while outperforming it on Rosenbrock.
\item \textbf{Stability and monotonicity:} Adagrad is unconditionally stable due to the monotonically non-decreasing $G_k$ accumulator, which prevents the step from growing. GD with a fixed step is stable when $\alpha < 2/L$. RMSprop may occasionally exhibit mild oscillations near the minimum due to non-monotonic effective steps.
\item \textbf{Adaptivity to anisotropy:} On Benchmark~B (condition number $\approx$5--7), per-coordinate adaptive methods (Adagrad, RMSprop) can independently scale the $x_1$ and $x_2$ updates, effectively precondition the gradient. This benefit increases with condition number.
\item \textbf{Practical recommendation:} In practice, $f^\star$ is unknown, so Polyak is rarely used as-is. Adagrad suits sparse gradient problems (monotonic decay helps infrequently-updated parameters); RMSprop is preferred for dense, non-stationary problems; Heavy Ball (with tuned $\beta$) accelerates convergence on moderately ill-conditioned problems.
\end{itemize}

\section{Question 2: Momentum and Stochastic Methods \hfill [30 marks]}
\label{sec:q2}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Theoretical Background}
\label{sec:q2_theory}

\textbf{Nesterov accelerated gradient} achieves the optimal $O(1/k^2)$ convergence rate for smooth convex functions, compared to $O(1/k)$ for gradient descent. Specifically, for an $L$-smooth convex $f$:
\begin{equation}
f(x_k) - f^\star \leq \frac{2L\|x_0 - x^\star\|^2}{(k+1)^2}.
\end{equation}
The key insight is the \emph{lookahead}: by evaluating the gradient at the extrapolated point $\tilde{x}_k = x_k + \beta_k z_k$ rather than $x_k$ itself, Nesterov's method anticipates where momentum will carry the iterate and corrects the trajectory before overshooting. The adaptive schedule $\beta_k = (k-1)/(k+2)$ is derived from an analysis of the convergence proof and saturates at $\beta_{\max}$ for practical stability.

\textbf{Adam} combines the first-moment estimate (like momentum) and the second-moment estimate (like RMSprop) with bias correction:
\begin{align}
\hat{m}_t &= \frac{m_t}{1-\beta_1^t}, \quad \hat{v}_t = \frac{v_t}{1-\beta_2^t}.
\end{align}
Without bias correction, $m_1 = (1-\beta_1)g_1$ would severely underestimate the true gradient at $t=1$; correction multiplies by $1/(1-\beta_1)$ to restore the true scale. Adam has no general worst-case convergence guarantee for non-convex functions, but empirically excels on neural network training.

\textbf{Mini-batch SGD} introduces gradient variance: for batch size $b$, the gradient estimator $\hat{g} = \frac{1}{b}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\nabla f_i(\theta)$ has variance $\sigma^2/b$, where $\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m\|\nabla f_i(\theta^\star)\|^2$ is the per-sample gradient noise at the optimum. The expected loss satisfies $\mathbb{E}[J(\theta_k)] - J^\star \leq O(\sigma^2 \alpha/(1-\beta\alpha\mu))$ asymptotically, showing that larger batch sizes ($b\uparrow$) reduce variance $\sigma^2/b$ and thus the noise floor.

\subsection{Algorithm Formulations}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Nesterov Momentum / Acceleration}
\label{alg:nesterov}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, max momentum $\beta_{\max}$, iterations $N$
\State $z_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 1, 2, \ldots, N$}
    \State $\beta_k \gets \min\!\left(\frac{k-1}{k+2},\ \beta_{\max}\right)$ \Comment{Adaptive momentum schedule}
    \State $\tilde{x}_k \gets x_k + \beta_k\, z_k$ \Comment{Lookahead (extrapolated) point}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets \beta_k\, z_k - \alpha\, \nabla f(\tilde{x}_k)$ \Comment{Momentum update at lookahead}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k + z_{k+1}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The key distinction from Heavy Ball is the \emph{lookahead}: the gradient is evaluated at $\tilde{x}_k = x_k + \beta_k z_k$ rather than $x_k$, providing a corrective term that enables the $O(1/k^2)$ optimal rate.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Adam Optimiser}
\label{alg:adam}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, learning rate $\alpha$, moments $\beta_1, \beta_2$, tolerance $\varepsilon$, iterations $N$
\State $m_0 \gets \mathbf{0},\ v_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$t = 1, 2, \ldots, N$}
    \State $g_t \gets \nabla f(x_t)$
    \State $m_t \gets \beta_1\, m_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_1)\, g_t$ \Comment{First moment estimate (momentum)}
    \State $v_t \gets \beta_2\, v_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_2)\, g_t \odot g_t$ \Comment{Second moment estimate (adaptive scaling)}
    \State $\hat{m}_t \gets m_t\, /\, (1 - \beta_1^t)$ \Comment{Bias correction for first moment}
    \State $\hat{v}_t \gets v_t\, /\, (1 - \beta_2^t)$ \Comment{Bias correction for second moment}
    \State $x_{t+1} \gets x_t - \alpha\, \hat{m}_t\, /\, (\sqrt{\hat{v}_t} + \varepsilon)$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Adam combines the momentum of Heavy Ball (via $m_t$) with per-coordinate adaptive scaling (via $v_t$), inheriting advantages of both RMSprop and momentum methods. The bias correction terms $1/(1-\beta^t)$ account for the fact that the EMA estimates are biased towards zero during the first iterations (since $m_0=v_0=0$).

\subsection{Local Approximations of $g(x) = x^4$}

\textbf{First-order (Taylor) approximation} around $x_0 = 0.25$:
\begin{equation}
g(x) \approx g(x_0) + g'(x_0)(x - x_0) = 0.25^4 + 4 \cdot 0.25^3\,(x - 0.25) = 0.00391 + 0.0625(x - 0.25).
\end{equation}
This is the tangent line at $x_0$. Setting it to zero gives the stationary point of the linear model at $x = 0.25 - g(0.25)/g'(0.25) = 0.25 - 0.0625/0.25 = -0.0$, which is the gradient descent step with step size $1/g'(x_0)$.

\textbf{Second-order approximation:}
\begin{equation}
g(x) \approx g(x_0) + g'(x_0)(x - x_0) + \tfrac{1}{2}g''(x_0)(x - x_0)^2 = 0.00391 + 0.0625(x - 0.25) + \tfrac{1}{2}(0.75)(x - 0.25)^2.
\end{equation}
where $g''(x_0) = 12x_0^2 = 12\cdot0.0625 = 0.75$. Setting the derivative of this quadratic model to zero:
\begin{equation}
g'(x_0) + g''(x_0)(x - x_0) = 0 \implies x_{k+1} = x_k - \frac{g'(x_k)}{g''(x_k)},
\end{equation}
which is the \textbf{Newton update} for the one-dimensional case. In multiple dimensions this generalises to $x_{k+1} = x_k - [\nabla^2 f(x_k)]^{-1}\nabla f(x_k)$. The Newton step finds the \emph{exact} minimiser of the local quadratic model, providing a much better descent direction than the gradient alone.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/q3_approximation.pdf}

The second-order approximation (parabola, red dash-dot) captures the curvature and fits well within a neighbourhood of radius $\approx0.5$ around $x_0$. \subsection{Newton's Method}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Newton's Method with Damping}
\label{alg:newton}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, damping $\lambda$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $H_k \gets \nabla^2 f(x_k) + \lambda I$ \Comment{Tikhonov-regularised Hessian for positive definiteness}
    \State $p_k \gets H_k^{-1}\, g_k$ \Comment{Newton direction (solve linear system)}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, p_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The damping term $\lambda I$ with $\lambda = 10^{-8}$ ensures the regularised Hessian is always positive definite, preventing descent in uphill directions when the true Hessian has small or negative eigenvalues. The step size $\alpha < 1$ (``damped Newton step'') is necessary when far from the optimum, where the quadratic model may not be globally accurate.

\begin{theorem}[Quadratic Convergence of Newton's Method]
Let $f$ be twice continuously differentiable and let $x^\star$ be a local minimum with $\nabla^2 f(x^\star)\succ 0$. Then there exist $r,C>0$ such that for $\|x_0-x^\star\|<r$:
\begin{equation}
\|x_{k+1} - x^\star\| \leq C\|x_k - x^\star\|^2.
\end{equation}
This \emph{quadratic convergence} means the number of correct significant figures doubles at each iteration near $x^\star$.
\end{theorem}

Settings: GD uses 80 iterations; Newton uses 20 iterations with Hessian damping $\lambda = 10^{-8}$.

\subsection{Part A: Finite Difference and Nesterov Random Search (Benchmark B)}

\subsubsection{Finite Difference Gradient Approximation}

The forward finite-difference approximation replaces each partial derivative with:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x) \approx \frac{f(x + \delta e_i) - f(x)}{\delta}.
\end{equation}
The approximation error decomposes as:
\begin{equation}
\left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x) - \frac{f(x+\delta e_i)-f(x)}{\delta}\right| \leq \underbrace{\frac{L_i}{2}\delta}_{\text{truncation}} + \underbrace{\frac{2\varepsilon_{\rm machine}}{\delta}}_{\text{round-off}},
\end{equation}
where $L_i = \|\partial^2 f/\partial x_i^2\|_\infty$ is the second-derivative bound and $\varepsilon_{\rm machine}\approx10^{-16}$. Minimising over $\delta$ gives the optimal perturbation $\delta^\star = 2\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\rm machine}/L_i}\approx 10^{-8}$. The experiments test $\delta=0.05$ (much larger than optimal but still small relative to problem scale) and $\delta=0.8$ (large enough to cause significant truncation error, since $L_{x_1}=\|2-\sin(x_1)\|\leq3$, giving truncation error $\geq0.8\times3/2=1.2$ per component).

Settings: good FD: $\alpha=0.08$, $\delta=0.05$, 120 iterations. Poor FD: $\alpha=0.08$, $\delta=0.8$, 120 iterations.

\subsubsection{Nesterov Random Search}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Nesterov Random Search (Zeroth-Order)}
\label{alg:nrs}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, perturbation $\delta$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State Sample $u_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, normalise: $u_k \gets u_k / \|u_k\|$ \Comment{Random unit vector on $S^{n-1}$}
    \State $\hat{g}_k \gets \dfrac{f(x_k + \delta u_k) - f(x_k)}{\delta}\, u_k$ \Comment{Directional derivative estimate}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \hat{g}_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

This zeroth-order method estimates a directional derivative along a random direction, requiring only \emph{two} function evaluations per iteration regardless of dimension $d$. The expected estimator satisfies $\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_k] = \frac{1}{d}\nabla f(x_k) + O(\delta^2)$, so on average it is proportional to the true gradient with constant $1/d$. The effective step size is thus $\alpha/d$ relative to the exact gradient, explaining slower convergence for large $d$. Settings: $\alpha=0.025$, $\delta=0.08$, 220 iterations.

\subsection{Part B: Derivative-Free Methods (Benchmark C)}

\subsubsection{Nelder--Mead Simplex Method}

The Nelder--Mead algorithm maintains a simplex of $n+1=3$ vertices in $\mathbb{R}^2$ and iteratively transforms it through four operations:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Reflection} ($\alpha_r=1$): Move worst vertex $x_n$ through the centroid $c$ of the remaining vertices: $x_r = c + \alpha_r(c - x_n)$.
\item \textbf{Expansion} ($\gamma=2$): If $f(x_r) < f(x_0)$ (better than best), try further expansion: $x_e = c + \gamma(c - x_n)$.
\item \textbf{Contraction} ($\rho=0.5$): If reflection failed, contract: $x_c = c + \rho(x_r - c)$ (outside) or $x_c = c + \rho(x_n - c)$ (inside).
\item \textbf{Shrink} ($\sigma=0.5$): If all else fails, contract all vertices towards best: $x_i \gets x_0 + \sigma(x_i - x_0)$.
\end{enumerate}
No gradient information is required; the method adapts the simplex shape to the local landscape geometry. Settings: initial simplex step $=0.35$, 160 iterations.

\label{sec:q5}

\subsection{Problem Setup}

Minimise Benchmark~B subject to $x_1 \geq 0.5$, starting from the infeasible point $x_0 = (0.2, 4.0)$:
\begin{equation}
\min_{x\in\mathcal{F}} f(x_1,x_2) = (x_1-1)^2 + 5(x_2-2)^2 + \sin(x_1), \quad \mathcal{F} = \{x: x_1\geq0.5\}.
\end{equation}
The unconstrained minimum is at $(0.582, 2.0)$ with $f^\star=0.7244$. Since $0.582>0.5$, the constrained optimum coincides with the unconstrained optimum (the constraint is inactive at the solution). This is an important observation: the two methods should ultimately converge to the same point, but differ in \emph{how} they handle the initial infeasibility.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Projected Gradient Descent}
\label{alg:pgd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, projection $\Pi_{\mathcal{F}}$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \nabla f(x_k)$ \Comment{Unconstrained gradient step}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets \Pi_{\mathcal{F}}(z_{k+1}) = \begin{bmatrix}\max(0.5,\,z_{k+1,1})\\ z_{k+1,2}\end{bmatrix}$ \Comment{Euclidean projection onto $\mathcal{F}$}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Penalty Method}
\label{alg:penalty}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, penalty weight $\lambda$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $F_{\lambda}(x) \gets f(x) + \lambda\,\max(0,\, -x_1 + 0.5)$ \Comment{Penalised objective}
    \State $\nabla F_\lambda(x) \gets \nabla f(x) + \lambda\,\mathbf{1}[x_1<0.5]\cdot(-e_1)$ \Comment{Subgradient of penalty}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \nabla F_{\lambda}(x_k)$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The projection $\Pi_{\mathcal{F}}$ is non-expansive: $\|\Pi(x) - \Pi(y)\| \leq \|x-y\|$ for all $x,y$, which ensures the convergence theory of gradient descent transfers to projected gradient descent. The penalty parameter $\lambda$ controls the trade-off: as $\lambda\to\infty$, the penalty method solution converges to the constrained optimum, but larger $\lambda$ stiffens the objective and requires smaller step sizes.

Settings: Projected GD ($\alpha = 0.08$); Penalty with $\lambda \in \{0.15, 1.8, 4.5\}$ ($\alpha = 0.05$ for $\lambda \leq 1.8$; $\alpha = 0.03$ for $\lambda = 4.5$); unconstrained GD ($\alpha = 0.07$). All run for 100 iterations.

\subsection{Feasible Set and Linear Programme}

Feasible set: $\mathcal{X} = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^2 : 0.5 \leq x_1 \leq 5,\ -5 \leq x_2 \leq 10\}$.

\textbf{Linear Programme:} For $f(x) = a^T x$ with $a = [1, 2]^T$, the minimum over $\mathcal{X}$ is obtained by minimising each coordinate independently (since $a>0$ coordinatewise and $\mathcal{X}$ is a box):
\begin{equation}
x^{\star} = (0.5,\, -5), \qquad f^{\star} = 1\cdot0.5 + 2\cdot(-5) = -9.5.
\end{equation}
This illustrates the fundamental theorem of linear programming: the optimum of a linear objective over a bounded polytope is attained at a vertex (extreme point). The level sets of $f(x) = x_1 + 2x_2$ are lines with slope $-1/2$; the minimum is at the vertex where the objective line first enters (``from above'') the feasible region.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/q6_lp.pdf}
\caption{Q6-I: Feasible region $\mathcal{X}$ (blue box) and linear objective contours $f(x) = x_1 + 2x_2$. The contour lines (parallel lines with slope $-1/2$) decrease towards the lower-left corner $(0.5,-5)$. Since the objective is linear, the minimum over any convex polytope is attained at a vertex, and the vertex $(0.5,-5)$ is identified as the unique minimiser. This analytical result ($f^\star = -9.5$) serves as the reference point for the Frank--Wolfe analysis.}
\label{fig:q6_lp}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Frank--Wolfe Algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Frank--Wolfe (Conditional Gradient)}
\label{alg:fw}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0 \in \mathcal{X}$, mixing parameter $\beta$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_k \gets \arg\min_{x \in \mathcal{X}}\, \nabla f(x_k)^T x$ \Comment{Linear minimisation oracle (LMO)}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets \beta\, x_k + (1 - \beta)\, z_k$ \Comment{Convex combination (stays in $\mathcal{X}$)}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

For box constraints, the LP subproblem decomposes per coordinate:
\begin{equation}
(z_k)_i = \begin{cases} l_i & \text{if } (\nabla f(x_k))_i > 0 \\ u_i & \text{if } (\nabla f(x_k))_i \leq 0 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
This requires $O(d)$ operations per iteration, compared to $O(d^2)$ or more for general projections. Since $x_{k+1}$ is a convex combination of $x_k\in\mathcal{X}$ and $z_k\in\mathcal{X}$, the iterate \emph{always remains feasible} --- no projection is needed.

\begin{theorem}[Frank--Wolfe Convergence]
For an $L$-smooth convex function $f$ over a compact convex set $\mathcal{X}$ with diameter $D=\max_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\|x-y\|$, the Frank--Wolfe algorithm with step size $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}
f(x_k) - f^\star \leq \frac{2LD^2}{k+2} = O(1/k).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
The $O(1/k)$ rate is tight in general (cannot be improved for Frank--Wolfe). The fixed $\beta$ schedule used here corresponds to a constant step $1-\beta$ towards the LP vertex; this is a simplified variant that does not achieve the theoretical optimum but is practical.

The \textbf{Frank--Wolfe gap} $g_k = \nabla f(x_k)^T(x_k - z_k)$ provides a computable upper bound on the suboptimality: $f(x_k) - f^\star \leq g_k$. This makes Frank--Wolfe self-certifying: the gap can be used as a stopping criterion without knowing $f^\star$.

The unconstrained minimum $(1, 5)$ lies strictly inside $\mathcal{X}$ (since $0.5<1<5$ and $-5<5<10$). Two mixing parameters are tested: $\beta = 0.90$ (10\% step towards LP vertex per iteration) and $\beta = 0.985$ (1.5\% step). Starting point: $x_0=(1,1)$, 180 iterations.

\subsection{Interior vs Boundary Behaviour}

\textbf{Interior optimum:} Frank--Wolfe converges but exhibits a characteristic ``zig-zag'' oscillation. The LP vertex $z_k$ jumps between extreme points of $\mathcal{X}$ at each iteration, and the convex combination slowly averages these jumps. The convergence rate is $O(1/k)$. Smaller $\beta$ (larger steps towards $z_k$) can accelerate initial convergence but induces more oscillation near the optimum.

\textbf{Boundary optimum:} Frank--Wolfe is particularly well-suited to boundary optima because $z_k$ consistently selects the vertex closest to the constrained optimum direction. The iterate naturally approaches the boundary without explicit projection. For $f(x)=x_1^2+x_2^2$: since $\nabla f_{x_1} = 2x_1 > 0$ for all $x_1\in(0.5,5)$, the LP always selects $z_{k,1}=0.5$, correctly pushing the iterate towards the boundary. The residual gap ($f=0.364$ vs $f^\star=0.25$ after 140 iterations) indicates the boundary optimum converges more slowly than the interior case with 180 iterations and $\beta=0.985$, because the $x_2$ component oscillates as $z_{k,2}$ alternates between $-5$ and $10$.

\textbf{Comparison with Projected GD:} Frank--Wolfe avoids projection (requires only a linear minimisation oracle) but converges at $O(1/k)$ vs projected GD's $O(1/k)$ for convex functions, $O(e^{-\mu k/L})$ for strongly convex. Frank--Wolfe's advantage is the LMO, which for structured sets (sparse, low-rank, flow polytopes) can be computed in closed form faster than projection.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

This study demonstrates that no single optimisation algorithm dominates across all problem settings: the best choice depends on the problem structure, available information, and computational budget.

\textbf{Adaptive methods (Q1--2):} When exact gradients are available, Nesterov acceleration achieves the optimal $O(1/k^2)$ rate for smooth convex functions and provides the strongest empirical performance on the ill-conditioned Rosenbrock function. Adam is robust and self-tuning but lacks the theoretical acceleration on non-quadratic objectives. Adagrad and RMSprop provide stable, automatic step-size control suitable for problems with heterogeneous coordinate scales. The Polyak step is theoretically optimal when $f^\star$ is known, but this requirement limits its practical applicability.

\textbf{Second-order methods (Q3):} Newton's method dramatically outperforms first-order methods when the Hessian is available and the problem is well-approximated locally by a quadratic (one-step convergence on exact quadratics; rapid convergence on near-quadratic problems). The main cost is computing and inverting the Hessian ($O(d^3)$ per iteration), which becomes prohibitive for large $d$. Quasi-Newton methods (e.g., L-BFGS) approximate the Hessian to retain second-order benefits at lower cost.

\textbf{Derivative-free methods (Q4):} Forward finite differences provide near-exact gradient information at $O(d)$ function evaluations per step. Nesterov random search uses only 2 evaluations per step and is dimension-scalable but converges slowly. Nelder--Mead is practical for low-dimensional black-box problems. Grid search is global but suffers from the curse of dimensionality beyond $d\approx3$.

\textbf{Constrained optimisation (Q5--6):} Projected gradient descent guarantees feasibility at every iteration and is simple when projection is cheap. Penalty methods require no projection oracle but need careful tuning of $\lambda$ and may require smaller step sizes for large $\lambda$. Frank--Wolfe is natural for linear-constrained problems with cheap linear oracle calls, achieving $O(1/k)$ convergence while maintaining exact feasibility throughout.

def gradient_descent(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Polyak Step Size}]
def polyak_step(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, f_star, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        alpha_k = (loss_fn(x) - f_star) / (np.dot(g, g) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(alpha_k)
        x = x - alpha_k * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Adagrad}]
def adagrad(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha0, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    G = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        G += g**2
        eff_alpha = alpha0 / (np.sqrt(G) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(np.mean(eff_alpha))
        x = x - eff_alpha * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={RMSprop}]
def rmsprop(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha0, beta, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    v = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        v = beta * v + (1 - beta) * g**2
        eff_alpha = alpha0 / (np.sqrt(v) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(np.mean(eff_alpha))
        x = x - eff_alpha * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

def heavy_ball(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    z = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        z = beta * z + alpha * g
        x = x - z
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Nesterov Momentum}]
def nesterov_momentum(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta_max, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    z = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for k in range(1, n_iters + 1):
        beta_k = min((k - 1) / (k + 2), beta_max)
        lookahead = x + beta_k * z
        g = grad_fn(lookahead)
        z = beta_k * z - alpha * g
        x = x + z
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Adam Optimiser}]
def adam_optimiser(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta1, beta2, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    m_vec, v_vec = np.zeros_like(x), np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for t in range(1, n_iters + 1):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        m_vec = beta1 * m_vec + (1 - beta1) * g
        v_vec = beta2 * v_vec + (1 - beta2) * g**2
        m_hat = m_vec / (1 - beta1**t)
        v_hat = v_vec / (1 - beta2**t)
        x = x - alpha * m_hat / (np.sqrt(v_hat) + eps)
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Newton's Method with Damping}]
def newtons_method(grad_fn, hess_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, n_iters, damping=1e-8):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, update_norms = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        H = hess_fn(x) if callable(hess_fn) else hess_fn
        H_reg = H + damping * np.eye(len(x))
        try:
            p = np.linalg.solve(H_reg, g)
        except np.linalg.LinAlgError:
            p = g
        update_norms.append(np.linalg.norm(alpha * p))
        x = x - alpha * p
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(update_norms)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Frank--Wolfe Algorithm}]
def frank_wolfe(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, bounds, beta, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, z_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        z = np.array([bounds[i][0] if g[i] > 0 else bounds[i][1]
                       for i in range(len(g))])
        z_hist.append(z.copy())
        x = beta * x + (1 - beta) * z
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(z_hist)
\end{lstlisting}
