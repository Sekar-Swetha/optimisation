\begin{abstract}
This report presents a comprehensive comparative study of six families of optimisation algorithms applied to a suite of benchmark functions of varying difficulty, from a simple convex quadratic (condition number $\kappa \approx 1.12$) through a lightly non-convex toy neural-network loss ($\kappa \approx 6.9$) to the severely ill-conditioned Rosenbrock function ($\kappa \approx 2508$).
The methods investigated are:
(1)~adaptive step-size methods --- Polyak, Adagrad, RMSprop, and Heavy Ball;
(2)~momentum and stochastic methods --- Nesterov acceleration, Adam, and mini-batch SGD with variance reduction;
(3)~Newton's method with Hessian regularisation, quasi-Newton (BFGS/L-BFGS) extensions, and local second-order approximations;
(4)~derivative-free and zeroth-order approaches --- forward and central finite differences, Nesterov random search, Nelder--Mead simplex, and grid search;
(5)~constrained optimisation via projected gradient descent, penalty methods, and the augmented Lagrangian method (ALM); and
(6)~the Frank--Wolfe conditional-gradient algorithm for linear-constrained problems, with extensions to structured constraint sets.
Each method is evaluated with respect to convergence speed, trajectory behaviour, stability under misspecification, and computational cost per iteration.
Formal convergence theorems are stated throughout, including the Nesterov $O(1/k^2)$ optimal rate theorem with its Nemirovski--Yudin lower bound certificate, Newton's quadratic local convergence theorem, the SGD noise floor bound, and the Frank--Wolfe $O(1/k)$ diameter-based convergence theorem.
The experiments confirm several key theoretical predictions: Newton's method achieves \emph{one-step} convergence on the quadratic benchmark ($\kappa = 1.12$); Nesterov acceleration achieves the optimal $O(1/k^2)$ rate confirmed on a log-log plot; Polyak's step size diverges whenever $f^\star$ is misspecified by as little as $0.4826$; projected gradient descent enforces feasibility as a hard constraint from the first iteration; and the augmented Lagrangian method achieves exact constraint satisfaction with finite penalty parameter, overcoming the fundamental limitation of penalty methods.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================

Optimisation is the backbone of modern machine learning and data analysis.
Selecting the right algorithm for a given problem requires understanding both the mathematical properties of the objective function and the computational constraints of the application.
This report systematically investigates six families of methods, moving from simple gradient descent baselines to more sophisticated adaptive, second-order, and constrained approaches.

Three properties of the objective function fundamentally govern algorithm behaviour:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{$L$-smoothness} ($L > 0$): $\|\nabla f(x) - \nabla f(y)\| \leq L\|x - y\|$ for all $x, y$. The smoothness constant $L$ bounds the maximum curvature and controls the largest safe step size: $\alpha \leq 1/L$ guarantees sufficient decrease for gradient descent.
\item \textbf{Strong convexity} ($\mu > 0$): $f(y) \geq f(x) + \nabla f(x)^T(y-x) + \frac{\mu}{2}\|y-x\|^2$. This ensures a unique minimum and linear convergence of gradient descent at rate $(1 - \mu/L)$ per iteration.
\item \textbf{Condition number} $\kappa = L/\mu$: A high condition number indicates an anisotropic landscape in which gradient descent zig-zags, motivating momentum, adaptive, or second-order methods.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Notation.}
Throughout this report: $f^\star = \min_{x} f(x)$ denotes the optimal value; $x^\star$ the optimal point; $\nabla f$ the gradient; $\nabla^2 f$ the Hessian; $\|\cdot\|$ the Euclidean norm; $\odot$ element-wise product; $I_d$ the $d \times d$ identity; $\mathcal{O}$ big-O notation; $\mathbb{E}[\cdot]$ expectation; and $\text{Var}[\cdot]$ variance.
All experiments use \texttt{np.random.seed(42)} for reproducibility.

Figure~\ref{fig:rate_hierarchy} summarises the key convergence rate distinctions that run throughout this report: the $O(1/k)$ vs $O(1/k^2)$ divide for convex objectives, the linear vs $\sqrt{\kappa}$ speedup from strong convexity, and the doubly-exponential acceleration from Newton's quadratic convergence.

\subsection{Benchmark A: Linear Regression Quadratic Loss}
A synthetic dataset with $m = 1000$ samples and $d = 2$ parameters is generated:
\begin{equation}
\theta^{\star} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad x^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_2), \quad y^{(i)} = \theta^{\star T} x^{(i)} + \varepsilon^{(i)}, \quad \varepsilon^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1).
\end{equation}
The loss function is the mean squared error:
\begin{equation}
J(\theta) = \frac{1}{2m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \left(\theta^T x^{(i)} - y^{(i)}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{2}\theta^T H \theta - b^T\theta + c,
\end{equation}
where $H = \frac{1}{m}X^T X \succ 0$, $b = \frac{1}{m}X^T y$, and $c = \frac{1}{2m}\|y\|^2$.
This is a \emph{strongly convex quadratic} with constant Hessian $H$.
The gradient is $\nabla J(\theta) = \frac{1}{m}X^T(X\theta - y) = H\theta - b$.
Due to observation noise, the irreducible minimum is $J^{\star} \approx 0.4826$, attained at the OLS solution $\theta_{\text{OLS}} = H^{-1}b \approx [3.03, 4.02]^T$.
With the given seed and $m = 1000$ Gaussian samples, the empirical Hessian has eigenvalues $\lambda_{\min}(H) \approx 0.9247$ and $\lambda_{\max}(H) \approx 1.0325$, giving $L \approx 1.033$, $\mu \approx 0.925$, and $\kappa = L/\mu \approx 1.12$ (near-isotropic, very well-conditioned).
The optimal GD step $2/(L + \mu) \approx 1.02$ is much larger than the conservative $\alpha = 0.08$ used in experiments; this conservative choice avoids oscillation near the noise floor.
Starting point: $\theta_0 = [0, 0]^T$.

\subsection{Benchmark B: Toy Neural Network Loss}
\begin{equation}
f(x_1, x_2) = (x_1 - 1)^2 + 5(x_2 - 2)^2 + \sin(x_1).
\end{equation}
The gradient is $\nabla f = [2(x_1 - 1) + \cos(x_1),\ 10(x_2 - 2)]^T$.
The Hessian is $H(x) = \operatorname{diag}(2 - \sin(x_1),\ 10)$, which is positive definite wherever $\sin(x_1) < 2$ (always true globally).
At the minimum $x_1^\star \approx 0.582$, $\sin(0.582) \approx 0.548$, giving $H(x^\star) = \operatorname{diag}(1.450, 10)$ with condition number $\kappa = 10/1.450 \approx 6.9$.
At the starting point $x_1 = -1$, $H = \operatorname{diag}(2.842, 10)$, so $\kappa_{\text{start}} \approx 3.5$.
The minimum satisfies $2(x_1^{\star} - 1) + \cos(x_1^{\star}) = 0$, yielding $x_1^{\star} \approx 0.582$, $x_2^{\star} = 2.0$, and $f^{\star} \approx 0.7244$.
Starting point: $x_0 = [-1, 4]^T$.

\subsection{Benchmark C: Rosenbrock Function}
\begin{equation}
f(x_1, x_2) = (1 - x_1)^2 + 100(x_2 - x_1^2)^2.
\end{equation}
The global minimum is at $(1, 1)$ with $f^{\star} = 0$.
At the minimum, the Hessian is
$H(1,1) = \bigl[\begin{smallmatrix} 802 & -400 \\ -400 & 200 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr]$
with eigenvalues $\lambda_{\min} \approx 0.399$ and $\lambda_{\max} \approx 1001.6$, giving $\kappa = L/\mu \approx 2508$.
Identical extreme conditioning holds at the starting point $(-1, 1)$.
This extreme ill-conditioning, combined with the narrow curved valley along $x_2 = x_1^2$, makes Rosenbrock one of the most challenging standard test functions.
The optimal GD step size $2/(L+\mu) \approx 0.002$ (used $\alpha = 0.0012$) yields a convergence rate of $1 - 2\mu/(L+\mu) \approx 0.9996$ per iteration, requiring thousands of steps to converge.
The function is non-convex globally but strongly convex in a neighbourhood of the optimum.
Starting point: $x_0 = [-1, 1]^T$.

\subsection{Benchmark D: Box-Constrained Feasible Set}

\section{Question 1: Adaptive Step-Size Methods \hfill [30 marks]}

\subsection{Theoretical Background}

\paragraph{Regularity conditions.}
Gradient descent theory rests on two standard assumptions. First, $f$ is \emph{$L$-smooth}: $\|\nabla f(x) - \nabla f(y)\| \leq L\|x-y\|$ for all $x, y$, equivalent to the quadratic upper bound
\[
  f(y) \leq f(x) + \nabla f(x)^T(y-x) + \tfrac{L}{2}\|y-x\|^2.
\]
Second, $f$ is \emph{$\mu$-strongly convex}: $f(y) \geq f(x) + \nabla f(x)^T(y-x) + \tfrac{\mu}{2}\|y-x\|^2$ for all $x, y$, with $0 < \mu \leq L$.
The condition number $\kappa = L/\mu \geq 1$ governs the convergence rate: $\kappa = 1$ (spherical quadratic) gives single-step convergence; Rosenbrock has $\kappa \approx 2508$, explaining slow first-order convergence.

Gradient descent with constant step size $\alpha$ converges at the linear rate
\begin{equation}
f(x_k) - f^\star \leq \left(1 - \frac{\mu}{L}\right)^k \bigl(f(x_0) - f^\star\bigr)
\end{equation}
for $\mu$-strongly convex, $L$-smooth objectives with $\alpha = 1/L$.
The optimal step is $\alpha^\star = 2/(L + \mu)$, giving the better rate $(1 - 2\mu/(L+\mu))^k = ((\kappa - 1)/(\kappa + 1))^k$.

\begin{remark}[Polyak-\L{}ojasiewicz condition]
Strong convexity is sufficient but not necessary for linear convergence.
The weaker \emph{Polyak--\L{}ojasiewicz (PL) condition} requires only:
\[
  \tfrac{1}{2}\|\nabla f(x)\|^2 \geq \mu(f(x) - f^\star) \quad \forall x.
\]
Under PL, gradient descent with step $1/L$ satisfies $f(x_k) - f^\star \leq (1-\mu/L)^k(f(x_0) - f^\star)$---the \emph{same} rate as strongly convex functions---without requiring the unique-minimiser structure.
Many non-convex functions (including over-parameterised neural networks) satisfy PL locally, explaining linear convergence of gradient descent near the minimum observed in practice.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Backtracking line search.}
In practice, $L$ is often unknown.
Backtracking line search (Armijo, 1966) selects $\alpha_k$ without knowledge of $L$: start from $\alpha_k = 1$ and repeatedly halve until the \emph{sufficient decrease condition} $f(x_k - \alpha_k g_k) \leq f(x_k) - c\alpha_k\|g_k\|^2$ holds (with $c = 0.1$--$0.5$).
This guarantees the same convergence rate as optimal constant step with $L$ known, but with at most $\log_2(L/\alpha_{\rm init})$ extra evaluations per step.
The Polyak step, Adagrad, and RMSprop are alternative approaches to step-size adaptation that avoid line search.

Adaptive methods aim to remove the dependence on knowing $L$ a priori and to accelerate convergence by exploiting curvature information accumulated over iterations.

\subsection{Algorithm Formulations}

Four adaptive methods are compared against a constant step-size gradient descent baseline across all three benchmarks for 120 iterations.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Polyak Step Size}
\label{alg:polyak}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, optimal value $f^{\star}$, tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $\alpha_k \gets \dfrac{f(x_k) - f^{\star}}{\|g_k\|^2 + \varepsilon}$
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha_k\, g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The Polyak step size is theoretically optimal: for a convex function with known $f^\star$, it achieves the fastest worst-case convergence among all constant step-size rules, with rate $\sqrt{(1 - \mu/L)}$ per iteration for strongly convex problems.
The formula is derived by choosing the step that minimises the \emph{squared distance to the optimum}:
\[
  \alpha_k = \arg\min_\alpha \|x_k - \alpha g_k - x^\star\|^2
  \implies \alpha_k = \frac{g_k^T(x_k - x^\star)}{\|g_k\|^2}.
\]
Since $x^\star$ is unknown, the numerator is replaced using the convexity bound $g_k^T(x_k - x^\star) \geq f(x_k) - f^\star$, giving $\alpha_k \geq (f(x_k) - f^\star)/\|g_k\|^2$.
With the exact lower bound (substituting $f(x_k) - f^\star$ for the numerator), the formula achieves the minimum possible $\|x_{k+1} - x^\star\|^2$ compatible with convexity at each step.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Adagrad}
\label{alg:adagrad}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, base learning rate $\alpha_0$, tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$, iterations $N$
\State $G_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $G_{k+1} \gets G_k + g_k \odot g_k$ \Comment{Cumulative squared gradient, per coordinate}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \dfrac{\alpha_0}{\sqrt{G_{k+1}} + \varepsilon} \odot g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Adagrad automatically scales the step size per coordinate: dimensions with large historical gradients receive smaller effective steps.
Its effective step for coordinate $i$ after $k$ iterations is
$\alpha_k^{(i)} = \alpha_0 / \sqrt{\sum_{t=0}^{k} g_t^{(i)2}}$,
which decays monotonically --- an advantage on sparse problems but a limitation on dense problems, as the step eventually shrinks to zero.

\begin{theorem}[Adagrad regret bound, \emph{Duchi et al.~2011}]
For a convex objective, a bounded domain with diameter $D_\infty$, and sub-gradients $\|g_t\|_\infty \leq G$, Adagrad with $\alpha_0 = D_\infty$ achieves the regret bound
\[
  R(T) = \sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl[f(x_t) - f(x^\star)\bigr] \leq D_\infty \sum_{i=1}^{d} \sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T} g_{t,i}^2}.
\]
In particular, if only $s \ll d$ coordinates have non-zero gradients (sparse setting), the bound reduces to $O(s\sqrt{T})$ rather than the $O(d\sqrt{T})$ of constant-step subgradient methods---a factor $d/s$ improvement.
\end{theorem}

\noindent
This regret bound explains Adagrad's success on sparse data (e.g., NLP word embeddings) and its mediocre performance on dense, fully connected problems like our benchmarks: all coordinates receive large gradients, so the per-coordinate scaling provides no benefit over a global decay while causing the rate to shrink unnecessarily.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{RMSprop}
\label{alg:rmsprop}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, learning rate $\alpha_0$, decay $\beta \in (0,1)$, tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$, iterations $N$
\State $v_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $v_{k+1} \gets \beta\, v_k + (1 - \beta)\, g_k \odot g_k$ \Comment{Exponential moving average of $g^2$}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \dfrac{\alpha_0}{\sqrt{v_{k+1}} + \varepsilon} \odot g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

RMSprop addresses Adagrad's vanishing step-size problem by replacing the cumulative sum with an exponential moving average (EMA) with decay factor $\beta = 0.9$.
The EMA assigns weight $(1-\beta)\beta^j$ to the gradient squared $j$ steps ago, so recent gradients receive proportionally more influence.
The \emph{effective window length} of the EMA is $1/(1-\beta)$: for $\beta = 0.9$, this is 10 steps; for $\beta = 0.99$, it is 100 steps.
This allows the effective step size to adapt to local curvature rather than the entire history.

Figure~\ref{fig:rmsprop_ema} illustrates how the EMA window length $1/(1-\beta)$ governs both the weight decay and the convergence behaviour.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering

\caption{Q1: RMSprop EMA analysis. Left: convergence on Benchmark B for $\beta \in \{0.5, 0.7, 0.9, 0.95, 0.99\}$. Smaller $\beta$ (short effective window) adapts quickly but noisily; larger $\beta$ (long window) accumulates more history, converging more smoothly but requiring careful step-size tuning since the long-running EMA underestimates current curvature. \begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Polyak Momentum / Heavy Ball}
\label{alg:heavyball}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, momentum $\beta \in [0,1)$, iterations $N$
\State $z_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets \beta\, z_k + \alpha\, \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - z_{k+1}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The Heavy Ball method (also called Polyak momentum) introduces a velocity variable $z_k$ that accumulates a momentum term $\beta z_k$ from the previous step.
For strongly convex quadratics with condition number $\kappa$, the optimal parameters are $\beta^{\star} = \bigl((\sqrt{\kappa}-1)/(\sqrt{\kappa}+1)\bigr)^2$ and $\alpha^{\star} = 4/(\sqrt{L}+\sqrt{\mu})^2$, achieving a convergence rate of $\bigl((\sqrt{\kappa}-1)/(\sqrt{\kappa}+1)\bigr)^2 \approx 1 - 2/\sqrt{\kappa}$ --- a quadratic improvement in the exponent over vanilla GD's rate of $1 - 2/(\kappa+1)$.
For our benchmarks, the theoretically optimal Heavy Ball momentum parameters are: Bench.\ A: $\beta^\star \approx 0.001$ (near $\kappa \approx 1.12$, momentum barely helps); Bench.\ B: $\beta^\star \approx 0.201$ (rate $0.448$ vs GD rate $0.749$); Bench.\ C: $\beta^\star \approx 0.923$ (rate $0.961$ vs GD rate $0.9996$).
The tuned $\beta = 0.86$--$0.90$ values used in experiments are close to these optima, explaining why Heavy Ball substantially outperforms GD on Rosenbrock.

\begin{remark}[Chebyshev polynomials and optimal linear momentum]
For strongly convex quadratics, the optimal Heavy Ball method is equivalent to applying the \emph{Chebyshev iteration}: the iterate sequence $\{x_k\}$ minimises the error polynomial $\|p_k(H)(x_0 - x^\star)\|_H$ over all degree-$k$ polynomials satisfying $p_k(0) = 1$ (the ``zero residual'' condition).
The Chebyshev polynomial $T_k$ minimises the sup-norm on $[\mu, L]$ subject to $T_k(0) = 1$, yielding the convergence factor $\bigl((\sqrt{\kappa}-1)/(\sqrt{\kappa}+1)\bigr)^k$ --- the same rate as optimal Heavy Ball.
This connection to approximation theory proves that Heavy Ball is \emph{optimal} among all two-term linear recurrences for quadratic objectives.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Heavy Ball instability on non-quadratics]
The optimal Heavy Ball parameters ($\alpha^\star, \beta^\star$) are derived for \emph{quadratic} objectives; for general non-quadratic functions, Heavy Ball has \emph{no global convergence guarantee}.
Polyak (1987) and later Lessard et al.~(2016) showed that for non-quadratic strongly convex functions, the optimal quadratic parameters can cause divergence.
This explains why Heavy Ball underperforms Nesterov on Benchmark C (Rosenbrock): the changing curvature along the valley violates the quadratic assumption, and the fixed momentum $\beta = 0.86$ may be sub-optimal or even destabilising at different points along the trajectory.
Nesterov's adaptive schedule $\beta_k = (k-1)/(k+2)$, combined with the lookahead evaluation, provides convergence guarantees for general convex functions.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Subgradient method for non-smooth objectives]
All methods studied here assume $f$ is differentiable.
For non-smooth objectives (e.g.\ $f(x) = \|x\|_1$, hinge loss, or general convex functions with kinks), the gradient $\nabla f$ does not exist everywhere.
The \emph{subgradient} $g \in \partial f(x) = \{g : f(y) \geq f(x) + g^T(y-x)\ \forall y\}$ replaces the gradient, and the subgradient method uses the update $x_{k+1} = x_k - \alpha_k g_k$, $g_k \in \partial f(x_k)$.
Unlike gradient methods, the subgradient method does \emph{not} guarantee descent at every step; the iterate may increase in function value.
For convex objectives with $\|g\|_2 \leq G$, the subgradient method with diminishing steps $\alpha_k = R/(G\sqrt{k})$ achieves:
\[
  \min_{j \leq k} f(x_j) - f^\star \leq \frac{RG}{\sqrt{k}} = O(1/\sqrt{k}),
\]
where $R = \|x_0 - x^\star\|$.
This $O(1/\sqrt{k})$ rate is optimal for non-smooth convex objectives (Nemirovski \& Yudin, 1983).
Proximal gradient (ISTA/FISTA) extends first-order methods to the \emph{composite} setting $f + \psi$ (smooth $f$, non-smooth $\psi$) and achieves $O(1/k)$ or $O(1/k^2)$ rates by exploiting the structure of $\psi$ via its proximal operator.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Polyak divergence mechanism]
The Polyak step computes $\alpha_k = (f(x_k) - f^\star_{\text{spec}})/(\|g_k\|^2 + \varepsilon)$.
As iterates approach the true minimum $x^\star$ on Benchmark A, $\|g_k\| \to 0$ while $f(x_k) \to J^\star_A \approx 0.4826$.
Since $f^\star_{\text{spec}} = 0$, the numerator stays bounded away from zero while the denominator vanishes, making $\alpha_k \to \infty$.
The resulting large step overshoots the minimum, causing oscillation and eventual divergence.
This demonstrates why knowledge of $f^\star$ is not merely a technical assumption but a fundamental operational requirement.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Theoretical Background}

Vanilla gradient descent has convergence rate $O(1/k)$ for smooth convex functions.
Nesterov~(1983) showed that a simple momentum modification achieves the optimal $O(1/k^2)$ rate for the same problem class, matching the lower bound for first-order methods.
Adam combines momentum with adaptive per-coordinate scaling, making it the default optimiser for non-convex deep learning problems.

\subsection{Algorithm Formulations}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Nesterov Accelerated Gradient}
\label{alg:nesterov}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, max momentum $\beta_{\max}$, iterations $N$
\State $z_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 1, 2, \ldots, N$}
    \State $\beta_k \gets \min\!\left(\dfrac{k-1}{k+2},\ \beta_{\max}\right)$ \Comment{Adaptive momentum schedule}
    \State $\tilde{x}_k \gets x_k + \beta_k\, z_k$ \Comment{Lookahead / extrapolated point}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets \beta_k\, z_k - \alpha\, \nabla f(\tilde{x}_k)$
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k + z_{k+1}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The key distinction from Heavy Ball is the \emph{lookahead}: the gradient is evaluated at the extrapolated point $\tilde{x}_k = x_k + \beta_k z_k$ rather than $x_k$.
This ``corrective'' evaluation prevents the momentum term from overshooting the minimum because the gradient at the extrapolated point already anticipates the upcoming position.
The momentum schedule $\beta_k = (k-1)/(k+2)$ increases from $0$ to $\beta_{\max}$ as $k$ grows, with the specific schedule $\beta_k = (k-1)/(k+2)$ arising from the sequence $\lambda_k = (1 + \sqrt{1 + 4\lambda_{k-1}^2})/2$ that Nesterov used to prove the $O(1/k^2)$ rate.

Figure~\ref{fig:momentum_schedule} illustrates the adaptive momentum schedule and its effect on convergence across different $\beta_{\max}$ values.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/momentum_schedule_analysis.pdf}
\caption{Q2: Nesterov momentum schedule analysis. Left: GD vs Heavy Ball vs Nesterov on Rosenbrock --- Nesterov's accumulated directional momentum substantially outperforms both. Centre: the adaptive schedule $\beta_k = \min((k-1)/(k+2), \beta_{\max})$ for five values of $\beta_{\max}$. The schedule starts at 0 (pure gradient step) and saturates at $\beta_{\max}$; higher $\beta_{\max}$ allows more momentum accumulation. Right: effect of $\beta_{\max}$ on convergence speed for Benchmark B. Too low ($\beta_{\max} = 0.7$) under-accelerates; too high ($\beta_{\max} = 0.99$) causes oscillation from excessive momentum; $\beta_{\max} = 0.90$--$0.92$ is optimal for this landscape.}
\label{fig:momentum_schedule}
\end{figure}

\begin{theorem}[Nesterov acceleration, \emph{Nesterov 1983}]
For a convex, $L$-smooth function with $\alpha = 1/L$ and the standard momentum schedule $\beta_k = (k-1)/(k+2)$:
\[
  f(x_k) - f^\star \leq \dfrac{2L\|x_0 - x^\star\|^2}{(k+1)^2} = O(1/k^2).
\]
This rate is \emph{optimal}: no first-order algorithm can achieve $o(1/k^2)$ worst-case convergence on the class of convex $L$-smooth functions \emph{(Nemirovski--Yudin lower bound)}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}[Iteration complexity for $\varepsilon$-accuracy]
To achieve $f(x_k) - f^\star \leq \varepsilon$, gradient descent requires $O(L/\varepsilon)$ iterations, while Nesterov acceleration requires only $O(\sqrt{L/\varepsilon})$ iterations---a quadratic improvement in the dependence on $L/\varepsilon$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[Lower bound and optimality of Nesterov]
The Nemirovski--Yudin (1983) lower bound states: for any first-order algorithm (one that uses only $\nabla f(x_0), \ldots, \nabla f(x_{k-1})$ to choose $x_k$), there exist $L$-smooth convex functions where:
\[
  f(x_k) - f^\star \geq \frac{3L\|x_0 - x^\star\|^2}{32(k+1)^2}.
\]
The Nesterov rate $O(2L\|x_0-x^\star\|^2/(k+1)^2)$ matches this up to a constant factor of $\approx 21$, making Nesterov acceleration \emph{optimal in the worst case}.
In contrast, gradient descent achieves $O(1/k)$, which is also tight: the lower bound for GD is $\Omega(L/\varepsilon)$ (Drori \& Teboulle, 2014).
The $O(\sqrt{L/\varepsilon})$ vs $O(L/\varepsilon)$ gap between Nesterov and GD is not an artifact of the analysis --- it is a fundamental information-theoretic gap, proved by the hard function:
\[
  f_{\rm hard}(x) = \tfrac{L}{4}\left[\tfrac{3}{2}\|x\|^2 - \sum_{i=1}^{k} (x_i - x_{i-1})^2\right]
\]
(a ``trilinear Chebyshev'' function), which is L-smooth, convex, and forces any first-order method to achieve $\Omega(1/k^2)$ error.
\end{remark}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Adam Optimiser}
\label{alg:adam}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, learning rate $\alpha$, moment parameters $\beta_1, \beta_2 \in (0,1)$, tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$, iterations $N$
\State $m_0 \gets \mathbf{0},\ v_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$t = 1, 2, \ldots, N$}
    \State $g_t \gets \nabla f(x_t)$
    \State $m_t \gets \beta_1\, m_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_1)\, g_t$ \Comment{First moment (EMA of gradients)}
    \State $v_t \gets \beta_2\, v_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_2)\, g_t \odot g_t$ \Comment{Second moment (EMA of $g^2$)}
    \State $\hat{m}_t \gets m_t\, /\, (1 - \beta_1^t)$ \Comment{Bias-corrected first moment}
    \State $\hat{v}_t \gets v_t\, /\, (1 - \beta_2^t)$ \Comment{Bias-corrected second moment}
    \State $x_{t+1} \gets x_t - \alpha\, \hat{m}_t\, /\, (\sqrt{\hat{v}_t} + \varepsilon)$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Adam can be viewed as RMSprop with momentum: $m_t$ tracks the gradient direction (first moment), while $v_t$ tracks per-coordinate curvature (second moment).
The bias correction $1/(1-\beta_j^t)$ is critical in early iterations: without it, $m_t \approx (1-\beta_1)g_t$ is biased towards zero because the EMA initialises at $\mathbf{0}$.
The effective step size per coordinate is bounded as $|\Delta x_t^{(i)}| \leq \alpha(1-\beta_1)/\sqrt{1-\beta_2}$, giving reliable learning rate control.

\begin{remark}[Adam convergence and AMSGrad]
Reddi et al.~(2018) showed that Adam can \emph{fail to converge} on convex online optimisation problems: the exponential moving average of $v_t$ can ``forget'' large past gradients, causing the effective step size to increase spuriously and preventing convergence.
Their proposed fix, \emph{AMSGrad}, uses $\hat{v}_t = \max(\hat{v}_{t-1}, v_t)$ (a running maximum) instead of the direct EMA, restoring convergence guarantees while preserving Adam's adaptivity.
In practice on non-convex deep learning objectives, the failure modes of Adam are rarely observed, and Adam with careful learning-rate tuning remains the default optimiser for training neural networks.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Gradient clipping and heavy-tailed noise]
In deep learning, stochastic gradient norms can be extremely large due to sharp curvature regions (e.g., recurrent networks unrolled over long sequences), causing catastrophic step-size blowup.
\emph{Gradient clipping} truncates the gradient to a maximum norm $c$:
\[
  g_k^{\rm clip} = \min\!\left(1, \frac{c}{\|g_k\|}\right) g_k.
\]
This is equivalent to a Polyak step $\alpha_k = c / \|g_k\|$ when $\|g_k\| > c$, and a constant step when $\|g_k\| \leq c$.
Zhang et al.~(2020) proved that gradient clipping achieves convergence rates comparable to those of unclipped SGD under the \emph{$(L_0, L_1)$-smoothness} condition $\|\nabla^2 f(x)\| \leq L_0 + L_1\|\nabla f(x)\|$, which captures heavy-tailed gradient distributions better than standard $L$-smoothness.
Combined with Adam, gradient clipping is nearly universal in transformer and RNN training.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Mini-Batch Stochastic Gradient Descent}

Mini-batch SGD on Benchmark A uses a constant learning rate $\alpha = 0.06$ with batch sizes $b \in \{5, 40\}$, over 50 epochs with data shuffled at every epoch.
At each epoch, the dataset of $m = 1000$ samples is randomly permuted and split into mini-batches; the gradient is estimated from each mini-batch in turn.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Mini-Batch SGD}
\label{alg:sgd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Data $(X, y)$, initial $\theta_0$, learning rate $\alpha$, batch size $b$, epochs $E$
\For{$e = 1, 2, \ldots, E$}
    \State Randomly permute data indices $\{1, \ldots, m\}$
    \For{each mini-batch $\mathcal{B}$ of size $b$}
        \State $g \gets \frac{1}{b} X_{\mathcal{B}}^T(X_{\mathcal{B}}\theta - y_{\mathcal{B}})$ \Comment{Mini-batch gradient estimate}
        \State $\theta \gets \theta - \alpha\, g$
    \EndFor
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The mini-batch gradient $g_{\mathcal{B}}$ is an unbiased estimator of the full gradient $\nabla J(\theta)$ with variance $\text{Var}[g_{\mathcal{B}}^{(i)}] = \sigma^2_{\text{grad}} / b$, where $\sigma^2_{\text{grad}}$ is the per-sample gradient variance.
A larger batch size reduces gradient noise (at cost of more computation per step), while a smaller batch size introduces more stochasticity but allows more updates per epoch.

\begin{theorem}[SGD convergence and noise floor]
For a $\mu$-strongly convex, $L$-smooth objective, mini-batch SGD with constant learning rate $\alpha$ satisfies:
\[
  \mathbb{E}[f(\theta_k)] - f^\star \leq (1 - \mu\alpha)^k (f(\theta_0) - f^\star) + \frac{\alpha\sigma^2}{2\mu b},
\]
where $\sigma^2 = \mathbb{E}[\|g(\theta^\star; \xi)\|^2]$ is the per-sample gradient variance at the optimum.
The first term decays linearly; the second term is the irreducible \emph{noise floor} of size $O(\alpha\sigma^2/(\mu b))$.
To achieve $\varepsilon$-accuracy requires $\alpha = O(\varepsilon\mu b / \sigma^2)$ (small enough step) and $O(\sigma^2 / (\mu^2\varepsilon b))$ iterations.
\end{theorem}

\noindent
The noise floor explains the persistent oscillation of small-batch SGD near the optimum: reducing the batch size $b$ increases the floor, while decreasing the step size $\alpha$ reduces it but slows the initial convergence.
SVRG eliminates this tradeoff by driving $\sigma^2_{\rm effective} \to 0$ as iterates converge.

\subsection{Variance Reduction: SVRG and SAGA}

A fundamental limitation of mini-batch SGD is that gradient variance does not vanish as $\theta \to \theta^\star$: even near the optimum, each mini-batch provides a noisy estimate, causing persistent oscillation.
\emph{Variance reduction} methods eliminate this floor by incorporating a periodically-refreshed full gradient.

\textbf{SVRG} (Stochastic Variance Reduced Gradient, Johnson \& Zhang 2013): at the start of each epoch, compute the full gradient $\mu = \nabla J(\tilde\theta)$ at a snapshot point $\tilde\theta$.
Each stochastic step then uses the \emph{corrected} gradient:
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{g}_t = \nabla J_i(\theta_t) - \nabla J_i(\tilde\theta) + \mu,
\end{equation}
which is unbiased ($\mathbb{E}[\tilde{g}_t] = \nabla J(\theta_t)$) but has variance $O(\|\theta_t - \tilde\theta\|^2) \to 0$ as $\theta_t \to \theta^\star$.
For strongly convex functions, SVRG achieves \emph{linear convergence} $O(\rho^k)$ with $\rho < 1$---the same asymptotic rate as full GD, but with per-step cost comparable to SGD.

\textbf{SAGA} (Defazio et al.~2014): instead of a single snapshot, SAGA maintains a table of the last gradient for each data point.
The corrected gradient for sample $i$ is $\nabla J_i(\theta_t) - \nabla J_i(\phi_i) + \frac{1}{n}\sum_j \nabla J_j(\phi_j)$, where $\phi_i$ is the stored point for index $i$.
SAGA achieves the same linear convergence as SVRG but with better constant factors, and allows non-uniform sampling and proximal operators.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{SAGA (Defazio, Bach, Lacoste-Julien 2014)}
\label{alg:saga}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Step size $\alpha$, initialisation $\theta_0$, gradient table $g^{(i)} \gets \nabla J_i(\theta_0)$ for all $i$, $\bar{g} \gets \frac{1}{n}\sum_i g^{(i)}$
\For{$t = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$}
    \State Sample $i \sim \text{Uniform}(\{1,\ldots,n\})$
    \State Compute new gradient $v \gets \nabla J_i(\theta_t)$
    \State SAGA update: $\theta_{t+1} \gets \theta_t - \alpha\bigl(v - g^{(i)} + \bar{g}\bigr)$
    \State Update table: $g^{(i)} \gets v$; \quad $\bar{g} \gets \bar{g} + (v - g^{(i)\,\text{old}})/n$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\noindent \textbf{Key differences between SVRG and SAGA:}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{SVRG} requires a full pass over all $n$ data points once per epoch (to compute $\mu = \nabla J(\tilde\theta)$), then $O(m)$ stochastic steps with variance-reduced gradients. Memory: $O(d)$ (only the snapshot point $\tilde\theta$).
  \item \textbf{SAGA} avoids the full epoch pass but requires storing one gradient per data point: $O(nd)$ memory (e.g., $n=10^6$, $d=100$: 100M floats = 0.8 GB). SAGA achieves a tighter convergence constant than SVRG ($\rho_{\rm SAGA} \approx 1 - \mu\alpha$ vs $\rho_{\rm SVRG} \approx 1 - 1/(4\kappa)$ for optimal $\alpha$).
  \item \textbf{Proximal extension:} SAGA admits a proximal step $\theta_{t+1} = \mathrm{prox}_{\alpha\psi}(\theta_t - \alpha \tilde{g}_t)$ for composite objectives $J(\theta) + \psi(\theta)$ (e.g., $\ell_1$ regularisation), enabling sparse solutions.
\end{itemize}

\begin{remark}[Learning rate schedules for SGD]
Constant step size $\alpha$ causes the SGD noise floor $O(\alpha\sigma^2/(\mu b))$ to dominate at convergence.
The two main alternatives to avoid this are:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Diminishing step size}: $\alpha_k = \alpha_0 / (k+1)^\gamma$ with $\gamma \in (1/2, 1]$.
        The Robbins--Monro conditions $\sum_k \alpha_k = \infty$ and $\sum_k \alpha_k^2 < \infty$ guarantee convergence to $x^\star$ without the noise floor, but the $O(1/\sqrt{k})$ convergence is slow.
  \item \textbf{Cosine annealing / warm restarts}: decay $\alpha_k = \alpha_{\min} + \frac{1}{2}(\alpha_{\max} - \alpha_{\min})(1 + \cos(\pi k / T))$ with periodic restarts, widely used in deep learning.
  \item \textbf{Linear warmup}: ramp from $0$ to $\alpha_{\max}$ over the first $T_{\rm warmup}$ steps to allow the gradient estimates to stabilise before taking large steps; commonly combined with cosine decay in transformer training.
\end{enumerate}
The constant learning rate used in our experiments ($\alpha = 0.06$) is appropriate for demonstrating the batch-size tradeoff but would require scheduling for high-accuracy convergence in practice.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Local Approximations of $g(x) = x^4$}

At $x_0 = 0.25$, we compute:
$g(x_0) = 0.25^4 = 0.003906$,
$g'(x_0) = 4 \cdot 0.25^3 = 0.0625$,
$g''(x_0) = 12 \cdot 0.25^2 = 0.75$.

\textbf{First-order (Taylor) approximation:}
\begin{equation}
\hat{g}_1(x) = g(x_0) + g'(x_0)(x - x_0) = 0.003906 + 0.0625\,(x - 0.25).
\end{equation}
This tangent line has no minimum (it is unbounded below), so a gradient step on the first-order model would require choosing a step size $\alpha$ separately.

\textbf{Second-order approximation:}
\begin{equation}
\hat{g}_2(x) = g(x_0) + g'(x_0)(x - x_0) + \tfrac{1}{2}g''(x_0)(x - x_0)^2 = 0.003906 + 0.0625(x - 0.25) + 0.375(x - 0.25)^2.
\end{equation}

The Newton step minimises $\hat{g}_2$: setting $\hat{g}_2'(x) = 0$ gives the update
\begin{equation}
x_{k+1} = x_k - \frac{g'(x_k)}{g''(x_k)}.
\end{equation}
At $x_0 = 0.25$: $x_1 = 0.25 - 0.0625/0.75 = 0.25 - 0.0833 = 0.1667$.
Applying again from $x_1$: $x_2 \approx 0.057$, converging rapidly towards $x^\star = 0$.

\subsection{Newton's Method with Hessian Regularisation}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Newton's Method with Regularised Hessian}
\label{alg:newton}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha \in (0,1]$, damping $\lambda > 0$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $H_k \gets \nabla^2 f(x_k) + \lambda I$ \Comment{Tikhonov-regularised Hessian}
    \State $p_k \gets H_k^{-1}\, g_k$ \Comment{Newton direction: solve linear system}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, p_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The regularisation $\lambda I$ ensures $H_k \succ 0$ even when $\nabla^2 f(x_k)$ is indefinite or near-singular (as occurs on the Rosenbrock function far from the optimum).
The step $\alpha < 1$ (damping) provides a safety margin when the quadratic approximation is inaccurate globally.
When $\alpha = 1$ and $\lambda = 0$, this is pure Newton's method, which achieves \emph{quadratic} local convergence.

\begin{theorem}[Newton quadratic convergence]
Suppose $f$ is twice continuously differentiable, $\nabla^2 f(x^\star) \succ 0$, and $\nabla^2 f$ is $M$-Lipschitz continuous near $x^\star$.
Then there exists $r > 0$ such that for $\|x_0 - x^\star\| \leq r$:
\begin{equation}
\|x_{k+1} - x^\star\| \leq \frac{M}{2\,\mu}\,\|x_k - x^\star\|^2,
\end{equation}
where $\mu = \lambda_{\min}(\nabla^2 f(x^\star)) > 0$.
In particular, once $\|x_k - x^\star\| < 2\mu/M$, the number of correct decimal digits doubles each iteration.
\end{theorem}

\noindent
On Benchmark A (constant Hessian), $M = 0$ and Newton converges in exactly one step from any starting point.
On Benchmark B, $M = \|\nabla^3 f\| \approx \|\sin(x_1)\|_\infty \leq 1$ near $x^\star$, explaining the rapid but not single-step convergence observed.
On Rosenbrock, $M$ is large (the third-order tensor has large entries), so the quadratic phase only begins once iterates are close to $(1, 1)$; damping ($\alpha < 1$) is needed to enter this local basin.

\begin{remark}[Convergence order and the secant method]
The convergence order $p$ of a method is defined by $\|x_{k+1} - x^\star\| = O(\|x_k - x^\star\|^p)$.
Newton's method has $p = 2$ (quadratic), meaning errors square at each step.
The \emph{secant method} replaces the Hessian with a rank-1 finite difference approximation $B_k = (g_k - g_{k-1})/(x_k - x_{k-1})$, achieving order $p = (1 + \sqrt{5})/2 \approx 1.618$ (golden ratio), which lies between linear ($p=1$) and quadratic ($p=2$).
BFGS generalises the secant idea to $d > 1$ via rank-2 updates, achieving superlinear convergence ($p \to 2$ asymptotically).
A key distinction: quadratic convergence (Newton) requires $O(d^2)$ storage and $O(d^3)$ work per step; superlinear convergence (BFGS/L-BFGS) requires $O(d^2)$ or $O(md)$ storage with only $O(d^2)$ or $O(md)$ work, respectively. This hierarchy (GD $\to$ L-BFGS $\to$ BFGS $\to$ Newton) represents the fundamental tradeoff between iteration count and per-iteration cost.
\end{remark}

Newton's method offers \emph{quadratic} local convergence in contrast to GD's linear convergence.
The price is computing and inverting the $d \times d$ Hessian at each step, costing $O(d^2)$ per gradient evaluation and $O(d^3)$ for the linear solve --- prohibitive for large-scale problems (e.g.\ deep neural networks with $d \sim 10^6$--$10^{12}$).

\begin{remark}[Affine invariance of Newton's method]
A key theoretical property of Newton's method is \emph{affine invariance}: the Newton direction $H_k^{-1}\nabla f(x_k)$ is unchanged by any invertible linear transformation $x \mapsto Ax$.
This means Newton's convergence rate is independent of the condition number $\kappa$ --- unlike gradient descent, whose rate degrades as $O(\kappa \log(1/\varepsilon))$.
Specifically, for our Rosenbrock benchmark with $\kappa \approx 2508$, gradient descent requires $O(2508)$ iterations while Newton requires only $O(\log\log(1/\varepsilon))$ --- a factor $\sim 10^4$ improvement in iteration count for machine precision.
The affine invariance is the fundamental reason Newton's method converges in a fixed number of iterations on quadratics regardless of their conditioning: the Hessian exactly captures the anisotropy and corrects for it in a single step.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Self-concordance and global Newton convergence]
Standard Newton convergence proofs assume proximity to $x^\star$ (local convergence).
The concept of \emph{self-concordance} (Nesterov \& Nemirovski, 1994) extends this to a \emph{global} theory: a function $f$ is $M$-self-concordant if $|\nabla^3 f(x)[h,h,h]| \leq 2M (\nabla^2 f(x)[h,h])^{3/2}$ for all $x, h$.
Self-concordant functions include $-\log x$, $-\log(b - a^Tx)$, and quadratics (trivially).
Under self-concordance, the damped Newton method (step $\alpha_k = 1/(1 + \lambda(x_k))$ where $\lambda(x_k)$ is the Newton decrement) satisfies:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Damped phase} ($\lambda(x_k) \geq 1/4$): $f(x_{k+1}) \leq f(x_k) - M^{-2}\gamma$ for a universal constant $\gamma$; the method makes constant progress globally.
  \item \textbf{Quadratic phase} ($\lambda(x_k) < 1/4$): full Newton steps are taken; quadratic convergence applies.
\end{enumerate}
Self-concordance is why Newton's method with damping can be applied globally to interior-point methods without tuning, enabling polynomial-time algorithms for LP and SDP.
For our Rosenbrock benchmark, $f$ is not self-concordant (third derivative grows without bound), so Newton requires the Hessian regularisation $H + \delta I$ to handle indefiniteness away from $x^\star$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Hessian-free / conjugate gradient Newton]
When the Hessian-vector product $\nabla^2 f(x) v$ can be computed efficiently (e.g.\ via second-order backpropagation at cost $O(d)$), the Newton direction $p_k = H_k^{-1} g_k$ can be found by the conjugate gradient method applied to $H_k p = g_k$, without ever forming $H_k$ explicitly.
This \emph{Hessian-free Newton} or \emph{truncated Newton} method achieves superlinear convergence with $O(d\cdot r)$ cost per step, where $r \ll d$ is the number of CG iterations (typically the effective rank of $H_k$).
It is used in training large-scale neural networks (e.g.\ Martens 2010) and is competitive with L-BFGS on structured problems.
\end{remark}

\textbf{Quasi-Newton methods} approximate the inverse Hessian using gradient information accumulated over iterations.
The BFGS update rule maintains a positive definite approximation $B_k \approx \nabla^2 f(x_k)$ via:
\begin{equation}
B_{k+1} = B_k + \frac{y_k y_k^T}{y_k^T s_k} - \frac{B_k s_k s_k^T B_k}{s_k^T B_k s_k}, \quad s_k = x_{k+1} - x_k,\quad y_k = \nabla f(x_{k+1}) - \nabla f(x_k).
\end{equation}
This satisfies the quasi-Newton \emph{secant condition} $B_{k+1}s_k = y_k$ --- ensuring the approximation is consistent with the observed gradient change over the last step --- and achieves \emph{superlinear} convergence.

\begin{theorem}[BFGS superlinear convergence, \emph{Dixon 1972}]
For a twice continuously differentiable strictly convex function near $x^\star$ with $\nabla^2 f(x^\star) \succ 0$, BFGS with exact line search satisfies:
\[
  \lim_{k\to\infty} \frac{\|x_{k+1} - x^\star\|}{\|x_k - x^\star\|} = 0 \quad \text{(superlinear convergence)}.
\]
The rate is determined by how quickly $B_k \to \nabla^2 f(x^\star)^{-1}$; in practice the convergence resembles a rate between linear ($O(\rho^k)$) and quadratic ($O(\rho^{2^k})$).
\end{theorem}
The limited-memory variant L-BFGS stores only the last $m$ updates (typically $m = 5$--$20$), giving $O(md)$ storage and $O(md)$ per-step cost via the two-loop recursion --- the standard method for large-scale smooth unconstrained optimisation.

\begin{remark}[Newton decrement stopping criterion]
For Newton's method, the natural stopping criterion is the \emph{Newton decrement} $\lambda(x) = \sqrt{g^T H^{-1} g}$.
This satisfies $f(x) - f^\star \leq \lambda(x)^2/2$ for strongly convex $f$, providing a self-certified upper bound on the suboptimality gap.
At iteration $k$, if $\lambda(x_k)^2/2 \leq \varepsilon$, then $f(x_k) - f^\star \leq \varepsilon$.
The Newton decrement also equals $\|p_k\|_H = \sqrt{p_k^T H_k p_k}$ (the Newton step length in the Hessian norm), which decreases as iterates approach the optimum.
On Benchmark A, the Newton decrement drops from $\lambda_0 \approx 5.3$ (at $\theta_0 = [0,0]^T$) to $\lambda_1 < 10^{-8}$ after a single step, explaining the one-step convergence.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Why L-BFGS in practice]
Full BFGS requires $O(d^2)$ storage for the $d\times d$ approximation matrix, prohibitive for $d \geq 10^4$.
L-BFGS circumvents this by implicitly representing the inverse Hessian through the last $m$ ($s_k$, $y_k$) pairs, making it the \emph{de facto} standard for smooth optimisation in machine learning (L-BFGS-B in SciPy, L-BFGS in PyTorch).
On our benchmarks, L-BFGS would converge in $O(\sqrt{\kappa})$ iterations (superlinear rate) with only $O(md)$ cost per step, bridging the gap between first-order methods ($O(\kappa)$ or $O(\sqrt{\kappa})$ iterations, $O(d)$ per step) and full Newton ($O(\log\log 1/\varepsilon)$ iterations, $O(d^3)$ per step).
\end{remark}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{L-BFGS Two-Loop Recursion}
\label{alg:lbfgs}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial $x_0$, memory $m$, gradient $g_0 = \nabla f(x_0)$, empty lists $\{s_k\}$, $\{y_k\}$, $\{\rho_k\}$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State \textbf{Compute descent direction} $p_k$ via two-loop recursion:
    \State \quad $q \gets g_k$
    \For{$i = k-1, k-2, \ldots, k-m$} \Comment{backward pass}
        \State $\alpha_i \gets \rho_i\, s_i^T q$; \quad $q \gets q - \alpha_i\, y_i$
    \EndFor
    \State $r \gets \gamma_k\, q$ \quad where $\gamma_k = s_{k-1}^T y_{k-1} / (y_{k-1}^T y_{k-1})$ (initial Hessian scaling)
    \For{$i = k-m, \ldots, k-1$} \Comment{forward pass}
        \State $\beta \gets \rho_i\, y_i^T r$; \quad $r \gets r + s_i (\alpha_i - \beta)$
    \EndFor
    \State $p_k \gets -r$ \Comment{quasi-Newton descent direction}
    \State Choose $\alpha_k > 0$ via Wolfe line search; set $x_{k+1} \gets x_k + \alpha_k\, p_k$
    \State $s_k \gets x_{k+1} - x_k$; \quad $y_k \gets g_{k+1} - g_k$; \quad $\rho_k \gets 1/(y_k^T s_k)$
    \State Maintain only last $m$ pairs; discard oldest if $|\{s_i\}| > m$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\noindent The two-loop recursion computes the product $H_k^{-1} g_k$ implicitly in $O(md)$ time, where $H_k$ is the L-BFGS inverse Hessian approximation.
The secant condition $H_k y_{k-1} = s_{k-1}$ is satisfied by construction.
The scaling $\gamma_k = s_{k-1}^T y_{k-1}/\|y_{k-1}\|^2$ initialises the implicit inverse Hessian at each step using the most recent curvature information, providing a dimension-free adaptive learning rate.

\subsection{Part A: Finite Differences and Nesterov Random Search (Benchmark B)}

\subsubsection{Forward Finite-Difference Gradient Approximation}

The forward finite-difference (FD) approximation replaces each partial derivative with:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x) \approx \frac{f(x + \delta e_i) - f(x)}{\delta}.
\end{equation}
The truncation error from Taylor expansion is $O(\delta)$ (the omitted higher-order terms), while floating-point cancellation introduces an error of $O(\varepsilon_{\text{mach}}/\delta)$.
Specifically, a Taylor expansion gives:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{f(x + \delta e_i) - f(x)}{\delta} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x) + \underbrace{\frac{\delta}{2}\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i^2}(x)}_{\text{truncation error}} + \underbrace{O(\varepsilon_{\text{mach}}/\delta)}_{\text{cancellation error}}.
\end{equation}
The total error $\varepsilon_{\rm FD}(\delta) \approx \frac{|f''|}{2}\delta + \frac{\varepsilon_{\text{mach}}|f|}{|\delta|}$ is minimised at
$\delta_{\text{opt}} \approx \sqrt{\varepsilon_{\text{mach}} \cdot |f| / |f''|} \approx \sqrt{\varepsilon_{\text{mach}}} \approx 10^{-8}$,
giving an overall FD accuracy of $O(\varepsilon_{\text{mach}}^{1/2}) \approx 10^{-8}$.

\begin{remark}[Central differences]
The \emph{central} finite difference $\bigl[f(x + \delta e_i) - f(x - \delta e_i)\bigr]/(2\delta)$ cancels the $O(\delta)$ truncation term (the even terms cancel by symmetry), yielding $O(\delta^2)$ truncation error. The optimal $\delta$ then becomes $\delta_{\rm opt} \approx \varepsilon_{\rm mach}^{1/3} \approx 10^{-5.3}$, giving an FD accuracy of $O(\varepsilon_{\rm mach}^{2/3}) \approx 10^{-10.7}$. Central differences cost $2d$ function evaluations per gradient (vs $d+1$ for forward FD) but achieve two extra digits of accuracy.
\end{remark}

\subsubsection{Nesterov Random Search (Zeroth-Order Method)}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Nesterov Random Search}
\label{alg:nrs}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, perturbation $\delta$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State Sample $u_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, normalise: $u_k \gets u_k / \|u_k\|$ \Comment{Uniform on unit sphere}
    \State $\hat{g}_k \gets \dfrac{f(x_k + \delta u_k) - f(x_k)}{\delta}\, u_k$ \Comment{Directional derivative estimate}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \hat{g}_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Nesterov random search estimates a \emph{smoothed} gradient using only two function evaluations per step, regardless of dimension $d$.
Let $f_\delta(x) = \mathbb{E}_{u \sim \mathcal{U}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}[f(x + \delta u)]$ be the spherically-smoothed objective.
The estimator
\[
  \hat{g}_k = \frac{d}{\delta}\bigl[f(x_k + \delta u_k) - f(x_k)\bigr]u_k
\]
is an unbiased estimator of $\nabla f_\delta(x_k)$ (not $\nabla f(x_k)$ exactly), with bias $\|f_\delta - f\|_\infty = O(L\delta^2/2)$ from the smoothing.
The variance satisfies $\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{g}_k - \nabla f_\delta(x)\|^2] \leq d^2 M^2$ where $M = \sup_u |f(x + \delta u) - f(x)|/\delta$ is the range of directional derivatives.
This $d^2$ factor explains why random search converges at rate $O(d^2/\varepsilon)$ iterations --- $d$ times worse than the zeroth-order lower bound $O(d/\varepsilon)$, yet still far better than grid search's $O((1/\varepsilon)^d)$ evaluations.

\begin{remark}[Two-point estimator efficiency]
The two-point estimator $\hat{g}_k = (d/\delta)[f(x+\delta u) - f(x-\delta u)](u/2)$ (antithetic sampling) reduces variance by a factor of 4 with the same cost.
Duchi et al.~(2015) showed this achieves the minimax optimal sample complexity $O(d/\varepsilon^2)$ for non-smooth objectives.
In our experiments, the one-point estimator is used (consistent with the provided code), which explains the slower convergence relative to exact GD.
\end{remark}

The critical advantage is \emph{dimension-independence of cost}: only 2 function evaluations per step versus $d+1$ for forward FD.
This makes random search attractive in very high dimensions or when gradient computations are expensive.

\subsection{Part B: Derivative-Free Methods on Benchmark C (Rosenbrock)}

\subsubsection{Nelder--Mead Simplex Method}

The Nelder--Mead algorithm maintains a simplex of $n+1 = 3$ vertices in $\mathbb{R}^2$ and iteratively transforms it through four operations:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Reflection} ($\alpha_r = 1.0$): move the worst vertex through the centroid of the remaining vertices.
\item \textbf{Expansion} ($\gamma = 2.0$): if the reflection improved, try a further step in the same direction.
\item \textbf{Contraction} ($\rho = 0.5$): if the reflection worsened, contract towards the centroid.
\item \textbf{Shrink} ($\sigma = 0.5$): if contraction fails, contract all vertices towards the best.
\end{enumerate}
No gradient information is required: each step uses at most 1--3 function evaluations.
Settings: initial simplex step $= 0.35$, $N = 160$ iterations.

The simplex adapts its shape to the local landscape --- elongating along ridges and contracting near minima --- making it effective on smooth functions in low dimensions.

\begin{theorem}[Nelder--Mead convergence (McKinnon 1998)]
For $d \geq 2$, Nelder--Mead is \emph{not guaranteed to converge} to a stationary point.
McKinnon (1998) constructed an explicit $d=2$ example on a smooth strictly convex function where Nelder--Mead converges to a non-stationary point.
In practice, however, on smooth well-behaved functions in low dimensions ($d \leq 5$), Nelder--Mead typically converges at a superlinear rate before stagnation occurs.
\end{theorem}

Figure~\ref{fig:nm_simplex_evolution} shows how the simplex shape adapts over iterations on the Rosenbrock function: initially a large regular triangle, it elongates along the curved valley floor, then contracts progressively as it approaches the minimum at $(1, 1)$.

\subsubsection{Grid Search}

Grid search evaluates the objective on a uniform $55 \times 55 = 3025$-point grid over $x_1 \in [-2, 2]$, $x_2 \in [-1, 3]$.
The grid spacing is $\Delta x_1 = \Delta x_2 = 4/54 \approx 0.074$.
The best grid point found approximates the true optimum $(1, 1)$ to within $\|\hat{x}^\star - x^\star\| \leq \Delta x \sqrt{2} \approx 0.105$.

\begin{remark}[Curse of dimensionality]
The grid search cost scales as $N^d$ where $N$ is the number of grid points per dimension and $d$ is the problem dimension.
At the resolution $N = 55$ and tolerance $\Delta x \approx 0.074$ used here: $d=2$ costs $3{,}025$ evaluations; $d=5$ would cost $55^5 \approx 5 \times 10^8$; $d=10$ costs $55^{10} \approx 2.5 \times 10^{17}$ --- beyond all computation.
This \emph{curse of dimensionality} (Bellman 1957) means grid search is only practical for $d \leq 3$ at any reasonable resolution, and motivates all gradient-based and derivative-free iterative methods for higher-dimensional problems.
Bayesian optimisation (using a Gaussian process surrogate) is the practical global optimisation method for $d \leq 20$; evolutionary algorithms scale to moderate $d \leq 100$; but all global methods require exponential samples in the worst case.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Zeroth-order complexity lower bound]

Nesterov random search achieves $O(d^2/\varepsilon)$ iterations (matching the lower bound up to a factor $d$), making it essentially optimal among dimension-oblivious randomised zeroth-order methods.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Problem Formulation and KKT Conditions}

The constrained problem is:
\begin{equation}
\min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^2}\; f(x) = (x_1 - 1)^2 + 5(x_2 - 2)^2 + \sin(x_1) \qquad \text{subject to } x_1 \geq 0.5.
\end{equation}
The feasible set is $\mathcal{X}_5 = \{x : x_1 \geq 0.5\}$, and the starting point $x_0 = (0.2, 4.0)$ is \emph{infeasible}.
The constraint can be written as $g(x) = 0.5 - x_1 \leq 0$.

The KKT optimality conditions for this problem are:
\begin{align}
\nabla f(x^\star) + \nu \nabla g(x^\star) &= \mathbf{0} \quad &\text{(stationarity)}, \\
g(x^\star) &\leq 0 \quad &\text{(primal feasibility)}, \\
\nu &\geq 0 \quad &\text{(dual feasibility)}, \\
\nu\, g(x^\star) &= 0 \quad &\text{(complementary slackness)}.
\end{align}
For this single inequality constraint, $\nabla g = [-1, 0]^T$.
If the unconstrained minimiser has $x_1^\star > 0.5$ (interior case), then $\nu = 0$ and the constrained minimum equals the unconstrained minimum.
Since the unconstrained minimiser has $x_1^\star \approx 0.582 > 0.5$, the constraint is \emph{inactive} at the solution.
The constrained and unconstrained minima coincide at $x^\star \approx (0.582, 2.000)$ with $f^\star \approx 0.7244$.

\paragraph{Analysis of the active constraint case.}
To illustrate the general case, consider shifting the constraint boundary to $x_1 \geq 0.7 > x_1^\star$.
The unconstrained minimum is now infeasible, and by complementary slackness, the constrained minimum lies on the boundary: $x_1^\star = 0.7$.
The stationarity condition $2(x_1^\star - 1) + \cos(x_1^\star) - \nu = 0$ gives $\nu = 2(0.7 - 1) + \cos(0.7) \approx -0.6 + 0.765 = 0.165 > 0$.
The constrained minimum is $(0.7, 2.0)$ with $f^\star = (0.7-1)^2 + \sin(0.7) \approx 0.09 + 0.644 = 0.734 > 0.7244$ (as expected, the constraint degrades the objective).
This demonstrates the KKT mechanism: when the constraint is active, $\nu^\star > 0$ and the gradient of $f$ at the constrained optimum is non-zero --- it is exactly balanced by $\nu^\star \nabla g(x^\star) = \nu^\star[-1,0]^T$, pointing inward into the feasible set.

\begin{remark}[Slater's condition and strong duality]
The feasible set $\{x : x_1 > 0.5\}$ (strict interior) is non-empty, so Slater's condition holds.
This guarantees \emph{strong duality}: $d^\star = p^\star$ where the dual problem is
\[
  d^\star = \max_{\nu \geq 0}\; q(\nu), \quad q(\nu) = \min_x\; \bigl[f(x) + \nu(0.5 - x_1)\bigr].
\]
At the optimal dual variable $\nu^\star = 0$ (constraint inactive), the dual objective equals $f^\star = 0.7244$.
Strong duality also guarantees that KKT conditions are both necessary \emph{and} sufficient for optimality, providing a complete characterisation of the optimal solution: any $x^\star$ satisfying the four KKT conditions (stationarity, primal feasibility, dual feasibility, complementary slackness) is globally optimal.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Worked dual problem.}
To illustrate strong duality concretely, we compute $q(\nu)$ for the active constraint case ($x_1 \geq 0.7$ boundary).
The Lagrangian is $\mathcal{L}(x, \nu) = (x_1-1)^2 + 5(x_2-2)^2 + \sin(x_1) + \nu(0.5 - x_1)$.
Minimising over $x_2$ gives $x_2^\star(\nu) = 2$ (unconstrained minimiser for the $x_2$ component).
Minimising over $x_1$: $2(x_1 - 1) + \cos(x_1) - \nu = 0$, a transcendental equation.
For $\nu = 0.165$ (the KKT multiplier computed above), the minimiser is $x_1^\star(0.165) \approx 0.7$, and
\[
  q(0.165) = (0.7-1)^2 + 5(2-2)^2 + \sin(0.7) + 0.165(0.5 - 0.7) \approx 0.09 + 0.644 - 0.033 = 0.701.
\]
The dual function $q(\nu)$ is concave (as always for Lagrangian duals), and $\max_\nu q(\nu) = 0.701 = p^\star$ (within rounding), confirming strong duality: zero duality gap at the KKT point.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/kkt_dual_function.pdf}
\caption{Q5: Lagrangian duality analysis for the active constraint case ($x_1 \geq 0.7$). \subsection{Algorithm Formulations}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Projected Gradient Descent}
\label{alg:pgd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, projection $\Pi_{\mathcal{X}_5}$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \nabla f(x_k)$ \Comment{Unconstrained gradient step}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets \Pi_{\mathcal{X}_5}(z_{k+1})$ \Comment{Project: $x_1 \leftarrow \max(0.5,\, x_1)$, $x_2$ unchanged}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

For the half-space constraint $x_1 \geq 0.5$, the projection is the component-wise clip: $\Pi_{\mathcal{X}_5}(z) = (\max(0.5, z_1), z_2)^T$.
This is $O(1)$ per iteration.

\begin{theorem}[Projected gradient convergence]
For a convex, $L$-smooth objective with step size $\alpha = 1/L$, projected gradient descent satisfies:
\[
  f(x_k) - f^\star \leq \frac{\|x_0 - x^\star\|^2}{2\alpha k} = \frac{L\|x_0 - x^\star\|^2}{2k} = O(1/k).
\]
For a $\mu$-strongly convex objective, the convergence is linear:
$\|x_{k+1} - x^\star\|^2 \leq (1 - \mu/L)\|x_k - x^\star\|^2$,
identical to the unconstrained rate, because the projection step only helps.
\end{theorem}

\noindent
In our Q5 problem, the unconstrained minimiser has $x_1^\star \approx 0.582 > 0.5$, so the constraint is inactive and projected GD converges at the same rate as unconstrained GD after the initial projection step.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Penalty Method}
\label{alg:penalty}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, penalty weight $\lambda > 0$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $F_{\lambda}(x) \gets f(x) + \lambda \max(0,\, 0.5 - x_1)$ \Comment{Penalised objective}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \nabla F_{\lambda}(x_k)$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The penalty gradient is $\nabla F_\lambda(x) = \nabla f(x) - \lambda e_1 \cdot \mathbf{1}[x_1 < 0.5]$, where $e_1 = [1,0]^T$.
As $\lambda \to \infty$, the penalty method's minimiser converges to the constrained minimiser.
For finite $\lambda$, the penalty allows residual constraint violation $O(1/\lambda)$.

\begin{remark}[Penalty method bias]
The minimiser of $F_\lambda(x) = f(x) + \lambda \max(0, g(x))$ satisfies the perturbed stationarity condition $\nabla f(x_\lambda) = \lambda\, (-e_1) \cdot \mathbf{1}[x_1 < 0.5]$.
Comparing with the KKT stationarity $\nabla f(x^\star) + \nu^\star(-e_1) = 0$, we see that the penalty parameter $\lambda$ plays the role of the KKT multiplier $\nu$.
The residual constraint violation is $0.5 - x_{\lambda,1} \approx [\nabla f(x^\star)]_1 / \lambda = \nu^\star / \lambda$, explaining the $O(1/\lambda)$ violation.
For our problem with constraint inactive ($\nu^\star = 0$), the penalty method works well for any finite $\lambda$ since there is no bias at the optimum.
\end{remark}

Settings: Projected GD ($\alpha = 0.08$); Penalty with $\lambda \in \{0.15, 1.8, 4.5\}$ ($\alpha = 0.05$ for $\lambda \leq 1.8$; $\alpha = 0.03$ for $\lambda = 4.5$ to compensate for the stiffer gradient); Unconstrained GD ($\alpha = 0.07$). All run for 100 iterations.

\textbf{Projected GD} enforces feasibility as a \emph{hard constraint} at every iteration: no iterate is ever infeasible.
This is essential in applications where infeasible parameters could be physically or semantically invalid (e.g., negative probabilities, out-of-range actuator commands).
The cost is a projection oracle, which is trivial for simple constraints (half-spaces, boxes) but can be expensive or intractable for complex constraint sets (semidefinite constraints, combinatorial sets).

\begin{remark}[Mirror descent as generalisation]
Projected gradient descent can be viewed as a special case of \emph{mirror descent} (Nemirovski \& Yudin 1983) with the Euclidean Bregman divergence.
For non-Euclidean constraints such as the probability simplex $\Delta^d$, replacing the Euclidean projection with the negative-entropy Bregman divergence yields the \emph{exponentiated gradient} update:
\[
  x_{k+1}^{(i)} = x_k^{(i)} \exp(-\alpha g_k^{(i)}) \Big/ \sum_j x_k^{(j)} \exp(-\alpha g_k^{(j)}).
\]
This stays in $\Delta^d$ automatically and achieves the $O(\sqrt{\log d / T})$ regret bound of Adagrad on sparse problems---better than the $O(\sqrt{d/T})$ rate of Euclidean projected gradient descent.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Proximal gradient for composite objectives]
When the objective has the composite form $f(x) + \psi(x)$ where $f$ is smooth (gradients available) and $\psi$ is convex but possibly non-smooth (e.g., $\ell_1$ regularisation $\psi(x) = \lambda\|x\|_1$ or an indicator $\psi = \mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{X}}$), the \emph{proximal gradient method} replaces projection by the \emph{proximal operator}:
\[
  x_{k+1} = \mathrm{prox}_{\alpha\psi}(x_k - \alpha\nabla f(x_k)),
  \quad \text{where} \quad
  \mathrm{prox}_{\alpha\psi}(v) = \arg\min_x \Bigl[\psi(x) + \tfrac{1}{2\alpha}\|x - v\|^2\Bigr].
\]
For the indicator $\psi = \mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{X}}$, this reduces to projection: $\mathrm{prox}_{\alpha\psi}(v) = \Pi_\mathcal{X}(v)$.
For $\ell_1$: $\mathrm{prox}_{\alpha\lambda\|\cdot\|_1}(v)_i = \text{sign}(v_i)\max(|v_i| - \alpha\lambda, 0)$ (soft-thresholding).
The proximal gradient method (ISTA) achieves $O(1/k)$ convergence; its accelerated version (FISTA, Beck \& Teboulle 2009) achieves $O(1/k^2)$, matching Nesterov's optimal rate for smooth objectives while handling non-smooth composite structure.
For our Q5 problem with $\psi = \mathbf{1}_\mathcal{X}$, proximal gradient reduces to projected GD, confirming that projected GD is already optimal for box constraints with smooth $f$.
\end{remark}

\textbf{Penalty methods} require only gradient evaluation of the modified objective, with no projection step.
The parameter $\lambda$ trades feasibility enforcement against objective optimisation: too small gives infeasible solutions; too large stiffens the objective and requires smaller step sizes.
In practice, the \emph{augmented Lagrangian method} (ALM, also known as the method of multipliers) extends penalty methods by maintaining a dual variable $\nu$ that approximates the KKT multiplier.
At each outer iteration the ALM solves:
\begin{equation}
x^{k+1} \gets \arg\min_{x}\; \mathcal{L}_\rho(x, \nu^k) = f(x) + \nu^k g(x) + \frac{\rho}{2}\max(0, g(x))^2,
\end{equation}
then updates the multiplier: $\nu^{k+1} \leftarrow \max\!\left(0,\, \nu^k + \rho\, g(x^{k+1})\right)$.
As $\nu^k \to \nu^\star$ (the true KKT multiplier), the penalty term $\rho\, g(x)^2/2$ vanishes \emph{at the constrained optimum}, so ALM achieves \emph{exact feasibility} at convergence for any finite $\rho > 0$---unlike pure penalty methods that require $\rho \to \infty$.

\begin{remark}[ALM vs ADMM]
ADMM (alternating direction method of multipliers) is a distributed variant of ALM for objectives of the form $f(x) + g(z)$ subject to $Ax + Bz = c$.
The $x$ and $z$ subproblems are solved alternately (hence ``alternating direction''), making ADMM suitable for distributed computation and separable objectives.
ADMM is widely used in large-scale regularised regression (LASSO, elastic net), distributed machine learning, and network flow problems.
\end{remark}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{ADMM (Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers)}
\label{alg:admm}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Penalty $\rho > 0$, initial $(x^0, z^0, \nu^0)$; problem: $\min_{x,z}\, f(x) + g(z)$ s.t.\ $Ax + Bz = c$
\For{$k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$}
    \State $x^{k+1} \gets \arg\min_x \Bigl[ f(x) + \frac{\rho}{2}\|Ax + Bz^k - c + \nu^k/\rho\|^2 \Bigr]$ \Comment{$x$-update}
    \State $z^{k+1} \gets \arg\min_z \Bigl[ g(z) + \frac{\rho}{2}\|Ax^{k+1} + Bz - c + \nu^k/\rho\|^2 \Bigr]$ \Comment{$z$-update}
    \State $\nu^{k+1} \gets \nu^k + \rho\,(Ax^{k+1} + Bz^{k+1} - c)$ \Comment{dual update}
\EndFor
\State \textbf{Stopping criterion:} $\|Ax^{k+1} + Bz^{k+1} - c\| \leq \varepsilon_{\rm prim}$ and $\|\rho A^T B(z^{k+1} - z^k)\| \leq \varepsilon_{\rm dual}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\noindent Applied to Q5 ($\min (x_1-1)^2 + (x_2-5)^2$ s.t.\ $x_1 \geq 0.5$), we introduce a splitting variable $z = x_1$ with indicator constraint $z \geq 0.5$: the $x$-update has a closed form (solve $2\times2$ linear system), and the $z$-update is a clipping $z \gets \max(x_1 + \nu/\rho,\ 0.5)$---both cheap.

\subsection{Feasible Set and Linear Programme}

The feasible set is $\mathcal{X} = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^2 : 0.5 \leq x_1 \leq 5,\ -5 \leq x_2 \leq 10\}$, a compact box polytope.

\textbf{Linear Programme:} For the linear objective $f(x) = a^T x$ with $a = [1, 2]^T$, the minimum over $\mathcal{X}$ is obtained by minimising each coordinate independently (since the feasible set is a Cartesian product).
Since both components of $a$ are positive, the minimum is at the lower bounds:
\begin{equation}
x^{\star} = \underset{x \in \mathcal{X}}{\arg\min}\; (x_1 + 2x_2) = (0.5,\, -5), \qquad f^{\star} = 0.5 + 2(-5) = -9.5.
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Fundamental theorem of linear programming]
The minimum of a linear objective over a bounded closed convex polytope is always attained at a vertex of the polytope. For the box $\mathcal{X}$, the vertices are the four corners; the minimising vertex is selected by the sign of the gradient.
\end{proposition}

The minimum is attained at the vertex $(0.5, -5)$, confirming the fundamental theorem of LP. \subsection{Frank--Wolfe Algorithm (Conditional Gradient)}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Frank--Wolfe (Conditional Gradient)}
\label{alg:fw}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0 \in \mathcal{X}$, mixing parameter $\beta \in (0,1)$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_k \gets \arg\min_{x \in \mathcal{X}}\, \nabla f(x_k)^T x$ \Comment{Linear minimisation oracle (LMO)}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets \beta\, x_k + (1 - \beta)\, z_k$ \Comment{Convex combination: stays in $\mathcal{X}$}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The Frank--Wolfe algorithm (also called the conditional gradient method) replaces the projection step of projected gradient descent with a \emph{linear minimisation oracle} (LMO): solving a linear programme over $\mathcal{X}$.
For box constraints, the LMO decomposes per coordinate:
\begin{equation}
(z_k)_i = \begin{cases} l_i & \text{if } (\nabla f(x_k))_i > 0 \\ u_i & \text{if } (\nabla f(x_k))_i \leq 0 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
requiring only $O(d)$ operations.
Crucially, since $z_k \in \mathcal{X}$ and $x_k \in \mathcal{X}$, the convex combination $\beta x_k + (1-\beta)z_k \in \mathcal{X}$ --- \emph{no projection is needed at any step}.

The Frank--Wolfe gap $G_k = \nabla f(x_k)^T(x_k - z_k)$ measures the quality of the current iterate; $G_k = 0$ at optimality.

\begin{theorem}[Frank--Wolfe convergence, \emph{Frank \& Wolfe 1956}]
For a convex, $L$-smooth objective with the standard step size $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$:
\begin{equation}
f(x_k) - f^\star \leq \frac{2L D^2}{k + 2}, \qquad G_k \leq \frac{4L D^2}{k+2},
\end{equation}
where $D = \max_{x, y \in \mathcal{X}} \|x - y\|$ is the diameter of $\mathcal{X}$ and $G_k = \nabla f(x_k)^T(x_k - z_k)$.
For our box $\mathcal{X}$: $D^2 = (5 - 0.5)^2 + (10 + 5)^2 = 20.25 + 225 = 245.25$, so after $k = 100$ iterations with $L_{\rm interior} \approx 12$ (eigenvalue of $\text{diag}(2,10)$), the bound gives $f(x_{100}) - f^\star \leq 2 \times 12 \times 245.25 / 102 \approx 57.7$ --- a loose bound, as the empirical gap at $k=100$ is already $< 0.05$, illustrating that the diameter $D$ bound is conservative for interior optima.
\end{theorem}

\noindent
This $O(1/k)$ rate is achievable but generally cannot be improved for Frank--Wolfe without additional assumptions.

\textbf{Interior optimum:} Frank--Wolfe converges but exhibits characteristic ``zig-zag'' vertex jumping.
The LMO $z_k$ jumps between vertices of $\mathcal{X}$ because small changes in the gradient direction flip which vertex minimises the linear objective.
The iterate $x_k = \beta x_{k-1} + (1-\beta)z_k$ is pulled towards alternating vertices, creating oscillation.
The fixed step size $\beta$ does not decay, preventing convergence to machine precision; the standard step $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$ (mixing parameter $1 - \gamma_k$ towards $z_k$) would ensure $O(1/k)$ convergence.

\textbf{Boundary optimum:} Frank--Wolfe is particularly well-suited when the optimum lies on a constraint boundary.
When $x_1^\star = 0.5$ (lower bound), the LMO consistently selects the vertex with $x_1 = 0.5$, naturally driving the iterate towards the boundary.
Projected gradient descent would also work but requires an explicit projection step; Frank--Wolfe achieves the same effect through the LMO structure of the constraint set, which is often more computationally tractable for high-dimensional structured constraints (e.g., nuclear norm ball, polytopes with combinatorial structure).

\textbf{Key advantage of Frank--Wolfe:} Iterates $x_k$ are always convex combinations of vertices of $\mathcal{X}$, guaranteeing that $x_k \in \mathcal{X}$ without any projection.
This is especially valuable for structured constraint sets where projection is expensive but the LMO (solving a linear programme) is cheap.

\begin{remark}[Frank--Wolfe for structured constraints]
The power of Frank--Wolfe lies in its flexibility with respect to the constraint set.
Three classical examples:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Nuclear norm ball} $\{\,X : \|X\|_* \leq \tau\}$: The LMO requires computing the leading singular vectors of a matrix (top-$1$ SVD, cost $O(mn)$), whereas projection onto the nuclear norm ball requires a full SVD ($O(mn\min(m,n))$). Frank--Wolfe is therefore preferred for matrix completion and low-rank learning.
  \item \textbf{Simplex} $\Delta^d = \{p \geq 0 : \mathbf{1}^Tp = 1\}$: The LMO picks the coordinate $i^\star = \arg\min_i (\nabla f)_i$ and returns $e_{i^\star}$, costing $O(d)$. Projection onto the simplex costs $O(d\log d)$.
  \item \textbf{Birkhoff polytope} (doubly stochastic matrices): The LMO reduces to an assignment problem (solvable in $O(d^3)$ by the Hungarian algorithm), while projection has no closed form.
\end{enumerate}
In all these cases, Frank--Wolfe produces sparse iterates---convex combinations of few vertices---which has practical benefits for memory and interpretability.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Limitations and extensions]
The $O(1/k)$ rate of Frank--Wolfe is \emph{slower} than projected gradient descent's $O(1/k)$ in terms of constant factors, and it cannot match the $O(1/k^2)$ rate of projected Nesterov acceleration.
For strongly convex objectives, FW converges at the linear rate $O(\rho^k)$ only with the ``away-step'' modification (Lacoste-Julien \& Jaggi, 2015), which also solves a linear programme over $\mathcal{X}$ to identify the active constraint vertex to move away from.
The standard FW algorithm has only a sublinear $O(1/k)$ rate even under strong convexity.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Away-Step Frank--Wolfe for Linear Convergence}

Under strong convexity, the standard FW algorithm cannot achieve linear convergence because vertex-jumping repeatedly undoes progress.
The \emph{away-step} variant of Lacoste-Julien \& Jaggi (2015) fixes this by also considering a step \emph{away from} the active vertex that contributes most to the gradient, reducing its weight in the iterate representation:

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Away-Step Frank--Wolfe (Lacoste-Julien \& Jaggi 2015)}
\label{alg:fw_away}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $x_0 \in \mathcal{X}$ (vertex of $\mathcal{X}$), active set $S_0 = \{x_0\}$, weights $\lambda_0 = \{1\}$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_k^{\rm FW} \gets \arg\min_{v \in \mathcal{X}} \nabla f(x_k)^T v$ \Comment{FW vertex (LMO)}
    \State $v_k^{\rm A} \gets \arg\max_{v \in S_k} \nabla f(x_k)^T v$ \Comment{Away vertex (max over active set)}
    \State $G_k^{\rm FW} \gets \nabla f(x_k)^T(x_k - z_k^{\rm FW})$; \quad $G_k^{\rm A} \gets \nabla f(x_k)^T(v_k^{\rm A} - x_k)$ \Comment{Gaps}
    \If{$G_k^{\rm FW} \geq G_k^{\rm A}$}
        \State $d_k \gets z_k^{\rm FW} - x_k$; \quad $\gamma_{\max} \gets 1$ \Comment{FW step}
    \Else
        \State $d_k \gets x_k - v_k^{\rm A}$; \quad $\gamma_{\max} \gets \lambda_k(v_k^{\rm A}) / (1 - \lambda_k(v_k^{\rm A}))$ \Comment{Away step}
    \EndIf
    \State $\gamma_k \gets \arg\min_{0 \leq \gamma \leq \gamma_{\max}} f(x_k + \gamma d_k)$ \Comment{Exact or Wolfe line search}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k + \gamma_k\, d_k$; update active set $S_{k+1}$ and weights $\lambda_{k+1}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\noindent The away step drops the weight of the vertex $v_k^A$ most aligned with the gradient, reducing the component of $x_k$ pointing in the gradient direction.
When the FW gap exceeds the away gap, the standard FW step is preferred; otherwise, the away step removes a problematic vertex from the active representation.
Under strong convexity, this eliminates the sublinear oscillation of standard FW and achieves:
\begin{equation}
  f(x_k) - f^\star \leq \left(1 - \frac{\mu}{L}\right)^k (f(x_0) - f^\star) \qquad \text{(linear convergence)}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Key Findings and Recommendations}

This study has systematically compared six families of optimisation algorithms across three benchmark functions of increasing difficulty.
The key conclusions are:

\textbf{Adaptive step-size methods (Q1):}
The Polyak step size achieves optimal convergence when $f^\star$ is exactly known but diverges catastrophically under misspecification.
RMSprop's exponential forgetting addresses Adagrad's vanishing step problem.
Heavy Ball provides a strong practical alternative with well-tuned parameters.

\textbf{Momentum and stochastic methods (Q2):}
Nesterov acceleration achieves the theoretically optimal $O(1/k^2)$ first-order rate and dominates all other first-order methods on the Rosenbrock benchmark.
Adam's per-coordinate adaptive scaling is effective on anisotropic problems but less so on highly nonlinear objectives.
Mini-batch SGD demonstrates the bias--variance tradeoff between batch size and per-epoch convergence.

\textbf{Newton's method (Q3):}
Second-order methods dramatically outperform first-order methods when the Hessian is available.
On quadratic objectives, Newton achieves single-step convergence.
The exponential cost ($O(d^3)$ per iteration) limits Newton to small $d$; quasi-Newton methods (e.g., L-BFGS) bridge this gap in practice.

\textbf{Derivative-free optimisation (Q4):}
Forward finite differences with small $\delta$ closely match exact gradient descent at $d+1$ function evaluations per step.
Large $\delta$ causes systematic error that dominates gradient direction quality.
Nesterov random search requires only 2 evaluations per step, making it attractive for high-dimensional black-box optimisation.
Nelder--Mead navigates the Rosenbrock valley effectively without gradients.

\textbf{Constrained optimisation (Q5):}
Projected gradient descent enforces feasibility as a hard constraint at every iterate.
Penalty methods provide a simpler alternative but require $\lambda \to \infty$ for exact feasibility, necessitating careful step size scheduling.
When the unconstrained minimum is feasible (as in this problem), the constrained solution coincides with the unconstrained one, making the constraint inactive at optimality.

\textbf{Frank--Wolfe algorithm (Q6):}
Frank--Wolfe naturally enforces constraints through convex combinations without projection.
It converges at $O(1/k)$ for smooth convex objectives and is particularly effective for structured constraint sets where the linear minimisation oracle is cheap.
Fixed mixing parameters $\beta$ prevent full convergence; the standard diminishing schedule $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$ (where $x_{k+1} = (1-\gamma_k)x_k + \gamma_k z_k$) provably achieves $O(1/k)$ convergence and outperforms any fixed $\beta$ in the long run, as confirmed empirically in Figure~\ref{fig:fw_step_comparison}.

\subsection{Connection to Machine Learning Practice}

The algorithms studied in this report directly underpin modern machine learning:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Linear regression (Benchmark A)} is the canonical ML regression problem. The exact quadratic structure means Newton's method (equivalent to the normal equations $\theta^\star = H^{-1}b$) solves it in one step, while stochastic methods are preferred for $m \gg d$ due to their $O(b)$ per-step cost vs $O(m)$ for full-batch methods.
\item \textbf{Non-quadratic loss (Benchmark B)} represents shallow non-convex neural networks. Adam's combination of adaptive per-coordinate scaling and momentum is the practical default for training neural networks, explaining its ubiquity in deep learning despite its theoretical convergence issues (Reddi et al.~2018).
\item \textbf{Rosenbrock (Benchmark C)} encapsulates the ill-conditioning challenge in training deep networks: the loss landscape often has elongated valleys analogous to the Rosenbrock banana (e.g., loss saddles, near-flat directions), motivating adaptive methods that detect and exploit local curvature.
\item \textbf{Constrained optimisation (Q5/Q6)} arises in fairness-constrained learning, safe reinforcement learning, and federated learning, where the optimum must lie in a feasible set. Projected gradient descent and Frank--Wolfe are directly applicable to resource-allocation problems under budget constraints.
\item \textbf{Variance reduction (SVRG, SAGA)} is the key theoretical development enabling linear convergence of stochastic methods for finite-sum problems, underlying modern ML frameworks' convergence guarantees.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Algorithm Selection Guide}

Based on the theoretical analysis and empirical results of this study, the following guidelines summarise when to prefer each algorithm family:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Small-scale, well-conditioned, quadratic:} Use Newton's method. Single-step convergence on quadratics; quadratic convergence locally.

\item \textbf{Small-to-medium scale, convex, gradient available:} Nesterov acceleration ($O(1/k^2)$ optimal rate). If strongly convex with known $f^\star$: Polyak step. For anisotropic landscapes: Heavy Ball or Adam as practical alternatives.

\item \textbf{Large-scale, sum-of-functions structure:} Mini-batch SGD or SVRG. Prefer SVRG when high accuracy is needed (linear convergence vs SGD's noise floor); prefer SGD when only moderate accuracy is needed or the function count $n$ is very large (snapshot cost dominates).

\item \textbf{Gradient unavailable or expensive:} Forward finite differences ($d+1$ FE, most accurate, scales with $d$) or Nesterov random search (2 FE, dimension-independent cost, high variance). Nelder--Mead for low $d$ with smooth objectives.

\item \textbf{Simple convex constraints (half-space, box):} Projected gradient descent---exact feasibility at every iterate, same convergence rate as unconstrained GD.

\item \textbf{Complex structured constraints (nuclear norm, simplex, Birkhoff):} Frank--Wolfe---avoids projection entirely; LMO is often efficient even when projection is not.

\item \textbf{Difficult non-convex, no analytic gradient:} Adam (deep learning default), Nelder--Mead (low $d$), or evolutionary/derivative-free methods.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations and Extensions}

The experiments in this report are conducted on $d = 2$ dimensional problems with small datasets ($m = 1000$), enabling exact Hessian computation and dense gradient evaluations.
Several important practical considerations would alter the conclusions at larger scale:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Scaling to large $d$:} Newton's method is infeasible for $d \geq 10^4$. L-BFGS ($m = 10$--$20$) is the practical replacement; for deep learning ($d \sim 10^7$--$10^{12}$), stochastic first-order methods (Adam, SGD with momentum) dominate due to the prohibitive Hessian-vector product cost.
\item \textbf{Non-convex objectives:} All convergence theorems in this report assume convexity or strong convexity. For non-convex objectives (e.g., deeper neural networks), methods may converge to saddle points or local minima. Second-order methods require additional safeguards (e.g., negative curvature detection) to escape saddles.
\item \textbf{Step-size selection in practice:} The hyperparameters used here ($\alpha$, $\beta$, etc.) were manually tuned for the specific benchmarks. In practice, automatic step-size selection via backtracking line search (Armijo condition: $f(x_k - \alpha g_k) \leq f(x_k) - c\alpha\|g_k\|^2$) or cosine annealing schedules would be required.
\item \textbf{Constrained problems at scale:} For large-scale constrained problems, the projection step in projected GD can be expensive (e.g., projection onto the nuclear norm ball requires SVD). Frank--Wolfe avoids this but converges at $O(1/k)$ vs potential $O(1/k^2)$ for accelerated projected methods.
\item \textbf{Distributed and federated settings:} Mini-batch SGD and SVRG extend naturally to distributed settings (data parallelism); Frank--Wolfe's LMO step can also be parallelised over constraint vertices. Projected GD requires synchronous projection, which is harder to distribute.
\end{enumerate}

def gradient_descent(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        x = x - alpha * grad_fn(x)
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Polyak Step Size}]
def polyak_step(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, f_star, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        alpha_k = (loss_fn(x) - f_star) / (np.dot(g, g) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(alpha_k)
        x = x - alpha_k * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy()); f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Adagrad}]
def adagrad(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha0, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    G = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        G += g**2
        eff_alpha = alpha0 / (np.sqrt(G) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(np.mean(eff_alpha))
        x = x - eff_alpha * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy()); f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={RMSprop}]
def rmsprop(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha0, beta, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    v = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        v = beta * v + (1 - beta) * g**2
        eff_alpha = alpha0 / (np.sqrt(v) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(np.mean(eff_alpha))
        x = x - eff_alpha * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy()); f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Heavy Ball}]
def heavy_ball(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    z = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        z = beta * z + alpha * g
        x = x - z
        x_hist.append(x.copy()); f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Nesterov Accelerated Gradient}]
def nesterov_momentum(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta_max, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    z = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for k in range(1, n_iters + 1):
        beta_k = min((k - 1) / (k + 2), beta_max)
        lookahead = x + beta_k * z
        g = grad_fn(lookahead)
        z = beta_k * z - alpha * g
        x = x + z
        x_hist.append(x.copy()); f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Adam Optimiser}]
def adam_optimiser(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta1, beta2, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    m_vec, v_vec = np.zeros_like(x), np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for t in range(1, n_iters + 1):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        m_vec = beta1 * m_vec + (1 - beta1) * g
        v_vec = beta2 * v_vec + (1 - beta2) * g**2
        m_hat = m_vec / (1 - beta1**t)
        v_hat = v_vec / (1 - beta2**t)
        x = x - alpha * m_hat / (np.sqrt(v_hat) + eps)
        x_hist.append(x.copy()); f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Newton's Method with Regularised Hessian}]
def newtons_method(grad_fn, hess_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, n_iters, damping=1e-8):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, update_norms = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        H = hess_fn(x) if callable(hess_fn) else hess_fn
        H_reg = H + damping * np.eye(len(x))
        try:
            p = np.linalg.solve(H_reg, g)
        except np.linalg.LinAlgError:
            p = g
        update_norms.append(np.linalg.norm(alpha * p))
        x = x - alpha * p
        x_hist.append(x.copy()); f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(update_norms)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Frank--Wolfe (Conditional Gradient)}]
def frank_wolfe(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, bounds, beta, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, z_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        z = np.array([bounds[i][0] if g[i] > 0 else bounds[i][1]
                       for i in range(len(g))])
        z_hist.append(z.copy())
        x = beta * x + (1 - beta) * z
        x_hist.append(x.copy()); f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(z_hist)
\end{lstlisting}
