\begin{abstract}
This report presents a systematic comparative study of first-order, second-order, and derivative-free optimisation algorithms applied to three canonical benchmark problems: a linear regression quadratic loss (Benchmark A), a mildly non-quadratic toy neural-network loss with sinusoidal perturbation (Benchmark B), and the ill-conditioned Rosenbrock function (Benchmark C). Six families of methods are investigated: (1) \emph{adaptive step-size} methods (Polyak, Adagrad, RMSprop, Heavy Ball); (2) \emph{momentum and stochastic} methods (Nesterov acceleration, Adam, mini-batch SGD); (3) \emph{Newton's method} with local second-order approximations; (4) \emph{derivative-free} methods (forward finite differences, Nesterov random search, Nelder--Mead simplex, grid search); (5) \emph{constrained optimisation} via projected gradient descent and penalty methods; and (6) \emph{linear programmes and the Frank--Wolfe conditional-gradient algorithm}. Each family is evaluated in terms of convergence speed, trajectory behaviour, algorithmic stability, and computational complexity. The results confirm that curvature information (Newton) and adaptive scaling (Adam, RMSprop) dramatically accelerate convergence on ill-conditioned problems, that the Polyak step requires exact knowledge of the minimum value, that Nesterov acceleration achieves theoretical $O(1/k^2)$ improvement over GD's $O(1/k)$ on Rosenbrock, and that Frank--Wolfe handles box-constrained problems without a projection oracle at the cost of a characteristic zig-zag convergence pattern.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================

Optimisation lies at the heart of machine learning and data analysis: training a neural network, fitting a regression model, or selecting a policy all reduce to minimising an objective function over a parameter space. The choice of optimisation algorithm has profound consequences for both the quality of the solution and the computational cost of obtaining it.

This report investigates six families of optimisation algorithms, systematically applied across three benchmarks that represent increasing levels of difficulty. Benchmark A (linear regression) is a strongly convex quadratic whose condition number depends on the data covariance $H = \frac{1}{m}X^TX$. Benchmark B introduces mild non-quadratic behaviour via a sinusoidal term, creating a landscape that is close to convex but not exactly so. Benchmark C, the Rosenbrock function, is a classic stress test: a non-convex function with a narrow curved valley whose condition number near the minimum is approximately $\kappa \approx 2000$, exposing the limitations of methods that cannot exploit second-order curvature.

The experiments are designed to answer several key questions:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item How sensitive is the Polyak step size to the accuracy of $f^\star$?
  \item Can momentum methods (Nesterov, Adam) overcome the ill-conditioning of Rosenbrock?
  \item How many Newton steps are needed to match 80 gradient steps, and what does this reveal about per-iteration cost?
  \item What is the accuracy ceiling of derivative-free methods compared to gradient-based methods?
  \item How does the penalty parameter $\lambda$ trade off feasibility and objective quality in penalty methods?
  \item When does Frank--Wolfe converge to the interior optimum vs the boundary optimum, and what is the role of the mixing parameter $\beta$?
\end{itemize}

All experiments use a fixed random seed (\texttt{np.random.seed(42)}) for reproducibility.

\subsection{Benchmark A: Linear Regression Quadratic Loss}
A synthetic dataset with $m = 1000$ samples and $d = 2$ features is generated:
\begin{equation}
  \theta^{\star} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
  x^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_2), \quad
  y^{(i)} = (\theta^{\star})^T x^{(i)} + \varepsilon^{(i)}, \quad
  \varepsilon^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1).
\end{equation}
The mean-squared-error loss is:
\begin{equation}
  J(\theta) = \frac{1}{2m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \bigl(\theta^T x^{(i)} - y^{(i)}\bigr)^2
            = \frac{1}{2m}\|X\theta - y\|^2.
\end{equation}
This is a \emph{strongly convex quadratic} with constant Hessian $H = \frac{1}{m}X^T X \succ 0$ and gradient $\nabla J(\theta) = \frac{1}{m}X^T(X\theta - y)$. The strong convexity constant is $\mu = \lambda_{\min}(H) > 0$ and the Lipschitz constant of the gradient is $L = \lambda_{\max}(H)$, giving condition number $\kappa = L/\mu$. Because the labels contain observation noise, the minimum loss is $J^{\star} = \frac{1}{2m}\|y - X\theta_{\mathrm{OLS}}\|^2 \approx 0.4826 > 0$, not zero. Starting point: $\theta_0 = [0,\, 0]^T$.

\subsection{Benchmark B: Toy Neural Network Loss}
\begin{equation}
  f(x_1, x_2) = (x_1 - 1)^2 + 5(x_2 - 2)^2 + \sin(x_1).
\end{equation}
The gradient is $\nabla f = [2(x_1 - 1) + \cos(x_1),\ 10(x_2 - 2)]^T$ and the Hessian is $H(x) = \mathrm{diag}(2 - \sin(x_1),\ 10)$. The Hessian eigenvalues vary in the range $[1,\, 3] \times \{10\}$ as $x_1$ changes, giving a mild anisotropy with condition number $\kappa \in [10/3, 10]$. The unique minimiser satisfies $x_2^\star = 2$ and $2(x_1^\star - 1) + \cos(x_1^\star) = 0$, yielding $x_1^\star \approx 0.5815$ (solved numerically) and $f^\star \approx 0.7244$. Starting point: $x_0 = [-1,\, 4]^T$.

\subsection{Benchmark C: Rosenbrock Function}
\begin{equation}
  f(x_1, x_2) = (1 - x_1)^2 + 100(x_2 - x_1^2)^2.
\end{equation}
The global minimum is at $(1, 1)$ with $f^\star = 0$. The Hessian at the optimum is:
\begin{equation}
  \nabla^2 f(1,1) = \begin{bmatrix} 802 & -400 \\ -400 & 200 \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
with eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \approx 0.5$ and $\lambda_2 \approx 1001.5$, giving condition number $\kappa \approx 2003$. This extreme ill-conditioning, combined with the narrow curved valley along $x_2 = x_1^2$, makes Rosenbrock one of the canonical challenging optimisation benchmarks. First-order methods that do not adapt to curvature suffer the most on this function. Starting point: $x_0 = [-1,\, 1]^T$.

\subsection{Benchmark D: Constrained Feasible Set}
For constrained problems (Questions 5--6):
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{X} = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^2 : 0.5 \leq x_1 \leq 5,\ -5 \leq x_2 \leq 10\}.
\end{equation}
This is a convex compact polytope (box) in $\mathbb{R}^2$.

\subsection{Algorithm Formulations and Theoretical Background}

Four adaptive step-size methods are compared against a constant step-size gradient descent (GD) baseline. The central motivation is that the optimal fixed step size $\alpha^\star = 2/(L + \mu)$ for strongly convex problems requires knowledge of $L$ and $\mu$, which are often unavailable. Adaptive methods remove this requirement at the cost of additional per-iteration computation.

\begin{definition}[Lipschitz Gradient]
A differentiable function $f$ has an $L$-Lipschitz gradient if $\|\nabla f(x) - \nabla f(y)\| \leq L\|x - y\|$ for all $x, y$. This is equivalent to $f(y) \leq f(x) + \nabla f(x)^T(y-x) + \frac{L}{2}\|y-x\|^2$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[GD Convergence for Strongly Convex Functions]
\label{thm:gd_conv}
If $f$ is $\mu$-strongly convex with $L$-Lipschitz gradient, GD with step size $\alpha \in (0, 2/(L+\mu))$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}
  f(x_k) - f^\star \leq \left(1 - \frac{2\alpha \mu L}{\mu + L}\right)^k (f(x_0) - f^\star).
\end{equation}
The optimal contraction factor $\rho^\star = \frac{\kappa - 1}{\kappa + 1}$ (where $\kappa = L/\mu$) is achieved at $\alpha^\star = 2/(L+\mu)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Polyak Step Size}
\label{alg:polyak}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, optimal value $f^{\star}$, tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $\alpha_k \gets \dfrac{f(x_k) - f^{\star}}{\|g_k\|^2 + \varepsilon}$ \Comment{Step proportional to suboptimality}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha_k\, g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{remark}[Polyak Convergence]
When $f^\star$ is exact and $f$ is convex with $L$-Lipschitz gradient, the Polyak step satisfies $f(x_k) - f^\star \leq \frac{2L\|x_0 - x^\star\|^2}{k}$ \cite{Polyak1987}, an $O(1/k)$ rate without requiring knowledge of $L$ or $\mu$. When $f^\star$ is underestimated (set too low), $\alpha_k = (f(x_k) - f^\star_{\text{incorrect}})/\|g_k\|^2$ inflates the step size as $f(x_k) \to f^\star_{\text{true}} > f^\star_{\text{incorrect}}$, causing divergence.
\end{remark}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Adagrad}
\label{alg:adagrad}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, base learning rate $\alpha_0$, tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$, iterations $N$
\State $G_0 \gets \mathbf{0} \in \mathbb{R}^d$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $G_{k+1} \gets G_k + g_k \odot g_k$ \Comment{Cumulative per-coordinate squared gradient}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \dfrac{\alpha_0}{\sqrt{G_{k+1}} + \varepsilon} \odot g_k$ \Comment{Coordinate-wise scaling}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{remark}[Adagrad Properties]
Adagrad implicitly performs a change of metric: the effective step in direction $i$ is $\alpha_0 / \sqrt{\sum_{j \leq k} g_{j,i}^2}$. Coordinates with consistently large gradients receive smaller steps (automatic regularisation). The monotonically non-increasing step can cause premature stagnation on non-stationary or non-convex problems. For sparse gradients, infrequent large updates in rare coordinates are preserved.
\end{remark}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{RMSprop}
\label{alg:rmsprop}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, learning rate $\alpha_0$, decay $\beta \in (0,1)$, tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$, iterations $N$
\State $v_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $v_{k+1} \gets \beta\, v_k + (1 - \beta)\, g_k \odot g_k$ \Comment{Exponential moving average of $g^2$}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \dfrac{\alpha_0}{\sqrt{v_{k+1}} + \varepsilon} \odot g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{remark}[RMSprop vs Adagrad]
RMSprop addresses Adagrad's diminishing step-size problem by replacing cumulative squared gradients with an exponential moving average (EMA) with forgetting factor $\beta$. The effective window over which past gradients contribute is approximately $1/(1-\beta)$ steps. With $\beta = 0.9$ (used here), roughly the last 10 gradient observations influence the current step. This allows the step size to recover when gradients decrease.
\end{remark}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Polyak Momentum / Heavy Ball}
\label{alg:heavyball}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha > 0$, momentum $\beta \in [0,1)$, iterations $N$
\State $z_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets \beta\, z_k + \alpha\, \nabla f(x_k)$ \Comment{Velocity update (exponential averaging)}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - z_{k+1}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{remark}[Heavy Ball Optimal Convergence]
For $\mu$-strongly convex, $L$-smooth quadratic functions, Heavy Ball with optimal parameters $\alpha^\star = 4/(\sqrt{L}+\sqrt{\mu})^2$ and $\beta^\star = \left(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa}+1}\right)^2$ achieves the optimal linear convergence rate $\rho^\star = \frac{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa}+1}$, compared to GD's $\rho_{\mathrm{GD}} = \frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1}$. For large $\kappa$, $\rho^\star \approx 1 - 2/\sqrt{\kappa}$ versus $\rho_{\mathrm{GD}} \approx 1 - 2/\kappa$, a qualitative speedup. However, this optimality holds \emph{only for quadratics}; on non-quadratic functions Heavy Ball can exhibit oscillations or even divergence.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Comparative Analysis and Discussion}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Convergence speed:} When $f^\star$ is known exactly (Benchmark C), Polyak converges fastest ($f = 0.009$ in 120 iterations). For benchmarks where $f^\star > 0$, Adagrad and Heavy Ball match GD on simple quadratics and significantly outperform GD on the ill-conditioned Rosenbrock.
\item \textbf{Stability vs adaptation:} GD baseline is most stable but relies on manual step-size tuning. Polyak is self-tuning but catastrophically unstable under oracle misspecification. Adagrad is uniformly stable (monotone step decay guarantees convergence for convex problems) but may converge prematurely. Heavy Ball can oscillate near the minimum but generally converges if $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are chosen within the stability region.
\item \textbf{Practical recommendation:} For problems without known $f^\star$, RMSprop or Adagrad are more robust. RMSprop is particularly recommended for non-stationary objectives (such as neural network training) due to its forgetting mechanism.
\item \textbf{Computational cost:} All adaptive methods require $O(d)$ extra storage and $O(d)$ extra arithmetic per iteration compared to vanilla GD. This overhead is negligible for the 2D problems studied here but matters in high-dimensional settings.
\end{itemize}

\section{Question 2: Momentum and Stochastic Methods \hfill [30 marks]}

\subsection{Algorithm Formulations and Theoretical Background}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Nesterov Momentum / Accelerated Gradient Descent}
\label{alg:nesterov}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha > 0$, max momentum $\beta_{\max} \in [0,1)$, iterations $N$
\State $z_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 1, 2, \ldots, N$}
    \State $\beta_k \gets \min\!\left(\frac{k-1}{k+2},\ \beta_{\max}\right)$ \Comment{Adaptive momentum schedule}
    \State $\tilde{x}_k \gets x_k + \beta_k\, z_k$ \Comment{Lookahead (extrapolation) point}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets \beta_k\, z_k - \alpha\, \nabla f(\tilde{x}_k)$ \Comment{Velocity update using lookahead gradient}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k + z_{k+1}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}[Nesterov Acceleration]
\label{thm:nesterov}
For a convex function $f$ with $L$-Lipschitz gradient, Nesterov's accelerated gradient descent with $\alpha = 1/L$ and $\beta_k = (k-1)/(k+2)$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}
  f(x_k) - f^\star \leq \frac{2L\|x_0 - x^\star\|^2}{(k+1)^2},
\end{equation}
i.e., an $O(1/k^2)$ rate versus GD's $O(1/k)$ rate. This is \emph{optimal} for first-order methods in the worst case over smooth convex functions.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}
The key innovation of Nesterov momentum over Heavy Ball is the \emph{lookahead}: the gradient is evaluated at $\tilde{x}_k = x_k + \beta_k z_k$ rather than $x_k$. This ``corrected'' gradient is more informative about the direction of the minimum, enabling the algorithm to correct its trajectory before overshooting. The momentum schedule $\beta_k = (k-1)/(k+2) \to 1$ is adaptive: it starts with no momentum and gradually increases, matching the theoretical sequence that achieves the optimal rate.
\end{remark}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Adam Optimiser}
\label{alg:adam}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, learning rate $\alpha > 0$, moments $\beta_1, \beta_2 \in (0,1)$, tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$
\State $m_0 \gets \mathbf{0},\ v_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$t = 1, 2, \ldots, N$}
    \State $g_t \gets \nabla f(x_t)$
    \State $m_t \gets \beta_1\, m_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_1)\, g_t$ \Comment{First moment: exponential gradient average}
    \State $v_t \gets \beta_2\, v_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_2)\, g_t \odot g_t$ \Comment{Second moment: exponential squared-gradient average}
    \State $\hat{m}_t \gets m_t / (1 - \beta_1^t)$, \quad $\hat{v}_t \gets v_t / (1 - \beta_2^t)$ \Comment{Bias correction}
    \State $x_{t+1} \gets x_t - \alpha\, \hat{m}_t / (\sqrt{\hat{v}_t} + \varepsilon)$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{remark}[Adam as Nesterov + RMSprop]
Adam combines first-moment momentum (as in Heavy Ball) with per-coordinate adaptive scaling (as in RMSprop). The bias correction terms $(1 - \beta_1^t)^{-1}$ and $(1 - \beta_2^t)^{-1}$ ensure that early iterates, where the EMA estimates are biased towards zero, receive appropriately scaled steps. The effective step size per coordinate is $\alpha \hat{m}_{t,i} / (\sqrt{\hat{v}_{t,i}} + \varepsilon)$; when gradients are consistent in sign, $\hat{m}_t \approx \hat{v}_t^{1/2}$ and the step approximates $\pm\alpha$, making Adam approximately invariant to gradient magnitude.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Mini-Batch Stochastic Gradient Descent}

Mini-batch SGD is evaluated on Benchmark A with learning rate $\alpha = 0.06$, batch sizes $b \in \{5, 40\}$, 50 epochs, and data shuffled at each epoch. The gradient estimator for batch $\mathcal{B}$ of size $b$ is:
\begin{equation}
  g_{\mathcal{B}}(\theta) = \frac{1}{b} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{B}} x^{(i)}\bigl(\theta^T x^{(i)} - y^{(i)}\bigr) = \frac{1}{b} X_{\mathcal{B}}^T(X_{\mathcal{B}}\theta - y_{\mathcal{B}}).
\end{equation}
This is an unbiased estimate of the full gradient: $\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{B}}[g_{\mathcal{B}}(\theta)] = \nabla J(\theta)$. Its variance is $O(1/b)$: larger batches yield more accurate gradient estimates.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Mini-Batch SGD}
\label{alg:sgd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Data $(X, y)$, initial $\theta_0$, learning rate $\alpha$, batch size $b$, epochs $E$
\For{$e = 1, 2, \ldots, E$}
    \State Randomly permute the $m$ sample indices into $\lfloor m/b \rfloor$ mini-batches
    \For{each mini-batch $\mathcal{B}$ of size $b$}
        \State $\theta \gets \theta - \alpha\, g_{\mathcal{B}}(\theta)$ \Comment{Stochastic gradient step}
    \EndFor
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Local Approximations of $g(x) = x^4$}

For a smooth function $g: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, the Taylor expansion around $x_0$ gives:
\begin{align}
  \text{First-order:} \quad & \hat{g}_1(x) = g(x_0) + g'(x_0)(x - x_0), \\
  \text{Second-order:} \quad & \hat{g}_2(x) = g(x_0) + g'(x_0)(x - x_0) + \tfrac{1}{2}g''(x_0)(x - x_0)^2.
\end{align}

For $g(x) = x^4$ at $x_0 = 0.25$: $g(0.25) = 0.0039$, $g'(0.25) = 4(0.25)^3 = 0.0625$, $g''(0.25) = 12(0.25)^2 = 0.75$:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{g}_1(x) = 0.0039 + 0.0625(x - 0.25),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \hat{g}_2(x) = 0.0039 + 0.0625(x - 0.25) + 0.375(x - 0.25)^2.
\end{equation}

The \emph{Newton step} minimises $\hat{g}_2$: setting $d\hat{g}_2/dx = 0$ gives $x_{k+1} = x_0 - g'(x_0)/g''(x_0) = 0.25 - 0.0625/0.75 = 0.167$. The true minimum of $g(x) = x^4$ is at $x = 0$; the Newton iterate overshoots due to the quartic's deviation from quadratic behaviour.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/q3_approximation.pdf}
\caption{Q3: Taylor approximations of $g(x) = x^4$ at $x_0 = 0.25$. The first-order approximation (tangent line, blue dashed) correctly captures the local slope but diverges rapidly from the true function outside a small neighbourhood. The second-order approximation (parabola, red dash-dot) fits the function well in a neighbourhood of $x_0$ and correctly captures the local curvature. The minimum of the parabola at $x = 0.167$ is much closer to the true minimum ($x=0$) than the gradient-step target at $x = 0.25 - \alpha \cdot 0.0625$ for any fixed $\alpha$. This illustrates the fundamental motivation for Newton's method: exploit curvature information to take more informative steps.}

\end{figure}

\subsection{Newton's Method}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Newton's Method with Regularisation (Damped Newton)}
\label{alg:newton}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha \in (0, 1]$, regularisation $\lambda > 0$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $H_k \gets \nabla^2 f(x_k) + \lambda I$ \Comment{Regularised Hessian ensures positive definiteness}
    \State Solve $H_k\, p_k = g_k$ for the Newton direction $p_k$ \Comment{$p_k = H_k^{-1}g_k$}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, p_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}[Quadratic Convergence of Newton's Method]
\label{thm:newton}
Let $f$ be twice continuously differentiable with $\rho$-Lipschitz Hessian in a neighbourhood of a local minimum $x^\star$ where $\nabla^2 f(x^\star) \succ 0$. For $x_0$ sufficiently close to $x^\star$ and $\alpha = 1$, Newton's method satisfies:
\begin{equation}
  \|x_{k+1} - x^\star\| \leq \frac{\rho}{2\mu}\|x_k - x^\star\|^2,
\end{equation}
where $\mu = \lambda_{\min}(\nabla^2 f(x^\star))$. This \emph{quadratic convergence} means the number of accurate digits roughly doubles each iteration.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}[Full Newton Step on Quadratics]
For a quadratic $f(\theta) = \frac{1}{2}\theta^T H \theta - b^T \theta + c$ with constant Hessian $H$, the Newton step $p = H^{-1}\nabla f(\theta_k) = H^{-1}(H\theta_k - b) = \theta_k - H^{-1}b = \theta_k - \theta^\star$ and thus $x_{k+1} = \theta_k - \alpha(\theta_k - \theta^\star)$. With $\alpha = 1$: $x_1 = \theta^\star$ in exactly one step, regardless of dimension or condition number. This is the fundamental advantage of second-order methods on quadratic objectives.
\end{remark}

Settings used: GD runs for 80 iterations; Newton runs for 20 iterations with Hessian regularisation $\lambda = 10^{-8}$.

\subsection{Per-Iteration Cost Discussion}

Newton's method requires $O(d^3)$ per iteration (Hessian inversion via LU decomposition or Cholesky factorisation), compared to $O(d^2)$ for GD (gradient computation on full-batch problems) or $O(md)$ for gradient computation on $m$ samples. For the 2D benchmarks here, this cost is negligible. For high-dimensional problems (e.g., neural networks with millions of parameters), Newton's exact Hessian inversion is infeasible, motivating quasi-Newton methods (L-BFGS) or Gauss--Newton approximations. The results here quantify the convergence-speed benefit that second-order methods provide, justifying the research effort in scalable second-order approximations.

\subsection{Part A: Finite Difference and Nesterov Random Search (Benchmark B)}

\subsubsection{Finite Difference Gradient Approximation}

The forward finite-difference (FFD) approximation of the $i$-th partial derivative is:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x) \approx \frac{f(x + \delta e_i) - f(x)}{\delta}, \qquad e_i = \text{$i$-th standard basis vector.}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Finite Difference Error]
For $f \in C^2$, the FFD error satisfies:
\begin{equation}
  \left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x) - \frac{f(x + \delta e_i) - f(x)}{\delta}\right|
  \leq \underbrace{\frac{\delta}{2}\max\left|\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i^2}\right|}_{\text{truncation error}} + \underbrace{\frac{2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}}{\delta}}_{\text{round-off error}}.
\end{equation}
Minimising over $\delta$ gives the optimal perturbation $\delta^\star \sim \sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}} \approx 10^{-8}$ (for double precision). Practical values must also ensure that $\delta \ll$ the scale of function variation.
\end{theorem}

The experiment uses $\delta = 0.05$ (``good'': truncation $O(0.025)$, round-off negligible) and $\delta = 0.8$ (``poor'': truncation $O(0.4)$, spanning a region of significant curvature change in Benchmark B).

\subsubsection{Nesterov Random Search}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Nesterov Random Search (Zeroth-Order Method)}
\label{alg:nrs}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha > 0$, perturbation $\delta > 0$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State Sample $u_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)$, normalise: $u_k \gets u_k / \|u_k\|$
    \State $\hat{g}_k \gets \dfrac{f(x_k + \delta u_k) - f(x_k)}{\delta}\, u_k$ \Comment{Directional derivative estimate}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \hat{g}_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{remark}[NRS as Gradient Estimator]
The NRS estimator satisfies $\mathbb{E}_{u}[\hat{g}_k] = \frac{1}{d}\nabla f_\delta(x_k)$ where $f_\delta(x) = \mathbb{E}_u[f(x + \delta u)]$ is a Gaussian-smoothed version of $f$. For small $\delta$, $f_\delta \approx f + O(\delta^2)$. The estimator is unbiased for the smoothed function's gradient but introduces $O(\delta)$ bias in the original gradient. Crucially, only \emph{two} function evaluations are needed per iteration regardless of dimension $d$, making NRS asymptotically cheaper than FFD ($d+1$ evaluations) in high dimensions. The cost is high variance: only one random direction is sampled per step.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Part B: Derivative-Free Methods on Benchmark C (Rosenbrock)}

\subsubsection{Nelder--Mead Simplex Method}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Nelder--Mead Simplex (for $d = 2$)}
\label{alg:nm}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step $s$, parameters $\alpha_r=1, \gamma=2, \rho=0.5, \sigma=0.5$, iterations $N$
\State Initialise simplex: $\{v_0, v_1, v_2\}$ with $v_0 = x_0$, $v_j = x_0 + s e_j$ for $j=1,2$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State Sort vertices: $f(v_0) \leq f(v_1) \leq f(v_2)$ \Comment{$v_0$ is best, $v_2$ is worst}
    \State Compute centroid $c = (v_0 + v_1)/2$
    \State $x_r \gets c + \alpha_r(c - v_2)$ (reflect worst through centroid)
    \If{$f(v_0) \leq f(x_r) < f(v_1)$} replace $v_2 \gets x_r$
    \ElsIf{$f(x_r) < f(v_0)$} expand: $x_e = c + \gamma(c-v_2)$; accept $\min(x_r, x_e)$
    \ElsIf{$f(x_r) < f(v_2)$} outside contraction: $x_c = c + \rho(x_r - c)$
    \Else{} inside contraction: $x_c = c + \rho(v_2 - c)$; if fail, shrink all vertices
    \EndIf
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Settings: initial simplex step $= 0.35$, iterations $= 160$.

\subsection{Problem Setup and KKT Conditions}

Minimise Benchmark B subject to $x_1 \geq 0.5$, starting from the infeasible point $x_0 = (0.2, 4.0)$:
\begin{equation}
  \min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^2}\; f(x_1, x_2) = (x_1 - 1)^2 + 5(x_2 - 2)^2 + \sin(x_1) \quad \text{s.t.}\quad x_1 \geq 0.5.
\end{equation}

Since the unconstrained minimum $x^\star \approx (0.5815, 2.0)$ satisfies $x_1^\star > 0.5$, the constraint is \emph{inactive} at the unconstrained minimum. The constrained and unconstrained optima coincide, and by the KKT conditions the Lagrange multiplier $\mu^\star = 0$ (complementary slackness is satisfied with strict feasibility).

\begin{remark}[KKT Conditions]
For this problem, $x^*$ is the constrained optimum if and only if there exists $\mu \geq 0$ such that:
\begin{align}
  \nabla f(x^*) + \mu \nabla g(x^*) &= 0, \quad g(x) = 0.5 - x_1 \leq 0, \\
  \mu g(x^*) &= 0 \quad \text{(complementary slackness)}, \\
  g(x^*) &\leq 0 \quad \text{(primal feasibility)}.
\end{align}
Since $x^*_1 \approx 0.5815 > 0.5$, we have $g(x^*) < 0$, hence $\mu^* = 0$ and the KKT conditions reduce to $\nabla f(x^*) = 0$.
\end{remark}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Projected Gradient Descent (PGD)}
\label{alg:pgd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha > 0$, projection $\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \nabla f(x_k)$ \Comment{Unconstrained gradient step}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets \Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(z_{k+1}) = \max(0.5, z_{k+1,1})\, e_1 + z_{k+1,2}\, e_2$ \Comment{Clip $x_1$ to $[0.5, \infty)$}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Quadratic Penalty Method}
\label{alg:penalty}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha > 0$, penalty weight $\lambda > 0$, iterations $N$
\State Define penalised objective: $F_\lambda(x) = f(x) + \lambda \max(0,\, 0.5 - x_1)$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State Compute $\nabla F_\lambda(x_k)$: gradient of $f$ plus $-\lambda e_1$ if $x_1 < 0.5$, else $\nabla f$
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \nabla F_\lambda(x_k)$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{remark}[Penalty Method Convergence]
As $\lambda \to \infty$, the minimiser of $F_\lambda$ converges to the constrained minimiser. However, large $\lambda$ causes the Hessian of $F_\lambda$ to be ill-conditioned in the infeasible region, requiring smaller step sizes for stability. The step size must satisfy $\alpha < 2/(L + \lambda)$ for convergence in the active-constraint region, explaining why $\alpha$ is reduced from $0.05$ to $0.03$ when $\lambda$ increases from $1.8$ to $4.5$.
\end{remark}

Settings: PGD ($\alpha = 0.08$), penalty with $\lambda \in \{0.15, 1.8, 4.5\}$ ($\alpha = 0.05$ for $\lambda \leq 1.8$; $\alpha = 0.03$ for $\lambda = 4.5$), unconstrained GD ($\alpha = 0.07$). All run for 100 iterations.

\subsection{Comparative Discussion}

\textbf{Projected GD} enforces feasibility as a hard constraint at every iteration. The projection onto the half-space $\{x : x_1 \geq 0.5\}$ is simply $\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x) = (\max(0.5, x_1), x_2)$, a $O(1)$ operation. For this simple constraint, PGD is the gold standard: it is both feasible and optimal. For complex constraint sets where projections are expensive ($O(d^2)$ or worse), penalty methods may be preferred.

\textbf{Penalty methods} trade feasibility for simplicity: only a modified gradient is required. The penalty weight $\lambda$ directly controls the trade-off between objective minimisation and constraint satisfaction. As $\lambda \to \infty$, the penalised minimiser converges to the constrained minimiser, but the conditioning of the subproblem worsens, requiring smaller step sizes. This is the fundamental tension in penalty methods: exact feasibility requires infinite $\lambda$, which makes the subproblem indefinitely hard.

\textbf{Practical implications}: Since the unconstrained minimum of Benchmark B is already in the feasible region, the constraint is inactive here. In general, a constrained minimum lies on the constraint boundary when the objective drives the solution towards infeasibility. The unconstrained GD converges to the same point as PGD, but this is a coincidence of the particular benchmark; in general the two methods give different solutions.

\subsection{Feasible Set and Linear Programme}

Feasible set: $\mathcal{X} = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^2 : 0.5 \leq x_1 \leq 5,\ -5 \leq x_2 \leq 10\}$, a bounded box polytope.

\textbf{Linear Programme:} Minimise $f(x) = a^T x = x_1 + 2x_2$ over $\mathcal{X}$, with $a = [1, 2]^T$.

\begin{proposition}[Fundamental Theorem of Linear Programming]
The minimum of a linear function over a bounded polytope $\mathcal{X}$ is attained at a vertex of $\mathcal{X}$.
\end{proposition}

Since both components of $a$ are positive, each coordinate is minimised at its lower bound independently:
\begin{equation}
  x^\star = (0.5,\, -5), \qquad f^\star = 0.5 + 2 \times (-5) = -9.5.
\end{equation}
For a box $\mathcal{X} = [l_1, u_1] \times [l_2, u_2]$, the LP solution for $\min a^T x$ is:
\begin{equation}
  x^\star_i = \begin{cases} l_i & \text{if } a_i > 0 \\ u_i & \text{if } a_i < 0 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
This $O(d)$ solution per LP subproblem is what enables Frank--Wolfe to avoid expensive projections.

\subsection{Frank--Wolfe Algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Frank--Wolfe (Conditional Gradient Method)}
\label{alg:fw}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0 \in \mathcal{X}$, mixing parameter $\beta \in (0,1)$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_k \gets \arg\min_{z \in \mathcal{X}}\, \nabla f(x_k)^T z$ \Comment{LP: linear minimisation oracle (LMO)}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets \beta\, x_k + (1 - \beta)\, z_k$ \Comment{Convex combination step towards LMO solution}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}[Frank--Wolfe Convergence Rate]
For a convex differentiable $f$ with $L$-Lipschitz gradient over a convex compact set $\mathcal{X}$ with diameter $D = \max_{x,y \in \mathcal{X}}\|x-y\|$, the Frank--Wolfe algorithm with step size $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}
  f(x_k) - f^\star \leq \frac{2LD^2}{k+2}, \qquad k \geq 1.
\end{equation}
This $O(1/k)$ rate is slower than projected gradient descent's $O(1/k^2)$ for strongly convex objectives, but Frank--Wolfe does not require a projection oracle, replacing it with the cheaper LMO.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}[Frank--Wolfe Duality Gap]
The \emph{Frank--Wolfe gap} at iteration $k$ is $g_k = \nabla f(x_k)^T(x_k - z_k) \geq 0$. This serves as a computable upper bound on the suboptimality $f(x_k) - f^\star$: since $f^\star \geq \min_{z \in \mathcal{X}} f(x_k) + \nabla f(x_k)^T(z - x_k) = f(x_k) - g_k$, we have $g_k \geq f(x_k) - f^\star$. The gap can be used as a stopping criterion: when $g_k < \varepsilon$, the iterate is $\varepsilon$-optimal.
\end{remark}

For box constraints, the LMO reduces to coordinate-wise comparison:
\begin{equation}
  (z_k)_i = \begin{cases} l_i & \text{if } (\nabla f(x_k))_i > 0 \\ u_i & \text{if } (\nabla f(x_k))_i \leq 0 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
requiring $O(d)$ operations, compared to $O(d)$ to $O(d^2)$ for projection onto general polytopes.

\subsection{Interior vs Boundary Behaviour: Comparative Analysis}

\textbf{Interior optimum (Case II):} Frank--Wolfe converges but with a characteristic zig-zag pattern as the LMO solution $z_k$ jumps between extreme points of $\mathcal{X}$. The convex combination gradually averages these jumps. The standard Frank--Wolfe convergence rate is $O(1/k)$; with a fixed mixing parameter $\beta$ this rate may not be achieved. Specifically, with $\beta = 0.90$ the algorithm takes effective step size $(1-\beta) = 0.10$ per iteration, which does not decay as required by the $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$ schedule for the $O(1/k)$ theorem. This explains why the gap between $\beta = 0.90$ and $\beta = 0.985$ persists rather than closing.

\textbf{Boundary optimum (Case III):} Frank--Wolfe is particularly well-suited to boundary optima because the LMO solution $z_k$ naturally points towards the boundary where the constrained optimum lies. The iterate converges to $(0.5, -0.337)$ after 140 iterations, still above $f^\star = 0.25$. The residual suboptimality arises from the same fixed-$\beta$ issue: using the decaying schedule $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$ would tighten convergence to $O(L D^2 / k)$ with the specific constant depending on the problem's Lipschitz constant and feasible set diameter.

\textbf{Projection-free advantage:} Frank--Wolfe's core advantage is the LMO oracle replacing projections. For structured constraint sets (nuclear norm balls, simplex, flow polytopes), the LMO can be solved efficiently in problem-specific ways, enabling Frank--Wolfe to scale to high-dimensional constrained problems where projection would be prohibitively expensive.

\subsection{Key Lessons}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Curvature matters most on ill-conditioned problems.} Newton's method with 20 iterations outperforms GD with 80 iterations on Rosenbrock by $5\times$ in objective value, and converges in 1 step on the quadratic. The cost in Hessian computation is justified when the condition number $\kappa$ is large.

\item \textbf{The Polyak step is theoretically optimal but oracle-sensitive.} Its $O(1/k)$ guarantee without knowing $L$ or $\mu$ is compelling, but exact knowledge of $f^\star$ is its Achilles heel. A practical remedy is to use a running estimate of $f^\star$ or combine Polyak with a backtracking mechanism.

\item \textbf{Nesterov acceleration achieves qualitative speedup.} The $O(1/k^2)$ vs $O(1/k)$ rate difference materialises visibly on Rosenbrock ($f = 0.479$ vs $3.39$ for GD after 150 steps). This motivates the widespread use of Nesterov-style momentum in modern deep learning optimisers.

\item \textbf{Adam and coordinate-wise scaling excel on heterogeneous curvature.} For problems with decoupled coordinate curvature (e.g., Benchmark B), Adam's per-coordinate adaptation effectively preconditiones the problem. The advantage diminishes for strongly coupled curvature (Rosenbrock), where full second-order information is needed.

\item \textbf{Derivative-free methods pay a convergence-rate penalty.} FD gradient descent closely tracks exact GD with appropriate $\delta$, but good $\delta$ requires problem knowledge. Nesterov random search converges slowly but requires only 2 function evaluations per step, a practical advantage in very high dimensions or black-box settings.

\item \textbf{Penalty vs projection trade-off.} Projected GD enforces constraints exactly at every step but requires a projection oracle. Penalty methods require only a modified gradient and achieve approximate feasibility for large $\lambda$; the required step size decreases as $\lambda$ increases, creating a computational trade-off.

\item \textbf{Frank--Wolfe avoids projections at the cost of $O(1/k)$ convergence.} The LMO is cheap for structured sets (boxes, simplices) and the algorithm naturally handles boundary optima. For strongly convex objectives inside the feasible set, projected gradient descent is faster; Frank--Wolfe is preferred when projections are expensive.
\end{enumerate}

def gradient_descent(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        x = x - alpha * grad_fn(x)
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Polyak Step Size}]
def polyak_step(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, f_star, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        alpha_k = (loss_fn(x) - f_star) / (np.dot(g, g) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(alpha_k)
        x = x - alpha_k * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Adagrad}]
def adagrad(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha0, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    G = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        G += g**2
        eff_alpha = alpha0 / (np.sqrt(G) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(np.mean(eff_alpha))
        x = x - eff_alpha * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={RMSprop}]
def rmsprop(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha0, beta, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    v = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        v = beta * v + (1 - beta) * g**2
        eff_alpha = alpha0 / (np.sqrt(v) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(np.mean(eff_alpha))
        x = x - eff_alpha * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Heavy Ball / Polyak Momentum}]
def heavy_ball(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    z = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        z = beta * z + alpha * g
        x = x - z
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Nesterov Momentum}]
def nesterov_momentum(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta_max, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    z = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for k in range(1, n_iters + 1):
        beta_k = min((k - 1) / (k + 2), beta_max)
        lookahead = x + beta_k * z
        g = grad_fn(lookahead)
        z = beta_k * z - alpha * g
        x = x + z
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Adam Optimiser}]
def adam_optimiser(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta1, beta2, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    m_vec, v_vec = np.zeros_like(x), np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for t in range(1, n_iters + 1):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        m_vec = beta1 * m_vec + (1 - beta1) * g
        v_vec = beta2 * v_vec + (1 - beta2) * g**2
        m_hat = m_vec / (1 - beta1**t)
        v_hat = v_vec / (1 - beta2**t)
        x = x - alpha * m_hat / (np.sqrt(v_hat) + eps)
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Newton's Method with Damping}]
def newtons_method(grad_fn, hess_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, n_iters, damping=1e-8):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, update_norms = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        H = hess_fn(x) if callable(hess_fn) else hess_fn
        H_reg = H + damping * np.eye(len(x))
        try:
            p = np.linalg.solve(H_reg, g)
        except np.linalg.LinAlgError:
            p = g
        update_norms.append(np.linalg.norm(alpha * p))
        x = x - alpha * p
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(update_norms)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Frank--Wolfe Algorithm}]
def frank_wolfe(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, bounds, beta, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, z_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        z = np.array([bounds[i][0] if g[i] > 0 else bounds[i][1]
                       for i in range(len(g))])
        z_hist.append(z.copy())
        x = beta * x + (1 - beta) * z
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(z_hist)
\end{lstlisting}
