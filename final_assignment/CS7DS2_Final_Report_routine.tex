\begin{abstract}
This report presents a comprehensive comparative study of optimisation algorithms for data
analysis, applied to benchmark problems of increasing difficulty: a linear regression quadratic
loss (convex, near-unit Hessian $\kappa \approx 1.12$, Benchmark A), a sinusoidally perturbed
quadratic (mildly non-quadratic, $\kappa = 10$, Benchmark B), and the Rosenbrock function
(non-convex, $\kappa \approx 2508$ at the optimum, Benchmark C). Six algorithm families are
investigated with theoretical depth:
(1) \textbf{Adaptive step-size rules} --- Polyak, Adagrad, RMSprop, and Heavy Ball --- with
analysis of oracle-complexity, coordinate-wise regret bounds, and stability guarantees;
(2) \textbf{Accelerated and stochastic methods} --- Nesterov momentum and Adam --- with
$O(1/k^2)$ optimality proofs and mini-batch SGD convergence floor analysis;
(3) \textbf{Newton's method} with Tikhonov regularisation and its trust-region interpretation,
demonstrating single-step convergence on quadratics and quadratic convergence near smooth minima;
(4) \textbf{Derivative-free methods} --- finite differences, Nesterov random search, Nelder--Mead,
grid search --- with oracle-complexity hierarchy and automatic differentiation comparison;
(5) \textbf{Constrained optimisation} --- projected gradient descent (with proximal gradient
interpretation) and $\ell_1$ exact penalty (with KKT and strong duality analysis); and
(6) \textbf{Frank--Wolfe} with convergence proofs, FW-gap certificate, and away-step linear
convergence discussion.
A unifying theme is the condition number $\kappa = L/\mu$ as the primary difficulty axis:
GD requires $O(\kappa)$, Nesterov $O(\sqrt{\kappa})$, and Newton $O(\log\log\kappa)$ iterations.
Key findings include: Nesterov achieves $f = 0.479$ vs.\ GD's $f = 3.5$ on Rosenbrock;
Newton converges in one step on quadratics; Frank--Wolfe's fixed $\beta$ cannot converge to
boundary optima; and Nelder--Mead achieves machine-precision accuracy on Rosenbrock without
any gradient information. 50 figures, 38 remarks, 6 theorems, and 4 propositions support the analysis.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================

Optimisation is the mathematical engine at the heart of modern machine learning: every
training procedure reduces to minimising a loss function, and the algorithm chosen to do so
directly impacts convergence speed, solution quality, and computational cost. This assignment
investigates a representative cross-section of the optimisation landscape as covered in
CS7DS2, from classical first-order methods to second-order and derivative-free techniques,
and from unconstrained to constrained settings.

The six questions are structured to build understanding progressively:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Questions 1--2} (Sections~\ref{sec:q1}--\ref{sec:q2}) address the step-size
  selection problem in gradient descent: when should we adapt the step size (Q1: Adagrad,
  RMSprop, Polyak, Heavy Ball), and can we achieve faster than $O(1/k)$ convergence using
  momentum (Q2: Nesterov, Adam, SGD)?
\item \textbf{Question 3} (Section~\ref{sec:q3}) examines second-order (Newton's) methods
  and the qualitative difference between first- and second-order Taylor approximations of
  the objective.
\item \textbf{Question 4} (Section~\ref{sec:q4}) studies derivative-free optimisation: when
  gradients are unavailable, how well do finite-difference approximations and gradient-free
  methods (Nesterov random search, Nelder--Mead, grid search) perform?
\item \textbf{Questions 5--6} (Sections~\ref{sec:q5}--\ref{sec:q6}) extend to constrained
  settings: projected gradient descent and penalty methods for inequality constraints (Q5),
  and the Frank--Wolfe algorithm for linear-constrained problems (Q6).
\end{itemize}

Throughout, a central theme is the interaction between the \emph{geometry of the objective}
(captured by the condition number $\kappa = L/\mu$) and the \emph{algorithm's ability to
exploit it}. The condition numbers span from $\kappa \approx 1$ (Benchmark A, well-conditioned)
to $\kappa \approx 2500$ (Benchmark C, Rosenbrock), with all theoretical predictions about
iteration complexity confirmed empirically. All experiments are implemented in Python (NumPy/Matplotlib,
seed 42) and reproduced from \texttt{CS7DS2\_Final\_Code\_25336453.py}; code listings are in
Appendix~\ref{app:code}.

\section{Benchmark Problems and Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:benchmarks}
% ============================================================

All experiments use a fixed random seed (\texttt{np.random.seed(42)}) for reproducibility. The
three benchmark functions were selected to span a range of difficulties and structural properties.

\begin{remark}[Starting point sensitivity]
The choice of starting point affects convergence speed but not the final limit for all
benchmark functions here, since all have unique local (and global) minima. The starting points
are chosen to be challenging: $\theta_0 = [0,0]^T$ for Benchmark A (far from $[3,4]^T$),
$x_0 = [-1,4]^T$ for Benchmark B (far from $[0.582, 2.0]^T$ and on the wrong side of the
$x_1$ valley), and $x_0 = [-1,1]^T$ for Rosenbrock (in the region of high curvature
$H_{11} \approx 1200$, before the valley floor). For the constrained problems (Q5, Q6),
the starting points are chosen to be infeasible, testing the methods' ability to restore
feasibility.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Benchmark A: Linear Regression Quadratic Loss}
\label{sec:bench_a}

A synthetic dataset with $m = 1000$ samples and two parameters is generated:
\begin{equation}
\theta^{\star} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
x^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_2), \quad
y^{(i)} = (\theta^{\star})^T x^{(i)} + \varepsilon^{(i)}, \quad
\varepsilon^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1).
\end{equation}
The loss function is the mean squared error:
\begin{equation}
J(\theta) = \frac{1}{2m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \left(\theta^T x^{(i)} - y^{(i)}\right)^2
          = \frac{1}{2m}\|X\theta - y\|^2.
\end{equation}
This is a convex quadratic with constant Hessian $H = \frac{1}{m}X^T X \succ 0$ and gradient
$\nabla J(\theta) = \frac{1}{m}X^T(X\theta - y)$. The Lipschitz constant of the gradient is
$L = \lambda_{\max}(H)$ and the strong convexity constant is $\mu = \lambda_{\min}(H)$.
For the dataset generated with \texttt{np.random.seed(42)}, $m = 1000$, and $d = 2$,
the empirical Hessian satisfies $\lambda_{\max}(H) \approx 1.033$ and $\lambda_{\min}(H) \approx 0.925$,
giving $\kappa \approx 1.12$. This near-identity Hessian ($X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_2)$ implies
$X^TX/m \to I$ by LLN) means the problem is nearly perfectly conditioned. The gradient descent
step size $\alpha = 0.08 \ll 1/L \approx 0.968$ is very conservative: using $\alpha = 2/(\mu+L)
\approx 1.02$ would give much faster convergence. With $\alpha = 0.08$:
$\rho = \max(|1 - \alpha L|, |1 - \alpha\mu|) = \max(0.917, 0.926) \approx 0.926$,
requiring $N_{10^{-4}} \approx 121$ iterations to reach $10^{-4}$ accuracy (consistent with
the observed convergence in 60--80 iterations to the noise floor $J^\star = 0.4826$).
Because of observation noise $\varepsilon$, the OLS minimum satisfies
$J(\theta^{\star}_{\text{OLS}}) \approx \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 = 0.5$, confirmed empirically as
$J^\star \approx 0.4826$. Starting point: $\theta_0 = [0, 0]^T$.

\subsection{Benchmark B: Toy Neural Network Loss}
\label{sec:bench_b}

\begin{equation}
f(x_1, x_2) = (x_1 - 1)^2 + 5(x_2 - 2)^2 + \sin(x_1).
\end{equation}
The gradient is
$\nabla f = \bigl[2(x_1 - 1) + \cos(x_1),\ 10(x_2 - 2)\bigr]^T$
and the Hessian is $H = \operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(2 - \sin(x_1),\ 10\bigr)$.
The eigenvalue ratio $\lambda_2/\lambda_1 = 10/(2 - \sin(x_1))$ varies between approximately
$10/1 = 10$ and $10/3 \approx 3.3$, creating moderate anisotropy. The $\sin$ term makes
this function \emph{non-quadratic} but only mildly so: the Hessian diagonal entry
$2 - \sin(x_1)$ deviates from 2 by at most 1. The global Lipschitz constant is
$L = \max(2 - \sin(x_1), 10) = 10$ (since $|2 - \sin x| \leq 3 < 10$). The strong convexity
constant is $\mu = \min(2 - \sin(x_1)) = 1$, giving $\kappa = L/\mu = 10$.
The minimum lies at approximately $(0.582, 2.0)$ with $f^\star \approx 0.7244$, found by
solving $2(x_1 - 1) + \cos(x_1) = 0$ numerically: at $x_1 = 0.582$,
$2(0.582 - 1) + \cos(0.582) = -0.836 + 0.836 = 0$ (\checkmark). Starting point: $x_0 = [-1, 4]^T$.

\subsection{Benchmark C: Rosenbrock Function}
\label{sec:bench_c}

The Rosenbrock function \cite{rosenbrock1960automatic} is defined as:
\begin{equation}
f(x_1, x_2) = (1 - x_1)^2 + 100(x_2 - x_1^2)^2.
\end{equation}
The global minimum is at $(1, 1)$ with $f^\star = 0$. The Hessian at the optimum is
\begin{equation}
H(1,1) = \begin{bmatrix} 802 & -400 \\ -400 & 200 \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
with eigenvalues approximately $1001.6$ and $0.4$ giving condition number
$\kappa = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min} \approx 2502$. Away from the optimum, for example at the
starting point $x_0 = (-1, 1)$, the Hessian is
$H(-1,1) = \operatorname{diag}(2 + 1200 - 400(-1+1),\ 200) + \text{off-diag terms}$.
The curved, narrow valley $x_2 \approx x_1^2$ and extreme anisotropy make
this the canonical hard test for local optimisation methods. Starting point: $x_0 = [-1, 1]^T$.

\subsection{Benchmark D: Constrained Feasible Set}
\label{sec:bench_d}

For constrained problems (Questions 5 and 6), the feasible set is:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{X} = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^2 : 0.5 \leq x_1 \leq 5,\ -5 \leq x_2 \leq 10\}.
\end{equation}
This is a box (hyperrectangle), for which both projection and the linear minimisation oracle
used by Frank--Wolfe decompose into independent per-coordinate operations.

\begin{remark}[Global vs. local convergence: non-convex landscape analysis]
The benchmarks span three qualitatively different global landscapes:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Benchmark A} (quadratic): globally convex with a unique minimiser. All
  convergent algorithms find the global minimum.
\item \textbf{Benchmark B} ($f = (x_1-1)^2 + 5(x_2-2)^2 + \sin x_1$): the additive
  $\sin x_1$ term creates periodic local bumps in the $x_1$ direction, but their amplitude
  (bounded by 1) is dominated by the quadratic terms (which grow as $(x_1-1)^2$), so the
  function has a unique global minimiser at $(0.582, 2.0)$ and no local minima. Methods can
  converge from any starting point.
\item \textbf{Benchmark C} (Rosenbrock): non-convex but with a unique local (and global)
  minimum at $(1,1)$. The flat curved valley can cause iterates to ``chase'' the valley
  floor for a long time before making progress towards $(1,1)$, but they cannot get trapped
  at a spurious local minimum.
\end{itemize}
The absence of local minima means that all algorithms that converge will find the global
optimum. The difficulty of Rosenbrock is entirely due to ill-conditioning ($\kappa \approx 2500$),
not non-convexity.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Why Rosenbrock is difficult]
Benchmark C (Rosenbrock) is the canonical ``hard'' test function for several compounding reasons:
(1) \emph{Narrow curved valley}: the path to the minimum follows $x_2 = x_1^2$, a parabolic
curve that first-order methods struggle to track since the gradient is mostly perpendicular to
the valley;
(2) \emph{High condition number}: $\kappa \approx 2500$ at the optimum, requiring tiny step
sizes to maintain stability across the narrow dimension while making progress along the valley
glacially slow;
(3) \emph{Non-convexity near the saddle}: the starting point $(-1, 1)$ lies in a region where
the function has a saddle-like structure (gradient changes sign in $x_1$) before the valley
floor is reached; and
(4) \emph{Changing curvature}: the Hessian $H_{11} = 2 + 1200x_1^2 - 400x_2$ varies dramatically
along the trajectory (from $\approx 1202$ to $\approx 802$), invalidating locally-chosen
step sizes after a few steps.
\end{remark}

\section{Question 1: Adaptive Step-Size Methods \hfill [30 marks]}
\label{sec:q1}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Motivation and Background}

Gradient descent with a constant step size $\alpha$ requires $\alpha \leq 1/L$ for convergence
(where $L$ is the Lipschitz constant of $\nabla f$), and achieves the optimal fixed-step rate
$\alpha = 2/(\mu + L)$ for strongly convex functions. In practice, $L$ and $\mu$ are rarely
known analytically, and the optimal step size must be estimated. Too large a step causes
divergence; too small a step causes slow convergence. The condition number
$\kappa = L/\mu$ determines how difficult it is to choose a good constant step: for large
$\kappa$, the optimal $\alpha$ is very small (to avoid instability in the steep direction)
while the convergence along the flat direction is correspondingly slow.

Adaptive step-size rules address this by estimating effective curvature on-the-fly from
gradient history, removing the need to specify a fixed global step. Conceptually, each method
tries to answer: given the history of gradients, what step size should I use next to make
the fastest progress without overshooting? We compare four adaptive methods against a constant
step-size gradient descent baseline across all three benchmarks for 120 iterations.

\begin{remark}[Gradient descent as steepest descent in the Euclidean norm]
Gradient descent can be viewed as steepest descent in the Euclidean norm: at each step,
$x_{k+1} = x_k - \alpha g_k$ solves $\min_{\|d\| \leq \alpha} f(x_k + d) \approx f(x_k) + g_k^T d$,
which gives $d^\star = -\alpha g_k / \|g_k\|$ (unit ball in the $\ell_2$ norm). Changing
the norm changes the update: steepest descent in the $H$-norm (for a positive-definite $H$)
gives the Newton direction $d = -H^{-1}g_k$. Adagrad's diagonal preconditioning
$x_{k+1} = x_k - \alpha_0 \text{diag}(G_k)^{-1/2} g_k$ corresponds to steepest descent in
the norm $\|d\|_A = \sqrt{d^T \text{diag}(G_k) d}$, reweighting coordinates by their
accumulated gradient magnitudes.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Effective step size]
For coordinate-wise methods, the \emph{effective step size} at iteration $k$ for coordinate $i$
is the actual multiplier applied to the gradient: for Adagrad,
$\alpha_k^{(i)} = \alpha_0 / (\sqrt{G_k^{(i)}} + \varepsilon)$; for RMSprop,
$\alpha_k^{(i)} = \alpha_0 / (\sqrt{v_k^{(i)}} + \varepsilon)$.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Algorithm Formulations}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Constant Step-Size Gradient Descent (Baseline)}
\label{alg:gd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \nabla f(x_k)$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Polyak Step Size}
\label{alg:polyak}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, optimal value $f^{\star}$, tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $\alpha_k \gets \dfrac{f(x_k) - f^{\star}}{\|g_k\|^2 + \varepsilon}$
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha_k\, g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{proposition}[Polyak convergence]
For a convex function with minimum $f^\star$, the Polyak step satisfies
$f(x_{k+1}) - f^\star \leq \tfrac{1}{2}\|x_k - x^\star\|^2 / \alpha_k$.
When $f^\star$ is known exactly and $f$ is strongly convex with constant $\mu$, this gives
the geometric convergence:
\begin{equation}
\|x_{k+1} - x^\star\|^2 \leq \left(1 - \frac{\mu}{\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2/(f(x_k)-f^\star)}\right)\|x_k - x^\star\|^2.
\end{equation}
For a strongly convex function, $f(x) - f^\star \leq \|\nabla f(x)\|^2 / (2\mu)$, so the
ratio $\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2/(f(x_k)-f^\star) \geq 2\mu$, yielding contraction by factor
$(1 - \mu \cdot \alpha_k) \leq (1 - 2\mu^2/\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Adagrad}
\label{alg:adagrad}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, base learning rate $\alpha_0 > 0$, $\varepsilon > 0$, iterations $N$
\State $G_0 \gets \mathbf{0} \in \mathbb{R}^d$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $G_{k+1} \gets G_k + g_k \odot g_k$ \Comment{Coordinate-wise squared gradient sum}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \dfrac{\alpha_0}{\sqrt{G_{k+1}} + \varepsilon} \odot g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Adagrad's update adapts the effective step size per coordinate: frequently-updated coordinates
receive smaller steps as $G_k^{(i)}$ grows, while infrequently-updated ones retain larger steps.
For convex online learning, Adagrad achieves $O(\log T / \sqrt{T})$ regret \cite{duchi2011adaptive}.
More precisely, for $f$ convex and $G_T^{(i)} = \sum_{k=1}^T g_k^{(i)2}$:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{k=1}^T \bigl(f(x_k) - f(x^\star)\bigr)
\leq \frac{1}{2\alpha_0}\sum_{i=1}^d \|x_0^{(i)} - x^{\star(i)}\|^2 \sqrt{G_T^{(i)}}
+ \frac{\alpha_0}{2}\sum_{i=1}^d \sqrt{G_T^{(i)}}.
\end{equation}
This is the \emph{adaptive regret bound}: the per-coordinate scaling by $1/\sqrt{G_T^{(i)}}$
replaces the global Lipschitz constant by the per-coordinate gradient history, giving tighter
bounds on sparse or heterogeneously scaled problems. A key limitation is the
\emph{monotonically decreasing} effective step: since $G_k$ only increases, the step eventually
shrinks to zero even if gradient magnitudes later decrease, potentially causing premature stalling.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{RMSprop}
\label{alg:rmsprop}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, learning rate $\alpha_0 > 0$, decay $\beta \in (0,1)$,
         $\varepsilon > 0$, iterations $N$
\State $v_0 \gets \mathbf{0} \in \mathbb{R}^d$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $v_{k+1} \gets \beta\, v_k + (1 - \beta)\, g_k \odot g_k$
    \Comment{Exponential moving average (EMA) of $g_k^2$}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \dfrac{\alpha_0}{\sqrt{v_{k+1}} + \varepsilon} \odot g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

RMSprop replaces Adagrad's unbounded accumulation with an exponential moving average of squared
gradients, using forgetting factor $\beta = 0.9$. The effective window is approximately
$1/(1-\beta) = 10$ recent iterations. This prevents the effective step from shrinking
monotonically, making RMSprop suitable for non-stationary problems. Unlike Adagrad, RMSprop
lacks a general worst-case convergence guarantee, but works well in practice for neural network
training.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Polyak Momentum / Heavy Ball}
\label{alg:heavyball}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha > 0$, momentum $\beta \in [0, 1)$, iterations $N$
\State $z_0 \gets \mathbf{0} \in \mathbb{R}^d$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets \beta\, z_k + \alpha\, \nabla f(x_k)$
    \Comment{Accumulated momentum buffer}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - z_{k+1}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The Heavy Ball method (also known as gradient descent with momentum, or the Polyak momentum
method, distinct from Polyak's adaptive step size) adds a momentum term that accumulates
gradient history, allowing the iterate to accelerate in consistent descent directions
and damp oscillations in alternating directions. The $z_k$ buffer acts as a ``heavy ball''
rolling in a potential well: the gradient exerts a restoring force while momentum maintains
the velocity in the downhill direction. For strongly convex quadratics with condition number $\kappa$, the optimal
Heavy Ball parameters are:
\begin{equation}
\alpha^\star = \frac{4}{(\sqrt{L} + \sqrt{\mu})^2}, \qquad
\beta^\star = \left(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa} - 1}{\sqrt{\kappa} + 1}\right)^2,
\end{equation}
achieving a convergence rate of $(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa}+1})^{2k}$,
which is the square root of gradient descent's rate $(\frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1})^{2k}$.

\begin{remark}[Heavy Ball instability on non-quadratic objectives]
The optimal Heavy Ball parameters $\alpha^\star$, $\beta^\star$ are derived under the assumption
that $f$ is a fixed quadratic. For non-quadratic objectives, three failure modes arise:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \emph{Varying curvature along the trajectory.} On Rosenbrock, $H_{11}(x) = 2 + 1200x_1^2 - 400x_2$
  changes from $\approx 1202$ at the start to $\approx 802$ at the optimum, shifting the
  optimal step size from $\alpha^\star(1202, 200) \approx 0.0004$ to $\alpha^\star(802, 200) \approx 0.0007$
  along the path. No single $\alpha$ is simultaneously optimal everywhere.
\item \emph{Momentum overshoot in non-descent directions.} Momentum accumulates a velocity
  vector $z_k = \sum_{j \leq k} \beta^{k-j}\alpha g_j$. If the gradient direction rotates
  (e.g., as the iterate rounds a corner of the Rosenbrock valley), the accumulated $z_k$
  points in the \emph{wrong} direction, causing overshoot. This is why Heavy Ball achieves
  $f = 1.40$ after 120 iterations on Rosenbrock while Nesterov (with its lookahead correction)
  achieves $f = 0.479$ after 150 iterations.
\item \emph{Sensitivity to $\beta$.} For quadratics, the optimal $\beta^\star \to 1$ as
  $\kappa \to \infty$ (Rosenbrock: $\beta^\star \approx 0.97$ for $\kappa = 2500$). Using
  this near-unity $\beta$ amplifies the overshooting problem because each step mostly preserves
  velocity. In practice, a conservative $\beta \approx 0.86$--$0.90$ (as used here) reduces
  oscillation at the cost of the theoretical speedup.
\end{enumerate}
The Nesterov method avoids failure mode (2) through its lookahead: by evaluating the gradient
at $\tilde{x}_k = x_k + \beta_k z_k$ (the predicted next position), it corrects the velocity
before committing, providing implicit robustness to gradient direction changes.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Why RMSprop exceeds the noise floor on Benchmarks A and B]
On Benchmark A, RMSprop converges to $J = 0.5027 > J^\star = 0.4826$, and on Benchmark B
to $f = 0.7271 > f^\star = 0.7244$. The cause is EMA inertia: near the minimum, the gradient
norm $\|g_k\|$ is small, but $v_k = 0.9 v_{k-1} + 0.1 g_k^2$ retains nonzero memory of the
large gradients encountered earlier (EMA window $\approx 10$ iterations). This causes
$\alpha_0/\sqrt{v_k}$ to remain larger than the safe step for the region, inducing persistent
oscillation around the minimum. This is the \emph{bias effect} of EMA: the effective step
adapts to average recent gradient magnitude, not the current local curvature, causing
over-stepping in flat regions after steep initial descent.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Comparative Analysis}

For an $L$-smooth, $\mu$-strongly convex function, gradient descent with $\alpha = 1/L$
achieves the linear convergence rate:
\begin{equation}
f(x_{k+1}) - f^\star \leq \left(1 - \frac{\mu}{L}\right)(f(x_k) - f^\star)
= \left(\frac{\kappa - 1}{\kappa}\right)(f(x_k) - f^\star),
\end{equation}
where $\kappa = L/\mu$ is the condition number. For our benchmarks:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Benchmark A: $\kappa \approx 1.12$ ($\lambda_{\max} \approx 1.033$, $\lambda_{\min} \approx 0.925$).
  With code step $\alpha = 0.08$, $\rho \approx 0.926$, $N_{10^{-4}} \approx 121$ iters
  (observed: converges to noise floor $J^\star = 0.4826$ in $\approx 60$--$80$ iterations).
\item Benchmark B: $\kappa = 10$ ($L = 10$, $\mu = 1$). With code step $\alpha = 0.06$,
  $\rho = 1 - 0.06 \cdot 1 = 0.94$, $N_{10^{-4}} \approx 152$ iters (observed: converges in
  $\approx 80$ iterations due to near-quadratic structure near minimum).
\item Benchmark C: $\kappa \approx 2508$ (near optimum at $(1,1)$), GD rate $\rho \approx 0.9992$,
  $N_{10^{-4}} \approx 51{,}500$ iters (observed: 120 iterations gives $f \approx 3.5$,
  far from converged — as predicted).
\end{itemize}
These condition numbers directly explain why GD struggles on Benchmark C while performing
well on A and B.

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Convergence speed:} When $f^{\star}$ is known exactly, Polyak converges fastest
  (Rosenbrock: $f = 0.009$). In the more common case where $f^\star$ is unknown (or
  misspecified), Adagrad and Heavy Ball match or exceed the GD baseline.
\item \textbf{Smoothness of trajectories:} RMSprop produces the smoothest convergence curves
  due to exponential averaging of gradient magnitudes. Heavy Ball shows characteristic
  overshoot followed by damped oscillation. Adagrad's trajectory is smooth but eventually
  stalls.
\item \textbf{Stability guarantees:} The GD baseline is most stable but slowest. Polyak is
  \emph{conditionally stable}: stable when $f^\star$ is known exactly, catastrophically
  unstable otherwise. Adagrad is unconditionally stable due to its monotonically decreasing
  step size, though this may cause premature stalling on non-stationary or high-dimensional
  problems where early gradients are large relative to later ones.
\item \textbf{Practical considerations:} In practice, $f^\star$ is rarely available, limiting
  Polyak's applicability. A standard alternative is the \emph{Armijo--Wolfe line search},
  which adaptively selects $\alpha_k$ to satisfy the Armijo (sufficient decrease) and Wolfe
  (curvature) conditions:
  $f(x_k - \alpha_k g_k) \leq f(x_k) - c_1 \alpha_k \|g_k\|^2$ and
  $|\nabla f(x_k - \alpha_k g_k)^T g_k| \leq c_2 \|g_k\|^2$,
  without requiring $f^\star$. Unlike Polyak, Armijo--Wolfe is unconditionally convergent
  for smooth functions and is the standard step-size rule for gradient descent and
  quasi-Newton methods in practice. RMSprop and Adagrad are more practical choices for deep
  learning, particularly when feature scales vary across coordinates.
\item \textbf{Heavy Ball provides a simple} momentum mechanism that requires only one
  additional hyperparameter ($\beta$) beyond the base step size and is well-understood
  theoretically for quadratic objectives.
\item \textbf{Heavy Ball and condition number:} For strongly convex quadratics, the optimal
  Heavy Ball parameters give convergence rate $\rho_{\text{HB}} = (\sqrt{\kappa} - 1)/(\sqrt{\kappa} + 1)$,
  squaring the GD rate $\rho_{\text{GD}} = (\kappa - 1)/(\kappa + 1)$. For
  $\kappa = 2500$: $\rho_{\text{GD}} \approx 0.9992$, $\rho_{\text{HB}} \approx 0.9608$ ---
  a significant improvement, explaining Heavy Ball's superior Rosenbrock performance.
\end{itemize}

The theoretical speedup for Heavy Ball ($\kappa = 2500$: $51{,}545/513 \approx 100\times$

\emph{lookahead} correction, which actively damps these oscillations.

\subsection{Algorithm Formulations and Theoretical Background}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Nesterov Accelerated Gradient (NAG)}
\label{alg:nesterov}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha > 0$, max momentum $\beta_{\max}$, iterations $N$
\State $z_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 1, 2, \ldots, N$}
    \State $\beta_k \gets \min\!\left(\dfrac{k-1}{k+2},\ \beta_{\max}\right)$
    \Comment{Increasing momentum schedule}
    \State $\tilde{x}_k \gets x_k + \beta_k\, z_k$
    \Comment{Gradient evaluated at \emph{lookahead} point}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets \beta_k\, z_k - \alpha\, \nabla f(\tilde{x}_k)$
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k + z_{k+1}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The key distinction from Heavy Ball is the \emph{lookahead}: the gradient is evaluated at the
extrapolated point $\tilde{x}_k = x_k + \beta_k z_k$ rather than at $x_k$ itself. This allows
the iterate to ``look ahead'' and correct its trajectory before committing, preventing the
overshooting that plagues Heavy Ball in non-quadratic regions.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/q2_momentum_analysis.pdf}
\caption{Q2: Nesterov momentum analysis. \textbf{Left:} The increasing momentum schedule
$\beta_k = \min((k-1)/(k+2), \beta_{\max})$ starts at 0 (pure gradient step) and grows towards
$\beta_{\max} = 0.90$. This gradual ramp-up is critical for the $O(1/k^2)$ guarantee: too
much momentum early (before the iterate settles into the valley) causes divergence. Heavy
Ball's constant $\beta = 0.90$ applies maximum momentum from the start, which can cause
oscillation on non-quadratic problems but is optimal for quadratics once calibrated.
\textbf{Right:} Theoretical convergence bounds for $\kappa = 10$. Nesterov's
$2L\|x_0-x^\star\|^2/(k+1)^2$ bound decays as $1/k^2$, faster than GD's $(\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1)^k$
linear rate. Heavy Ball with optimal parameters matches Nesterov for quadratics
(identical rates asymptotically for $\kappa = 10$), but this optimality degrades on
non-quadratic objectives.}
\label{fig:q2_momentum}
\end{figure}

\begin{theorem}[Nesterov acceleration]
For a convex, $L$-smooth function, Nesterov's method with step size $\alpha = 1/L$ and momentum
schedule $\beta_k = (k-1)/(k+2)$ achieves the convergence rate
\begin{equation}
f(x_k) - f^\star \leq \frac{2L\|x_0 - x^\star\|^2}{(k+1)^2} = O\!\left(\frac{1}{k^2}\right),
\end{equation}
which is \emph{optimal} for first-order methods on smooth convex functions~\cite{nesterov1983method}.
This is a quadratic improvement over gradient descent's $O(1/k)$ rate. Equivalently, to achieve
$\varepsilon$-accuracy, Nesterov requires $O(1/\sqrt{\varepsilon})$ iterations vs.\ gradient
descent's $O(1/\varepsilon)$: a $\sqrt{1/\varepsilon}$ speedup.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}[Why $O(1/k^2)$ is optimal: lower bounds for first-order methods]
The $O(1/k^2)$ rate of Nesterov's method is \emph{minimax optimal}: no first-order method
can achieve better than $O(1/k^2)$ on all $L$-smooth convex functions simultaneously.
The matching lower bound is established by constructing a ``worst-case'' function (essentially
a tridiagonal quadratic) for which any first-order method satisfying $x_{k+1} \in \text{span}(x_0, g_0, \ldots, g_k)$
requires at least $\Omega(1/k^2)$ iterations. This \emph{oracle complexity lower bound} is the
reason why $O(1/k^2)$ methods (Nesterov, FISTA, etc.) are called ``optimal first-order
methods'' --- they match the lower bound and cannot be improved without additional assumptions
(e.g., strong convexity gives $O(\rho^k)$ linear rates). For strongly convex functions
with condition number $\kappa$, the optimal first-order rate is $O((\sqrt{\kappa}-1)/(\sqrt{\kappa}+1))^k$,
achieved by Nesterov's strongly-convex variant.
\end{remark}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Adam Optimiser}
\label{alg:adam}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, learning rate $\alpha > 0$, $\beta_1, \beta_2 \in [0,1)$,
         $\varepsilon > 0$, iterations $N$
\State $m_0 \gets \mathbf{0},\ v_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$t = 1, 2, \ldots, N$}
    \State $g_t \gets \nabla f(x_t)$
    \State $m_t \gets \beta_1\, m_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_1)\, g_t$
    \Comment{EMA of gradient (first moment)}
    \State $v_t \gets \beta_2\, v_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_2)\, g_t \odot g_t$
    \Comment{EMA of squared gradient (second moment)}
    \State $\hat{m}_t \gets m_t\, /\, (1 - \beta_1^t)$
    \Comment{Bias-corrected first moment}
    \State $\hat{v}_t \gets v_t\, /\, (1 - \beta_2^t)$
    \Comment{Bias-corrected second moment}
    \State $x_{t+1} \gets x_t - \alpha\, \hat{m}_t\, /\, (\sqrt{\hat{v}_t} + \varepsilon)$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Adam \cite{kingma2014adam} combines momentum (via $m_t$) with per-coordinate adaptive scaling (via $v_t$), inheriting
advantages of both RMSprop and momentum methods. The bias correction $(1 - \beta_1^t)^{-1}$ and
$(1 - \beta_2^t)^{-1}$ correct for the zero-initialisation of the moment estimates: without
these, early iterations would have artificially small moments due to initialising at zero. The
effective step size for coordinate $i$ is approximately bounded by:
\begin{equation}
\left|\Delta x_t^{(i)}\right| \approx \frac{\alpha \hat{m}_t^{(i)}}{\sqrt{\hat{v}_t^{(i)}} + \varepsilon}
\leq \frac{\alpha |1 + \beta_1|}{1 - \beta_1},
\end{equation}
meaning the maximum update per coordinate is bounded by $\approx \alpha (1+\beta_1)/(1-\beta_1)$,
providing implicit stability.

\begin{remark}[Interpretation of Adam's $\beta_1 = 0.82$]
The standard default for Adam is $\beta_1 = 0.9$. The slightly reduced value $\beta_1 = 0.82$
used here gives less ``memory'' to gradient history, making the first moment estimate respond
more quickly to recent gradient information. On the tested benchmarks, this yields faster
initial convergence at the cost of slightly higher oscillation near the minimum. The choice
reflects the relatively small number of iterations (150) compared to typical deep learning
applications where $\beta_1 = 0.9$ is used over thousands of iterations.
\end{remark}

\textbf{Why Nesterov outperforms Adam on Rosenbrock:} Adam's second-moment estimates
$\hat{v}_t^{(i)} \approx \mathbb{E}[g_t^{(i)2}]$ are per-coordinate, but the Rosenbrock
objective's difficulty arises from \emph{cross-coordinate} curvature: the off-diagonal Hessian
term $H_{12} = -400x_1$ is large and position-dependent. Adam's diagonal curvature estimate
cannot capture this coupling, while Nesterov's lookahead momentum aligns more naturally with
the curved valley floor by building velocity in the locally consistent descent direction.

\begin{remark}[Adam's convergence in the deterministic setting]
Adam was designed for stochastic gradient settings where gradient noise provides implicit
regularisation. In a deterministic setting (like the benchmarks here), Adam is not guaranteed
to converge to a local minimum of smooth objectives: the non-decreasing nature of the
cumulative second-moment estimate $v_t$ can cause the adaptive step sizes to shrink too fast
in some coordinates, ``locking'' the iterate away from the minimum. Empirically, Adam performs
best on deep learning tasks with heterogeneous feature scales and non-stationary gradient
distributions. On smooth, low-dimensional, deterministic problems like the benchmarks here,
momentum methods (Nesterov, Heavy Ball) with their global momentum accumulation provide a
better inductive bias. For such problems, full-batch gradient methods with hand-tuned or
line-search step sizes typically dominate Adam.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Mini-Batch Stochastic Gradient Descent}

Mini-batch SGD on Benchmark A uses learning rate $\alpha = 0.06$, batch sizes $b \in \{5, 40\}$,
and 50 epochs, with data indices randomly permuted at each epoch.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Mini-Batch SGD with Epoch Shuffling}
\label{alg:sgd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Dataset $(X, y)$ with $m$ rows, initial $\theta_0$, learning rate $\alpha$, batch size $b$, epochs $E$
\For{$e = 1, 2, \ldots, E$}
    \State Sample a uniformly random permutation $\sigma$ of $\{1,\ldots,m\}$
    \For{$j = 0, b, 2b, \ldots$}
        \State $\mathcal{B} \gets \{\sigma(j+1), \ldots, \sigma(j+b)\}$ \Comment{Mini-batch indices}
        \State $g \gets \frac{1}{b} X_{\mathcal{B}}^T(X_{\mathcal{B}}\theta - y_{\mathcal{B}})$
        \Comment{Mini-batch gradient}
        \State $\theta \gets \theta - \alpha\, g$
    \EndFor
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The mini-batch gradient $g$ is an unbiased estimator of the full gradient $\nabla J(\theta)$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[g] = \nabla J(\theta), \qquad
\operatorname{Var}[g^{(i)}] = \frac{1}{b}\operatorname{Var}[\nabla J_j(\theta)],
\end{equation}
where $\nabla J_j(\theta) = (x^{(j)})^T(x^{(j)T}\theta - y^{(j)}) / m$ is the gradient
contribution from sample $j$. Larger batches reduce variance proportionally to $1/b$,
but perform fewer updates per pass over the data.

\begin{remark}[Batch size and the bias-variance trade-off in SGD]
Define $\sigma_g^2 = \operatorname{Var}[\nabla J_j(\theta)]$ as the per-sample gradient
variance. The mean squared error of the mini-batch gradient estimator is
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}\|g - \nabla J\|^2 = \frac{\sigma_g^2}{b}.
\end{equation}
For a fixed number of epochs $E$ (full passes over the data), the total number of gradient
steps is $T = Em/b$. The \emph{convergence floor} of SGD (the irreducible error that persists
even after many iterations) is proportional to $\alpha \sigma_g^2 / b$. To minimise the
floor, one should use large $b$ and/or small $\alpha$. However, the \emph{rate} of reaching
the floor is faster for small $b$ (more frequent updates). This trade-off drives the practical
rule: use the largest batch size that fits in GPU memory, then tune $\alpha$ to balance
convergence speed with the noise floor.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[SGD convergence floor for strongly convex objectives]
\label{thm:sgd_floor}
Let $J$ be $L$-smooth and $\mu$-strongly convex, and assume the per-sample gradient noise
satisfies $\mathbb{E}_j\|\nabla J_j(\theta) - \nabla J(\theta)\|^2 \leq \sigma_g^2$ for all
$\theta$. For mini-batch SGD with constant step $\alpha \leq 1/L$ and batch size $b$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}\bigl[J(\theta_k) - J^\star\bigr]
\leq (1 - \alpha\mu)^k \bigl(J(\theta_0) - J^\star\bigr)
+ \frac{\alpha\,\sigma_g^2}{2\mu b}.
\end{equation}
The first term decays geometrically to zero; the second is the irreducible
\emph{SGD convergence floor}:
\begin{equation}
\text{floor}(\alpha, b) = \frac{\alpha\,\sigma_g^2}{2\mu b}.
\end{equation}
To achieve floor $\leq \varepsilon$, one requires $\alpha \leq 2\mu b \varepsilon / \sigma_g^2$.
With Robbins--Monro step $\alpha_k = \alpha_0 / (1 + \gamma k)$ (satisfying $\sum_k \alpha_k =
\infty$, $\sum_k \alpha_k^2 < \infty$), the floor vanishes and $\mathbb{E}[J(\theta_k) - J^\star]
= O(1/k)$. This explains \cref{fig:q2_sgd_decay}: constant-$\alpha$ SGD floors above $J^\star$,
while decaying $\alpha_k$ eventually reaches $J^\star$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}[Floor vs.\ noise floor distinction]
The SGD convergence floor $\alpha\sigma_g^2/(2\mu b)$ in \cref{thm:sgd_floor} is an
\emph{optimisation} floor: it persists even if $\sigma_g^2 > 0$ solely due to mini-batch
sampling. It is \emph{distinct} from the statistical noise floor $J^\star = \sigma_\varepsilon^2/2$,
which is inherent to the data-generating process. In \cref{fig:q2_sgd_batch}, both batch sizes
converge to $J^\star \approx 0.4826$; the additional SGD floor (excess over $J^\star$) is
small because the mini-batch gradient noise at convergence is much smaller than the irreducible
OLS residual error. In a regime where $\sigma_\varepsilon^2 = 0$ (noiseless data), the SGD
floor would be the dominant effect, causing $J(\theta_k)$ to oscillate above 0.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Effect of Observation Noise on SGD Convergence}

The experiment is repeated with output noise scaled by a factor of 6 (standard deviation
$\sigma = 6$ instead of $\sigma = 1$). Let $\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ denote
the normalised noise draws and $y^{(i)} = \theta^{*T} x^{(i)} + \sigma\varepsilon_i$.
At the OLS minimiser $\hat{\theta}$, the residuals $r^{(i)} = y^{(i)} - \hat{\theta}^T x^{(i)}
\approx \sigma\varepsilon_i$, so the noise floor scales as:
\begin{equation}
J^\star_\sigma = \frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i=1}^m r_i^2
\approx \frac{\sigma^2}{2m}\sum_{i=1}^m \varepsilon_i^2
\xrightarrow{m \to \infty} \frac{\sigma^2}{2}
\approx \frac{36}{2} = 18,
\end{equation}
confirming that the floor scales as $\sigma^2$: a $6\times$ increase in noise gives a
$36\times$ increase in the irreducible loss. This is a consequence of the bias--variance
decomposition: the OLS residual variance is $\mathbb{E}[r_i^2] = \sigma^2(1 - h_{ii})$
where $h_{ii}$ are the hat-matrix diagonal entries, and $\frac{1}{m}\sum_i h_{ii} = d/m \to 0$
for $m \gg d$.

\begin{remark}
The noise floor increase from $0.48$ to approximately $18$ illustrates a key practical point:
for regression problems, reducing observation noise (e.g., through better data collection or
feature engineering) has a much larger impact on final model quality than tuning
optimisation hyperparameters. Additionally, this demonstrates that for regression tasks, the
SGD convergence problem is \emph{fundamentally statistical}, not purely algorithmic: no
optimisation algorithm can reduce $J$ below $\sigma^2/2$ since this irreducible error
is inherent to the data-generating process.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Beyond SGD: variance reduction methods]
The fundamental limitation of SGD is the constant noise floor created by the mini-batch
gradient variance. \emph{Variance reduction} methods (SVRG, SAGA, SARAH) overcome this by
periodically computing the full gradient and using it to de-correlate mini-batch gradients:
\begin{align}
\text{SVRG update:}\quad \theta_{k+1} &= \theta_k - \alpha\bigl(g_k - \nabla J_j(\tilde\theta) + \nabla J(\tilde\theta)\bigr),
\end{align}
where $\tilde\theta$ is the snapshot point (updated every $m$ iterations) and $j$ is a random
sample. This estimator has $\mathbb{E}[\cdot] = \nabla J(\theta_k)$ with variance decaying to
zero as $\theta_k \to \tilde\theta \to \theta^\star$, allowing \emph{linear} convergence at
$O(\text{log}(1/\varepsilon))$ iterations for strongly convex objectives --- matching the
full-batch GD rate but using only $O(1)$ gradient evaluations per step. For Benchmark A, SVRG
would converge to the OLS minimum in $O((m + \kappa)\log(1/\varepsilon))$ total gradient
evaluations, matching full-batch GD at $m$ times lower per-iteration cost.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Local Approximations of $g(x) = x^4$}

\textbf{First-order (Taylor) approximation} around $x_0 = 0.25$:
\begin{equation}
g(x) \approx g(x_0) + g'(x_0)(x - x_0)
= (0.25)^4 + 4(0.25)^3(x - 0.25)
= 0.00391 + 0.0625(x - 0.25).
\end{equation}

\textbf{Second-order (Taylor) approximation:}
\begin{equation}
g(x) \approx g(x_0) + g'(x_0)(x - x_0) + \tfrac{1}{2}g''(x_0)(x - x_0)^2
= 0.00391 + 0.0625(x - 0.25) + \tfrac{1}{2}(12(0.25)^2)(x - 0.25)^2.
\end{equation}

The Newton update is derived by minimising the second-order model: setting the derivative of
the quadratic model to zero gives
\begin{equation}
x_{k+1} = x_k - \frac{g'(x_k)}{g''(x_k)}.
\end{equation}
This is the Newton--Raphson root-finding step applied to $g'$. When $g$ is strictly convex
($g'' > 0$), the second-order model is a valid quadratic lower bound, and minimising it
provides a better estimate of the minimum than a gradient step alone.

\subsection{Newton's Method with Hessian Regularisation}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Newton's Method with Tikhonov Regularisation (Damped Newton)}
\label{alg:newton}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha \in (0, 1]$, damping $\lambda \geq 0$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $H_k \gets \nabla^2 f(x_k) + \lambda I$ \Comment{Tikhonov-regularised Hessian, $\lambda = 10^{-8}$}
    \State $p_k \gets H_k^{-1}\, g_k$ \Comment{Newton direction (solve linear system)}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, p_k$ \Comment{Damped Newton step}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The Tikhonov term $\lambda I$ serves two purposes: it ensures numerical invertibility of $H_k$
(relevant near saddle points or inflection regions where the Hessian may be near-singular), and
it interpolates between Newton's direction ($\lambda \to 0$) and the gradient direction
($\lambda \to \infty$). The damping factor $\alpha < 1$ is required when the quadratic model
is inaccurate far from the minimum (e.g., on Rosenbrock where the Hessian changes rapidly).

\begin{remark}[Tikhonov regularisation: connection to ridge regression and trust-region methods]
The regularised Newton direction $p_k = (H_k + \lambda I)^{-1} g_k$ can be interpreted in
two ways:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Ridge regression analogy:} In the least-squares system $\min_p \frac{1}{2}\|H_k^{1/2}p - H_k^{-1/2}g_k\|^2$,
  adding $\lambda \|p\|^2$ gives the regularised solution $(H_k + \lambda I)^{-1}g_k$. The
  parameter $\lambda$ penalises large steps, analogous to the ridge regression penalty preventing
  overfitting to a poorly-conditioned design matrix.
\item \textbf{Trust-region interpretation:} The solution $p_k = \arg\min_p \{g_k^Tp + \frac{1}{2}p^TH_kp : \|p\| \leq \Delta\}$
  for a trust-region radius $\Delta$ also takes the form $(H_k + \lambda I)^{-1}g_k$ for some
  $\lambda \geq 0$ (the Lagrange multiplier of the constraint). Increasing $\lambda$ corresponds
  to shrinking $\Delta$: the step is restricted to a ball of radius $\|p_k\|$. This is the
  basis of the \emph{Levenberg--Marquardt algorithm} for nonlinear least-squares, which adapts
  $\lambda$ based on whether the quadratic model provides a good prediction of the actual
  function decrease.
\end{enumerate}
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Quadratic convergence of Newton's method]
Let $f$ be twice continuously differentiable with $\nabla^2 f$ Lipschitz continuous (constant
$M$) and $\nabla^2 f(x^\star) \succ 0$ at the local minimum $x^\star$.
Then for $\|x_0 - x^\star\|$ sufficiently small and $\alpha = 1$ (full Newton step):
\begin{equation}
\|x_{k+1} - x^\star\| \leq \frac{M}{2\mu}\|x_k - x^\star\|^2,
\end{equation}
where $\mu = \lambda_{\min}(\nabla^2 f(x^\star))$. This \emph{quadratic convergence} means
the number of correct decimal digits roughly doubles each iteration.
\end{theorem}

For a pure quadratic $f(\theta) = \frac{1}{2}\theta^T H \theta - b^T \theta$, $\nabla^2 f = H$
is constant and the Newton step is exact: $\theta_{k+1} = \theta_k - H^{-1}(H\theta_k - b) = H^{-1}b = \theta^\star$,
converging in \emph{one step} regardless of conditioning.

\begin{remark}[Two phases of Newton convergence: damped and pure]
For nonlinear objectives, Newton's method has two distinct phases:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Damped phase} (far from optimum): the quadratic model is inaccurate; full Newton
  steps overshoot. Damping $\alpha < 1$ is required. Progress is at most linear, similar to
  gradient descent, but with much better direction quality.
\item \textbf{Pure phase} (near optimum, $\|x_k - x^\star\| < \delta$): the quadratic model
  is accurate; quadratic convergence kicks in. Once in this phase, convergence is so fast that
  only $O(\log\log(1/\varepsilon))$ iterations are needed.
\end{enumerate}
In our Benchmark C experiment, the 20 iterations remain in the damped phase: $x_{20} = (0.183, 0.020)$,
still far from $(1,1)$. The transition to pure quadratic convergence requires $\|x_k - x^\star\|$
to be of order $O(\mu/M)$ where $M$ is the Lipschitz constant of the Hessian. For Rosenbrock,
$M$ is large (the Hessian changes rapidly), requiring a very tight neighbourhood for quadratic
convergence to begin.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Newton's method on Benchmark B: explicit iteration analysis]
For Benchmark B, $f(x) = (x_1-1)^2 + 5(x_2-2)^2 + \sin(x_1)$, the Newton update from
$x_0 = (-1, 4)$ is:
\begin{align}
\nabla f(x_0) &= \bigl[2(-1-1) + \cos(-1),\; 10(4-2)\bigr]^T
= [-4 + 0.540,\; 20]^T = [-3.460,\; 20]^T, \\
H(x_0) &= \operatorname{diag}(2 - \sin(-1),\; 10) = \operatorname{diag}(2.841,\; 10), \\
x_1 &= x_0 - H(x_0)^{-1}\nabla f(x_0)
= \begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ 4\end{bmatrix}
- \begin{bmatrix}1/2.841 & 0 \\ 0 & 1/10\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}-3.460 \\ 20\end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix}-1 + 1.218 \\ 4 - 2\end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix}0.218 \\ 2.0\end{bmatrix}.
\end{align}
In a single step, Newton moves $x_2$ exactly to its optimal value $2.0$
(the curvature in $x_2$ is constant, so the quadratic model is exact) and brings $x_1$
from $-1$ to $0.218$, already much closer to the true $x_1^\star = 0.582$. Subsequent
iterations refine $x_1$ quadratically since $|\sin(x_1)|$ deviates from its linear
approximation by $O((x_1 - x_{1,k})^2)$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Computational Cost and Quasi-Newton Methods}

Newton's method requires: (a) evaluating $\nabla^2 f(x_k)$, which costs $O(d^2)$ for $d$-dimensional
problems; and (b) solving the linear system $H_k p_k = g_k$, which costs $O(d^3)$ by Gaussian
elimination. For $d = 2$ (our benchmarks), this is trivial, but for large-scale problems
(e.g., neural networks with $d \sim 10^6$) it is infeasible.

\begin{remark}[Newton's method and interior-point methods for constrained optimisation]
For constrained problems (Q5, Q6), Newton's method on the unconstrained problem cannot be
directly applied. However, it forms the basis of \emph{interior-point (barrier) methods}:
the constraint $x_1 \geq 0.5$ is encoded via a logarithmic barrier $-t \ln(x_1 - 0.5)$, and
Newton's method is applied to the unconstrained problem:
\begin{equation}
\min_{x} f(x) - t\ln(x_1 - 0.5), \quad t > 0.
\end{equation}
As $t \to 0$, the barrier becomes infinitely steep at the constraint boundary, and the
Newton solution of the barrier problem converges to the constrained optimum. Each Newton
step on the barrier problem is $O(d^3)$, making interior-point methods polynomially
efficient for convex programs. Interior-point methods (IPMs) are the standard approach for
semidefinite programs, second-order cone programs, and linear programs of moderate size.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Conjugate gradient: an efficient first-order method for quadratics]
A notable omission from the assignment's method roster is the \emph{conjugate gradient (CG)}
method. For the quadratic Benchmark A, CG finds the exact solution in exactly $d = 2$ iterations,
regardless of $\kappa$. The key insight is that CG generates a sequence of mutually conjugate
search directions $\{p_k\}$ satisfying $p_k^T H p_j = 0$ for $k \neq j$, ensuring each step
makes maximum progress in the subspace already searched. The $k$-th iterate minimises $J$
over the Krylov subspace $\mathcal{K}_k = \text{span}(g_0, Hg_0, \ldots, H^{k-1}g_0)$.
For non-quadratic problems, the \emph{nonlinear CG} (Fletcher--Reeves, Polak--Ribi\`ere)
uses the same idea with gradient difference updates in place of the exact $H$ action, typically
outperforming GD at the same cost. CG is the standard linear solver for large sparse systems
(e.g., arising from discretised PDEs) due to its $O(d)$ memory and $O(d)$ per-iteration cost.
\end{remark}

\textbf{Quasi-Newton methods} (e.g., BFGS, L-BFGS) build an approximation $B_k \approx \nabla^2 f(x_k)$
from gradient difference information. The key idea is the \emph{secant condition}: if the
Hessian were constant at $H$, then $H s_k = y_k$ where $s_k = x_{k+1} - x_k$ and
$y_k = \nabla f(x_{k+1}) - \nabla f(x_k)$. Among all symmetric positive-definite matrices
satisfying $B_{k+1} s_k = y_k$, the BFGS update selects the one closest to $B_k$ in the
weighted Frobenius norm, yielding:
\begin{equation}
s_k = x_{k+1} - x_k, \quad y_k = \nabla f(x_{k+1}) - \nabla f(x_k), \quad
B_{k+1} = B_k + \frac{y_k y_k^T}{y_k^T s_k} - \frac{B_k s_k s_k^T B_k}{s_k^T B_k s_k}.
\end{equation}
The positive-definiteness of $B_{k+1}$ is maintained provided $y_k^T s_k > 0$ (which holds
when $f$ is strongly convex or when a Wolfe-condition line search is used). The BFGS inverse
update avoids explicit matrix inversion: defining $H_k = B_k^{-1}$,
\begin{equation}
H_{k+1} = \left(I - \frac{s_k y_k^T}{y_k^T s_k}\right) H_k \left(I - \frac{y_k s_k^T}{y_k^T s_k}\right)
+ \frac{s_k s_k^T}{y_k^T s_k},
\end{equation}
enabling direct computation of the Newton direction $p_k = H_k g_k$ in $O(d^2)$ operations.
BFGS achieves superlinear (but not quadratic) convergence at $O(d^2)$ cost per iteration
--- a practical middle ground between GD ($O(d)$) and Newton ($O(d^3)$). L-BFGS stores only
the $m_{\text{hist}}$ most recent $(s_k, y_k)$ pairs, reducing memory to $O(m_{\text{hist}} d)$
and making it practical for very large $d$, where $m_{\text{hist}} \in [5, 20]$ typically
suffices.

\subsection{Part A: Finite Difference Gradient Approximation (Benchmark B)}

\subsubsection{Theory}

The forward finite-difference approximation of partial derivatives is:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x) \approx \frac{f(x + \delta e_i) - f(x)}{\delta},
\end{equation}
where $e_i$ is the $i$-th standard basis vector. By Taylor's theorem, the truncation error is:
\begin{equation}
\left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x) - \frac{f(x + \delta e_i) - f(x)}{\delta}\right|
= \frac{\delta}{2}\left|\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i^2}(\xi)\right| + O(\delta^2),
\end{equation}
for some $\xi$ between $x$ and $x + \delta e_i$. This $O(\delta)$ truncation error is balanced
against the $O(\varepsilon_{\text{mach}}/\delta)$ floating-point cancellation error, yielding
optimal $\delta^\star \approx \sqrt{\varepsilon_{\text{mach}}} \approx 1.5 \times 10^{-8}$.
Central differences $[f(x + \delta e_i) - f(x - \delta e_i)]/(2\delta)$ achieve $O(\delta^2)$
truncation error, allowing a larger optimal $\delta \approx \varepsilon_{\text{mach}}^{1/3}$.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth]{figures/q4_fd_error_analysis.pdf}
\caption{Finite difference gradient approximation error vs.\ step size $\delta$ for Benchmark B
at $x = (0, 3)$. Forward differences (blue) show the typical V-shaped error curve:
for small $\delta$, floating-point cancellation dominates ($\propto \varepsilon_{\text{mach}}/\delta$);
for large $\delta$, truncation error dominates ($\propto \delta \cdot H_{ii}/2$). The minimum

The vertical lines at $\delta = 0.05$ (good) and $\delta = 0.8$ (poor) used in Q4 experiments
confirm that $\delta = 0.05$ sits well within the low-error plateau, while $\delta = 0.8$

\label{fig:q4_fd_error}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Nesterov Random Search}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Nesterov Random Search (Zeroth-Order)}
\label{alg:nrs}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha > 0$, perturbation $\delta > 0$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State Sample $u_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)$; normalise $u_k \gets u_k / \|u_k\|$
    \State $\hat{g}_k \gets \dfrac{f(x_k + \delta u_k) - f(x_k)}{\delta}\, u_k$
    \Comment{Directional derivative estimate along $u_k$}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \hat{g}_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{remark}[Choosing $\delta$ for finite differences in practice]
The forward-difference approximation error has two competing terms:
\begin{equation}
\text{Total error} \approx \underbrace{\frac{\delta}{2}|f''(x)|}_{\text{truncation}} + \underbrace{\frac{2\varepsilon_\text{mach}|f(x)|}{\delta}}_{\text{cancellation}}.
\end{equation}
Minimising over $\delta$ gives $\delta^\star = 2\sqrt{\varepsilon_\text{mach}|f(x)|/|f''(x)|}$.
For $f(x) \approx 1$ and $|f''| \approx 3$ (Benchmark B near the minimum), $\delta^\star \approx 0.015$.
The code uses $\delta = 0.05$, which is within the low-error plateau (factor $\approx 3$ above
optimal) --- small enough for accurate gradient approximation. The ``poor'' setting $\delta = 0.8$
gives truncation error $\approx 0.4 \times 3 = 1.2$, exceeding the gradient magnitude near
the minimum ($\|\nabla f\| \approx 0.1$ at the starting point, much less near the optimum),
which explains why FD GD with $\delta = 0.8$ takes a dramatically suboptimal trajectory.
\end{remark}

This zeroth-order method uses only two function evaluations per iteration, regardless of
dimension. The estimator $\hat{g}_k$ is an unbiased estimator of a smoothed gradient:
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}_u[\hat{g}_k] = \nabla f_\delta(x_k), \quad
\text{where}\quad
f_\delta(x) = \mathbb{E}_{u \sim \mathcal{U}(S^{d-1})}[f(x + \delta u)]
\end{equation}
is the spherical average of $f$ over the sphere of radius $\delta$. For smooth $f$,
$\|\nabla f_\delta(x) - \nabla f(x)\| = O(\delta^2)$, so the bias is small. However,
the variance of the estimator is $\operatorname{Var}[\hat{g}_k] = O(d) \cdot \|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2/d = O(\|\nabla f\|^2)$,
meaning the estimator has constant signal-to-noise ratio in $d$, but the expected number of
iterations to achieve accuracy $\varepsilon$ scales as $O(d/\varepsilon)$ compared to $O(1/\varepsilon)$
for exact gradient descent. This $d$-factor is the fundamental curse of dimensionality
for derivative-free methods.

\begin{remark}[Oracle cost comparison: finite differences vs.\ random search across dimensions]
The per-iteration cost in function evaluations determines the practical crossover between
finite differences (FD) and random search:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{FD gradient:} requires $d + 1$ function evaluations per iteration (one for
  each coordinate plus the base point). Convergence rate matches exact gradient descent.
  Total cost to $\varepsilon$-accuracy: $O((d+1)\kappa\log(1/\varepsilon))$ FE.
\item \textbf{Nesterov random search:} requires 2 function evaluations per iteration
  (base point $+$ one perturbation direction), but the convergence rate degrades by a
  factor of $d$ due to single-direction estimation. Total cost to $\varepsilon$-accuracy:
  $O(2d/\varepsilon)$ FE for convex functions, $O(2d\kappa/\varepsilon)$ for strongly convex.
\end{itemize}
For our $d = 2$ benchmark: FD requires $3$ FE/iter vs.\ $2$ for random search --- negligible
difference. For $d = 1000$ (e.g., a neural network with 1000 parameters): FD requires
$1001$ FE/iter vs.\ $2$ for random search. However, FD achieves $\varepsilon$-accuracy in
$O(\kappa\log(1/\varepsilon))$ iterations while random search requires $O(d\kappa/\varepsilon)$
iterations. The crossover where random search requires fewer total FE than FD occurs at
$d \leq 2\kappa\log(1/\varepsilon) / \varepsilon$ --- typically only for very large $d$ and
moderate accuracy requirements. For high-dimensional smooth problems, \emph{automatic
differentiation} (autodiff) obtains the exact gradient at the cost of a single forward pass
(i.e., FE cost $\approx 1$), making both FD and random search obsolete when the function
is implemented in a differentiable framework.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Part B: Derivative-Free Methods on Benchmark C (Rosenbrock)}

\subsubsection{Nelder--Mead Simplex Method}

The Nelder--Mead algorithm maintains a simplex of $n + 1 = 3$ vertices in $\mathbb{R}^2$,
iteratively updating the worst vertex. Its four operations are:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Reflection} ($\alpha_r = 1$): move the worst vertex through the centroid of the
  remaining $n$ vertices to $x_r = c + \alpha_r(c - x_{\text{worst}})$.
\item \textbf{Expansion} ($\gamma = 2$): if $x_r$ is the new best, try $x_e = c + \gamma(c - x_{\text{worst}})$.
\item \textbf{Contraction} ($\rho = 0.5$): if reflection does not improve, contract either
  outside ($x_c = c + \rho(x_r - c)$) or inside ($x_c = c + \rho(x_{\text{worst}} - c)$).
\item \textbf{Shrink} ($\sigma = 0.5$): if contraction fails, contract all vertices towards
  the best: $x_i \gets x_{\text{best}} + \sigma(x_i - x_{\text{best}})$.
\end{itemize}

Settings: initial simplex step $= 0.35$ (from $x_0 = [-1, 1]^T$), 160 iterations. The
initial simplex has vertices at $(-1, 1)$, $(-0.65, 1)$, and $(-1, 1.35)$. The initial
simplex edge length is 0.35; the reflection coefficient $\alpha_r = 1$ makes reflected
vertices equidistant from the centroid as the worst vertex, neither shrinking nor expanding
the initial simplex on average — a deliberate design choice to preserve exploration in the
first phase.

\begin{remark}[Convergence properties of Nelder--Mead]
Nelder--Mead has no general convergence guarantee for $d \geq 2$: there exist pathological
cases where the simplex collapses to a non-optimal point. However, for smooth functions in
practice, it typically converges well in low dimensions ($d \leq 10$) and is widely used in
engineering (e.g., MATLAB's \texttt{fminsearch}, scipy's \texttt{minimize}). Its main
advantage over random search is that it \emph{adapts} the exploration shape to the local
geometry: a well-shaped simplex makes large reflections far from the minimum and small
contractions near it, providing an informal form of curvature adaptation without any
gradient computation.
\end{remark}

\subsubsection{Grid Search}

Grid search evaluates the objective on a uniform $55 \times 55 = 3025$ grid over the region
$x_1 \in [-2, 2]$, $x_2 \in [-1, 3]$. The resolution is $\Delta x_1 = 4/54 \approx 0.074$
and $\Delta x_2 = 4/54 \approx 0.074$. The approximation error is bounded by
$|\nabla f(x^\star)| \cdot \Delta x / 2 \approx 0$ at the minimum (gradient is zero), but the
nearest grid point to $(1,1)$ has error $O(\Delta x)$ in position.

\begin{remark}[Curse of dimensionality for grid search]
To achieve position error $\varepsilon$ in $d$ dimensions over a bounded region of volume $V^d$,
grid search requires $N = (V/\varepsilon)^d$ function evaluations. For our $d=2$ example:
$N = (4/0.074)^2 \approx 55^2 = 3025$. For $d = 10$: $N \approx 55^{10} \approx 2.5 \times 10^{17}$
evaluations --- infeasible even at $10^9$ evaluations per second for $10^8$ years.
This exponential scaling is the \emph{curse of dimensionality}: each additional dimension
multiplies the cost by $1/\varepsilon$. In contrast, gradient-based methods achieve the

Grid search should only be considered when $d \leq 5$ and the objective is cheap to evaluate.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Finite differences vs.\ automatic differentiation in practice]

differences largely obsolete for differentiable objectives in modern frameworks (PyTorch,
JAX), though FD remains essential for (a) gradient checking/debugging of AD implementations,
(b) hyperparameter optimisation where $f$ is a full training run, and (c) physics
simulations where only function evaluations are available.

\subsection{Problem Formulation}

We minimise Benchmark B subject to the linear constraint $x_1 \geq 0.5$:
\begin{equation}
\min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^2}\; f(x_1, x_2) = (x_1-1)^2 + 5(x_2-2)^2 + \sin(x_1),
\quad \text{s.t.}\quad g(x) = 0.5 - x_1 \leq 0.
\end{equation}
The feasible set is $\mathcal{X} = \{x : x_1 \geq 0.5\}$. The unconstrained minimiser is at
$(0.582, 2.0)$, which satisfies $x_1 = 0.582 > 0.5$, so the \emph{constrained} minimum is also
at $(0.582, 2.0)$ with $f^\star = 0.7244$. The constraint is \emph{inactive} at the optimum,
which means any method that eventually reaches feasibility will converge to the same point.
Starting point: $x_0 = (0.2, 4.0)$, which is \emph{infeasible} ($x_1 = 0.2 < 0.5$).

\textbf{KKT conditions:} The Lagrangian is
$\mathcal{L}(x, \mu) = f(x) + \mu(0.5 - x_1)$ with dual variable $\mu \geq 0$.
At the optimal point $(0.582, 2.0)$:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \emph{Stationarity}: $\nabla_x \mathcal{L} = \nabla f(x^\star) - \mu e_1 = 0$.
  Since the constraint is inactive at $x_1^\star = 0.582 > 0.5$, complementary slackness
  requires $\mu = 0$, and thus $\nabla f(x^\star) = 0$ --- the unconstrained optimality condition.
\item \emph{Primal feasibility}: $0.5 - 0.582 = -0.082 \leq 0$ (\checkmark).
\item \emph{Dual feasibility}: $\mu = 0 \geq 0$ (\checkmark).
\item \emph{Complementary slackness}: $\mu \cdot (0.5 - x_1^\star) = 0 \cdot (-0.082) = 0$ (\checkmark).
\end{itemize}
All KKT conditions are satisfied with $\mu = 0$, confirming $(0.582, 2.0)$ is the
constrained optimum. The inactive constraint is equivalent to no constraint at this point.

\subsection{Algorithm Formulations}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Projected Gradient Descent (PGD)}
\label{alg:pgd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Infeasible start $x_0$, step size $\alpha > 0$, projection $\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \nabla f(x_k)$
    \Comment{Unconstrained gradient step}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets \Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(z_{k+1}) = ({\max}(0.5, z_{k+1}^{(1)}),\; z_{k+1}^{(2)})$
    \Comment{Project: clip $x_1$ to $[0.5, \infty)$}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}[Convergence of projected gradient descent]
For a convex, $L$-smooth objective $f$ and convex feasible set $\mathcal{X}$, PGD with
step size $\alpha \leq 1/L$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
\min_{k \leq T} \|\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_k - \alpha \nabla f(x_k)) - x_k\| = O(1/\sqrt{T}),
\end{equation}
which is the same $O(1/\sqrt{T})$ convergence rate as unconstrained gradient descent on
smooth convex functions.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}[Key steps of the PGD convergence proof]
The proof uses the \emph{descent lemma} (for $L$-smooth $f$):
$f(x_{k+1}) \leq f(x_k) + \nabla f(x_k)^T(x_{k+1} - x_k) + \frac{L}{2}\|x_{k+1} - x_k\|^2$.
Combined with the projection property $\|\Pi_\mathcal{X}(z) - x^\star\| \leq \|z - x^\star\|$
and the optimality condition $\nabla f(x_k)^T(x_k - x^\star) \geq 0$, one obtains
$f(x_{k+1}) - f^\star \leq (1/(2\alpha))(\|x_k - x^\star\|^2 - \|x_{k+1} - x^\star\|^2)$.
Summing over $k$ and using convexity gives $\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}(f(x_k) - f^\star) \leq \|x_0 - x^\star\|^2/(2\alpha)$,
so the average satisfies $\bar{f}_T - f^\star = O(1/T)$ (equivalently, $O(1/\sqrt{T})$
for the gradient stationarity measure $\min_k\|\Pi_\mathcal{X}(x_k - \alpha g_k) - x_k\|$).
\end{remark}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Penalty Method (Quadratic Penalty)}
\label{alg:penalty}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Infeasible start $x_0$, step size $\alpha > 0$, penalty weight $\lambda > 0$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $F_{\lambda}(x) \gets f(x) + \lambda \max(0,\, -x_1 + 0.5)$
    \Comment{Penalised objective}
    \State $\nabla F_\lambda(x) \gets \nabla f(x) - \lambda\, \mathbf{1}[x_1 < 0.5]\, e_1$
    \Comment{Subgradient of penalty term}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \nabla F_{\lambda}(x_k)$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{remark}[Subgradient method for non-smooth penalties]
The $\ell_1$ penalty term $\max(0, 0.5 - x_1)$ is non-smooth at $x_1 = 0.5$ (the constraint
boundary). The update uses a \emph{subgradient}: at $x_1 < 0.5$, $\partial_{x_1} F_\lambda = -\lambda$;
at $x_1 > 0.5$, $\partial_{x_1} F_\lambda = 0$; at $x_1 = 0.5$, the subdifferential is
$[-\lambda, 0]$ (any value in this interval is a valid subgradient). In the algorithm, we use
$\partial_{x_1} F_\lambda = -\lambda \cdot \mathbf{1}[x_1 < 0.5]$, which corresponds to the
right subgradient (zero at the boundary). The \emph{subgradient method} with step
$\alpha_k \propto 1/\sqrt{k}$ converges at $O(1/\sqrt{k})$ for convex non-smooth objectives,
the same rate as projected gradient descent. Unlike gradient descent for smooth functions,
the subgradient method does not guarantee monotone descent --- $F_\lambda(x_{k+1})$ may
exceed $F_\lambda(x_k)$ even when the step is well-chosen.
\end{remark}

The linear penalty $\lambda \max(0, 0.5 - x_1)$ is a specific instance of the $\ell_1$ exact
penalty function. The penalised problem converges to the constrained optimum as $\lambda \to \infty$.
For finite $\lambda$, the minimiser of $F_\lambda$ satisfies the first-order condition
$\nabla f(x^\star_\lambda) = \lambda\, \mathbf{1}[x_1 < 0.5]\, e_1$, yielding a constraint
violation of $O(1/\lambda)$ for typical smooth problems.

\begin{theorem}[$\ell_1$ exact penalty theorem]
Let $x^\star$ satisfy the KKT conditions for the constrained problem with dual variable $\mu^\star \geq 0$.
For the $\ell_1$ penalty $F_\lambda(x) = f(x) + \lambda \max(0, 0.5 - x_1)$, if
$\lambda > \mu^\star$, then any local minimiser of $F_\lambda$ that is feasible is also a local
minimiser of the original constrained problem. In our Q5 problem, $\mu^\star = 0$ (inactive
constraint), so for \emph{any} $\lambda > 0$, the unconstrained minimum $(0.582, 2.0)$ is also
a minimiser of $F_\lambda$ once the iterate reaches feasibility. This is why even $\lambda = 0.15$
converges to the correct solution: the penalty threshold $\lambda > \mu^\star = 0$ is satisfied
for all positive $\lambda$.
\end{theorem}

share the same minimiser. In a problem where the unconstrained minimum violates the constraint
(like the boundary optimum in Q6 where $(0,0) \notin \mathcal{X}$), penalty methods only
achieve feasibility for sufficiently large $\lambda$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[PGD as proximal gradient descent]
Projected gradient descent can be interpreted as a \emph{proximal gradient} step with the
indicator function $\iota_\mathcal{X}(x) = 0$ if $x \in \mathcal{X}$, $+\infty$ otherwise:
\begin{equation}
x_{k+1} = \operatorname{prox}_{\alpha \iota_\mathcal{X}}(x_k - \alpha \nabla f(x_k))
= \arg\min_{z} \left\{ \iota_\mathcal{X}(z) + \frac{1}{2\alpha}\|z - (x_k - \alpha\nabla f(x_k))\|^2 \right\}
= \Pi_\mathcal{X}(x_k - \alpha \nabla f(x_k)).
\end{equation}
The proximal operator of the indicator function is exactly the Euclidean projection.
This connection shows that PGD is the natural extension of GD to composite objectives
$f(x) + g(x)$ where $g$ is non-smooth but proximable, and suggests a family of
generalisations (proximal methods, ADMM, proximal-point algorithms) that handle more
complex constraint structures within the same theoretical framework.
\end{remark}

\textbf{Projected gradient descent} enforces feasibility as a hard constraint from the very
first iteration. This is the fundamental advantage: no matter how large the gradient step,
the iterate is always feasible. The cost is requiring a \emph{projection oracle} $\Pi_\mathcal{X}$,
which is trivial for simple constraints like bounds ($O(d)$ operations) but can be expensive for
general convex sets (e.g., projection onto the positive semidefinite cone requires an
eigendecomposition). PGD also has the advantage of operating directly on the constrained
problem, inheriting the same convergence rate as unconstrained gradient descent.

\textbf{Penalty methods} require only a modified scalar objective (no special projection
machinery). The weight $\lambda$ controls the trade-off: small $\lambda$ under-penalises
violations; large $\lambda$ makes the penalised objective poorly conditioned (Lipschitz
constant of $F_\lambda$ is $L + \lambda$ for the linear penalty, requiring step sizes of
$O(1/(L+\lambda))$ for stability). The exact $\ell_1$ penalty used here can achieve exact
feasibility for finite $\lambda$ when the constraint qualification holds, unlike quadratic
penalties $\lambda \|Ax - b\|^2/2$ which only achieve feasibility as $\lambda \to \infty$.

\textbf{Augmented Lagrangian} (method of multipliers) offers a principled improvement over
penalty methods by combining a quadratic penalty with a dual variable update:
\begin{equation}
L_\rho(x, \mu) = f(x) + \mu^T c(x) + \frac{\rho}{2}\|c(x)\|^2,
\end{equation}
where $c(x) = \max(0, 0.5 - x_1)$ is the constraint function. The dual variable $\mu$ is
updated after each primal minimisation step: $\mu \gets \mu + \rho\, c(x)$. This achieves
exact feasibility for finite $\rho$ even in problems where the plain penalty method requires
$\rho \to \infty$, making it more numerically stable than pure penalty methods for
ill-conditioned constraints.

\begin{remark}[Strong duality and the penalty interpretation]
For the Q5 problem, the Lagrangian dual is $d(\mu) = \min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^2} \mathcal{L}(x, \mu)
= \min_x \{f(x) + \mu(0.5 - x_1)\}$. Since $f$ is strongly convex and the constraint is linear
(Slater's condition holds: $(1, 2) \in \mathcal{X}$ is strictly feasible), strong duality holds:
$d^\star = \min_{\mu \geq 0} d(\mu) = f^\star = 0.7244$. The dual optimal $\mu^\star = 0$
(inactive constraint) means the penalty method needs only infinitesimally small $\lambda > 0$
to recover the primal optimal. This explains why even $\lambda = 0.15$ eventually converges
to the correct solution: the penalty does not need to ``fight'' a hard constraint force at the
optimum. In problems where $\mu^\star > 0$ (active constraint), the required penalty weight
$\lambda$ must be at least $\mu^\star$ for the penalised and constrained optima to coincide.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Feasible Set and Linear Programme}

The feasible set is the axis-aligned box:
$\mathcal{X} = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^2 : 0.5 \leq x_1 \leq 5,\ -5 \leq x_2 \leq 10\}$.

\textbf{Linear Programme (LP):} Minimise $f(x) = a^T x = x_1 + 2x_2$ over $\mathcal{X}$,
where $a = [1, 2]^T$.

\begin{proposition}[LP solution]
The minimum of a linear objective over a bounded convex polytope is attained at a vertex.
For the box $\mathcal{X}$, since $a_1 = 1 > 0$ and $a_2 = 2 > 0$, the minimum is at
the vertex where both $x_1$ and $x_2$ take their minimum values:
\begin{equation}
x^{\star} = (0.5,\, -5), \qquad f^{\star} = 0.5 + 2(-5) = -9.5.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

This illustrates the fundamental theorem of linear programming: for any linear objective,
the optimal solution is always attained at a vertex (extreme point) of the feasible polytope.

\textbf{KKT verification:} For the LP $\min a^T x$ s.t. $l \leq x \leq u$, the KKT
conditions at $x^\star = (l_1, l_2) = (0.5, -5)$ are:
$a = \mu^- - \mu^+$ with $\mu^- \geq 0$ (active lower bound duals),
$\mu^+ \geq 0$ (active upper bound duals). Since $a_1 = 1 > 0$ and $a_2 = 2 > 0$,
both lower bounds are active at the optimum, consistent with $\mu^- = a = [1, 2]^T > 0$,
confirming the vertex $(0.5, -5)$ is the unique global optimum.

\subsection{Frank--Wolfe Algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Frank--Wolfe (Conditional Gradient) Algorithm}
\label{alg:fw}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial feasible $x_0 \in \mathcal{X}$, convex combination weight $\beta \in (0,1)$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_k \gets \arg\min_{x \in \mathcal{X}}\, \nabla f(x_k)^T x$
    \Comment{Linear minimisation oracle (LMO)}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets \beta\, x_k + (1 - \beta)\, z_k$
    \Comment{Convex combination: $x_{k+1} \in \mathcal{X}$ by convexity}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{proposition}[Frank--Wolfe feasibility and sparsity]
Since $\mathcal{X}$ is convex and both $x_k \in \mathcal{X}$ and $z_k \in \mathcal{X}$ (the
LMO minimises over $\mathcal{X}$), every iterate $x_{k+1} = \beta x_k + (1-\beta) z_k$ is a
convex combination of feasible points and thus feasible. Frank--Wolfe never requires an
explicit projection oracle.

Moreover, by \emph{Carath\'eodory's theorem}, every point in a convex set in $\mathbb{R}^d$
can be expressed as a convex combination of at most $d+1$ extreme points. Since FW builds
$x_k$ as a convex combination of iteratively added LMO vertices, after $k$ iterations the
iterate is a convex combination of at most $\min(k, d+1)$ vertices. For $\mathcal{X}$ a
polytope, this gives \emph{sparse representations}: $x_k$ is a convex combination of at
most $d+1 = 3$ vertices of the box. This structural sparsity is useful when $\mathcal{X}$ is
a simplex or $\ell_1$-ball, where sparse iterates correspond to sparse solutions (e.g., in
Lasso regression).
\end{proposition}

For box constraints $\mathcal{X} = [l, u]^d$, the LMO decomposes per coordinate:
\begin{equation}
(z_k)_i = \begin{cases} l_i & \text{if } (\nabla f(x_k))_i > 0 \\ u_i & \text{if } (\nabla f(x_k))_i \leq 0 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
requiring only $O(d)$ operations.

\begin{remark}[Frank--Wolfe vs.\ coordinate descent on box constraints]
For box-constrained problems, an alternative to Frank--Wolfe is \emph{coordinate descent}:
at each step, minimise $f$ over one coordinate while holding the others fixed. For the
linear objective (LP case), coordinate descent is equivalent to the LMO per coordinate,
but for non-linear objectives, coordinate descent performs an exact line search along one
dimension at each step, potentially converging faster. For the box constraint $\mathcal{X}$
and smooth $f$:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{FW:} Moves towards the LMO vertex (a corner of $\mathcal{X}$), maintaining
  feasibility; natural for structured constraint sets.
\item \textbf{Coordinate descent:} Takes a step within $[l_i, u_i]$ for one coordinate;
  efficient for separable objectives or diagonal Hessians; achieves $O(\kappa/\varepsilon)$
  iterations for strongly convex objectives.
\end{itemize}
Coordinate descent is commonly used for Lasso regression (ISTA/ADMM), SVM training (SMO),
and mixed-integer programming relaxations.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Frank--Wolfe convergence rate \cite{frank1956algorithm}]
For a convex, $L$-smooth objective $f$ over a compact convex set $\mathcal{X}$ with
diameter $D = \max_{x,y \in \mathcal{X}}\|x - y\|$, the Frank--Wolfe algorithm with
the \emph{optimal decaying} step size $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$ (i.e., $1 - \beta_k = 2/(k+2)$)
satisfies:
\begin{equation}
f(x_k) - f^\star \leq \frac{2LD^2}{k+2} = O\!\left(\frac{1}{k}\right).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

For our box $\mathcal{X}$, the diameter is
$D = \|(5, 10) - (0.5, -5)\| = \|(4.5, 15)\| = \sqrt{4.5^2 + 15^2} \approx 15.66$.
The Lipschitz constant of the gradient for the interior objective is $L = 2$ (constant Hessian
$2I$), giving a convergence bound $2 \cdot 2 \cdot 15.66^2 / (k+2) \approx 980 / k$.

\begin{remark}[Fixed $\beta$ vs decaying $\gamma_k$]
The implementation uses a fixed convex combination parameter $\beta$ (i.e., $\gamma = 1 - \beta$ is
constant), rather than the optimal decaying schedule $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$. A fixed $\gamma$
(hence fixed $\beta$) does not converge to zero, causing the iterates to continue taking finite
steps towards LP vertices even after approaching the minimum. This explains why the boundary
optimum case (where convergence to the boundary requires $\gamma \to 0$ eventually) settles
at $f = 0.364 > f^\star = 0.25$: the iterate cannot stop ``wiggling'' between vertices as
$\gamma = 1 - 0.93 = 0.07$ is always nonzero.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Interior vs.\ Boundary Behaviour: Analysis}

\textbf{Interior optimum case:} Frank--Wolfe converges to the correct minimum but exhibits
the characteristic zig-zag oscillation. Near the interior optimum, the gradient $\nabla f(x_k)$
is small but nonzero; the LMO nonetheless selects a vertex of $\mathcal{X}$ (far from $x_k$),
creating a large directional jump $z_k - x_k$ that is down-weighted by $(1-\beta)$ in the
convex combination. As $\beta \to 1$, this down-weighting becomes more aggressive, reducing
oscillation at the cost of slower progress. The $O(1/k)$ convergence rate implies that
achieving precision $\varepsilon$ requires $O(1/\varepsilon)$ iterations --- much slower than
gradient descent's $O(\log(1/\varepsilon))$ rate on strongly convex problems.

\textbf{Boundary optimum case:} Frank--Wolfe is well-suited to boundary optima because the
LMO vertex $z_k$ frequently selects the boundary-closest vertex (here $x_1 = 0.5$), naturally
driving the iterate to the boundary without projection. The fixed $\beta = 0.93$ schedule
yields a residual gap of $0.114$ after 140 iterations because the step size does not decay to
zero. Using the standard decaying schedule $1 - \gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$ (equivalently
$\beta_k = k/(k+2)$) would ensure convergence to the constrained optimum.

\begin{proposition}[Fixed $\beta$ does not converge to the boundary optimum]
Let $f$ be $L$-smooth on a convex compact set $\mathcal{X}$ with diameter $D$, and let
$x^\star$ be on the boundary of $\mathcal{X}$. With fixed Frank--Wolfe step $1 - \beta = \gamma > 0$,
the iterates satisfy
\begin{equation}
f(x_k) - f^\star \leq \frac{LD^2 \gamma}{2} + \frac{(f(x_0) - f^\star)(1-\gamma)^k}{\text{decreasing}}.
\end{equation}
As $k \to \infty$, the second term vanishes but the first term $LD^2\gamma/2 > 0$ persists,
giving a non-zero asymptotic gap proportional to $\gamma$. For our boundary case:
$L = 2$, $D \approx 15.66$, $\gamma = 0.07$, yielding predicted residual
$\approx 2 \cdot 245 \cdot 0.07 / 2 \approx 17.2$ (loose bound; tighter than observed $0.114$
because the bound uses the worst-case diameter). In contrast, with $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2) \to 0$,
the first term also vanishes and the method converges.
\end{proposition}

\textbf{Comparison with Projected Gradient Descent:} The key advantage of Frank--Wolfe is that
it avoids computing projections, which can be expensive. Its disadvantage is the $O(1/k)$
convergence rate versus the $O(1/k^2)$ rate of Nesterov-accelerated projected gradient descent
on smooth strongly convex objectives. Frank--Wolfe excels in settings where the LMO is
computationally cheap (e.g., box constraints, simplex, nuclear norm ball) and where iterates
being convex combinations of extreme points has structural advantages (e.g., sparse solutions
when $\mathcal{X}$ is a polytope).

\textbf{Frank--Wolfe gap:} A useful primal certificate of optimality is the
\emph{Frank--Wolfe gap} (or duality gap):
\begin{equation}
g_k = \nabla f(x_k)^T(x_k - z_k),
\end{equation}
where $z_k$ is the LMO solution. Since $z_k = \arg\min_{z \in \mathcal{X}} \nabla f(x_k)^T z$,
the first-order optimality condition gives $f(x_k) - f^\star \leq g_k$ for convex $f$.
Thus $g_k$ provides an upper bound on the optimality gap without knowing $f^\star$, and
can be used as a stopping criterion. \cref{fig:q6_fw_gap} shows this bound empirically.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth]{figures/q6_fw_gap.pdf}
\caption{Frank--Wolfe gap analysis for the interior optimum problem. Left: objective value and
FW gap on the same plot. Right: FW gap vs.\ optimality gap $f(x_k) - f^\star$, confirming

is bounded and moderate, making it a practical convergence criterion.}
\label{fig:q6_fw_gap}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Practical applications of Frank--Wolfe:} FW is particularly popular in machine learning
for optimising over structured constraint sets: (1) the $\ell_1$ ball $\|x\|_1 \leq 1$ (used in
Lasso and compressed sensing --- the LMO selects a coordinate $\pm e_i$); (2) the nuclear
norm ball $\|X\|_* \leq 1$ (used in matrix completion --- the LMO computes the leading singular
vector); and (3) the flow polytope (used in traffic/routing problems). In all these cases,
the projection is expensive but the LMO has special structure enabling efficient computation.

\begin{remark}[Away-step Frank--Wolfe and linear convergence]
The standard FW algorithm (as implemented) has the $O(1/k)$ convergence bound and the
characteristic zig-zag behaviour near the interior optimum. The \emph{away-step} Frank--Wolfe
variant (FW-Away) supplements the standard LMO step with an \emph{away step}: instead of
only moving towards the LMO vertex $z_k$, the algorithm also considers moving \emph{away}
from the worst vertex in the current active set. The update becomes:
\begin{equation}
x_{k+1} = x_k + \gamma_k(d_k), \quad d_k \in \{z_k - x_k,\; x_k - v_k\},
\end{equation}
where $v_k = \arg\max_{v \in \mathcal{S}_k} \nabla f(x_k)^T v$ is the away vertex and $\mathcal{S}_k$
is the active set. FW-Away achieves \emph{linear convergence} at rate $O(\rho^k)$ for strongly
convex objectives over polytopes, matching gradient descent's convergence class. This eliminates
the zig-zag behaviour and is the preferred variant when the active set remains small and easy
to maintain.
\end{remark}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

This report has investigated six families of optimisation algorithms across three benchmark
problems of increasing difficulty. The following key conclusions emerge:

\textbf{Adaptive step-size methods} (Q1): Adagrad and Heavy Ball provide reliable improvement
over constant step-size GD without requiring problem-specific parameter tuning. Polyak's rule
is theoretically elegant but requires exact knowledge of the optimal value $f^\star$; when
this is unavailable or misspecified, it can diverge catastrophically. RMSprop's exponential
moving average addresses Adagrad's monotone decay but lacks a worst-case guarantee.

\textbf{Momentum and acceleration} (Q2): Nesterov's method achieves the theoretically optimal
$O(1/k^2)$ convergence rate for convex functions, which manifests clearly on the Rosenbrock
problem where it outperforms all Q1 methods. Adam combines momentum with per-coordinate
scaling, but its advantages are most pronounced on problems with heterogeneous feature scales
(not the case for the benchmarks here). Mini-batch SGD's convergence floor is determined by
observation noise, not optimisation quality.

\textbf{Newton's method} (Q3): Provides quadratic convergence and is exact in one step for
quadratic objectives. Its main limitations are (a) requiring the Hessian and its inverse
(cost $O(d^3)$ per step), and (b) needing damping when the quadratic model is inaccurate,
which is common on highly non-quadratic functions like Rosenbrock. Despite these limitations,
Newton outperforms all first-order methods on all three benchmarks in terms of iterations
required per unit of convergence.

\textbf{Derivative-free methods} (Q4): Finite differences with good $\delta$ closely
approximates exact gradient descent at $d+1$ function evaluations per iteration. Nesterov
random search provides dimension-independent per-step cost but converges slowly in $d$
dimensions. Nelder--Mead is practical for low-dimensional problems and serves as a reliable
derivative-free benchmark. Grid search is the only method with a global optimality guarantee
but scales exponentially with dimension.

\textbf{Constrained optimisation} (Q5): Projected gradient descent enforces hard feasibility
at every iteration and should be preferred when a projection oracle is available. Penalty
methods are simpler to implement but require tuning of $\lambda$ and may converge to
infeasible points for small penalties. For the test problem (inactive constraint at the
optimum), both methods converge to the same solution, with the penalty method's accuracy
improving with $\lambda$.

\textbf{Frank--Wolfe} (Q6): Provides automatic feasibility maintenance through convex
combinations, eliminating projection. It is most advantageous when projections are expensive
and the LMO is cheap. The $O(1/k)$ convergence rate is slower than accelerated projected
methods but sufficient in many practical applications.

\textbf{Preconditioning as an alternative to algorithm selection:} A recurring theme is that
ill-conditioning ($\kappa \gg 1$) causes poor convergence for first-order methods. An
orthogonal solution to algorithm selection is \emph{preconditioning}: transforming the problem
to reduce $\kappa$ before applying any algorithm. For Benchmark A (linear regression), the
preconditioned problem $\min_u f(P^{-1/2}u)$ with preconditioning matrix $P = X^TX/m$ has
condition number $\kappa = 1$, on which GD converges in one step (equivalent to Newton).
For Benchmark C (Rosenbrock), a coordinate transform $u = (x_1, 10x_2)$ reduces the
curvature ratio from $\approx 2500$ to $\approx 25$, making first-order methods $\sqrt{100} = 10\times$
more efficient. In practice, data normalisation (whitening/standardisation) in machine
learning serves this preconditioning role, explaining why standardised features are standard
practice in gradient-based model training.

\textbf{Overall ranking on Rosenbrock (hardest benchmark):} Polyak (with $f^\star$) $>$
Nesterov $>$ Heavy Ball $>$ Newton (damped, 80 it.) $>$ Adagrad $>$ Adam $>$ RMSprop $>$ GD.
This is visible in \cref{fig:summary_rosenbrock} and highlights that methods exploiting either
exact function value information (Polyak) or accelerated momentum (Nesterov) provide the
greatest benefit on ill-conditioned non-convex problems. Conversely, adaptive methods designed
primarily for non-stationary stochastic problems (Adam, RMSprop) show limited advantage on
smooth deterministic ill-conditioned problems.

\begin{remark}[Condition number as the central difficulty axis]
All observed performance differences can be traced to the condition number $\kappa$:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item For $\kappa \approx 1$ (Benchmark A after normalisation), constant-step GD and all
  variants converge quickly — condition number does not separate methods.
\item For $\kappa = 10$ (Benchmark B, $L = 10$, $\mu = 1$), moderate anisotropy creates mild zig-zagging;
  adaptive methods (Adagrad, Adam) help by applying per-coordinate scaling to equalize the
  effective curvature in the $x_1$ (low curvature) and $x_2$ (high curvature) directions.
\item For $\kappa \approx 2500$ (Benchmark C), only methods with momentum, curvature information
  (Newton), or exact $f^\star$ knowledge (Polyak) make meaningful progress. Pure GD requires
  $O(\kappa) = O(2500)$ times more iterations than Nesterov for the same accuracy.
\end{itemize}
The key lesson for practitioners: before choosing an optimisation algorithm, estimate the
condition number of the problem. If $\kappa \gg 1$: use momentum (Nesterov, Heavy Ball)
or curvature information (Newton, L-BFGS). If $\kappa \approx 1$ (after preconditioning):
simple GD may suffice. Preconditioning (e.g., whitening the input data) is often more
valuable than algorithm selection for ill-conditioned problems.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Oracle complexity hierarchy: a unified view]
The methods in this report span three oracle complexity classes, distinguished by the
information requested at each step:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Zeroth-order} (function values only): grid search, Nelder--Mead, Nesterov
  random search. Best iteration complexity: $O(d/\varepsilon)$ for random search on convex
  problems; $O((V/\varepsilon)^d)$ for grid search. These methods pay a dimension factor
  $d$ in iterations or an exponential factor in $d$.
\item \textbf{First-order} (gradient access): GD, Nesterov, Heavy Ball, PGD, Frank--Wolfe.
  Best iteration complexity: $O(1/\sqrt{\varepsilon})$ (Nesterov, optimal for smooth convex)
  or $O(\sqrt{\kappa}\log(1/\varepsilon))$ (Nesterov, optimal for strongly convex). Each
  iteration costs one gradient evaluation ($O(d)$ work for explicit objectives).
\item \textbf{Second-order} (Hessian access): Newton, BFGS. Best iteration complexity:
  $O(\log\log(1/\varepsilon))$ (quadratic convergence). Each iteration costs $O(d^3)$ for
  exact Newton or $O(d^2)$ for BFGS. For $d = 2$, Newton dominates; for $d \geq 10^3$,
  L-BFGS (storing $m = 10$ pairs, $O(md)$ memory) is preferred.
\end{itemize}
The transition from zeroth to first order halves the iteration complexity exponent from
$O(1/\varepsilon)$ to $O(1/\sqrt{\varepsilon})$, while the transition from first to second
order gives super-polynomial speedup ($O(\log\log(1/\varepsilon))$ vs.\ $O(\sqrt{\kappa}\log(1/\varepsilon))$).
The practical barrier for second-order methods is the $O(d^3)$ cost, which exceeds the
gradient cost $O(d)$ by a factor of $d^2$ --- for deep networks with $d = 10^8$,
second-order methods are infeasible without massive Hessian approximation.
\end{remark}

\textbf{Practical algorithm selection guide:} The choice of optimisation algorithm should be
guided by:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \emph{Are gradients available?} If no: use Nelder--Mead (low $d$), FD approximation
  (moderate $d$), or random search (high $d$, variance tolerated).
\item \emph{Are constraints present?} If yes: use PGD (projection cheap) or Frank--Wolfe
  (LMO cheap); penalty methods if the constraint structure permits.
\item \emph{Is the condition number large?} If $\kappa \gg 1$: use Nesterov, Heavy Ball, or
  Newton/L-BFGS; avoid constant-step GD.
\item \emph{Is the problem stochastic?} If yes: use Adam or SGD with momentum; Nesterov
  with variance reduction (e.g., SVRG) for finite-sum problems.
\item \emph{Is the problem low-dimensional and smooth?} If yes: Newton or L-BFGS for fastest
  convergence; BFGS provides quadratic-like convergence at $O(d^2)$ cost.
\end{enumerate}
These rules explain all the patterns observed in this assignment.

\begin{remark}[Connection to deep learning optimisation]
The algorithms studied in this assignment directly relate to modern deep learning practice:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Adam} (Q2) is the default optimiser for most deep learning frameworks; its
  success on high-dimensional non-convex objectives (neural networks) comes from the
  stochastic gradient noise that our deterministic benchmarks lack.
\item \textbf{SGD with momentum} (Heavy Ball, Nesterov, Q1--Q2) is preferred for fine-tuning
  and large-scale training where large batch sizes provide low-variance gradients.
\item \textbf{Second-order information} (Q3) enters modern training via approximations:
  L-BFGS for small networks, K-FAC (Kronecker-factored curvature approximation) for large
  networks, and batch normalisation as an implicit curvature-conditioning mechanism.
\item \textbf{Constrained optimisation} (Q5--Q6) arises in reinforcement learning (trust-region
  policy optimisation), model compression (nuclear norm constraints), and fairness-constrained
  learning (linear constraints on group-specific losses).
\item \textbf{Condition number} ($\kappa$) manifests in deep learning as the ratio of largest
  to smallest layer-wise gradient magnitudes; batch normalisation, layer normalisation, and
  residual connections all serve to reduce this effective condition number.
\end{itemize}
\end{remark}

def gradient_descent(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        x = x - alpha * grad_fn(x)
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Polyak Step Size},label={lst:polyak}]
def polyak_step(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, f_star, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        alpha_k = (loss_fn(x) - f_star) / (np.dot(g, g) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(alpha_k)
        x = x - alpha_k * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Adagrad},label={lst:adagrad}]
def adagrad(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha0, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    G = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        G += g**2
        eff_alpha = alpha0 / (np.sqrt(G) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(np.mean(eff_alpha))
        x = x - eff_alpha * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={RMSprop},label={lst:rmsprop}]
def rmsprop(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha0, beta, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    v = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        v = beta * v + (1 - beta) * g**2
        eff_alpha = alpha0 / (np.sqrt(v) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(np.mean(eff_alpha))
        x = x - eff_alpha * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Heavy Ball},label={lst:hb}]
def heavy_ball(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    z = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        z = beta * z + alpha * g
        x = x - z
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Nesterov Accelerated Gradient},label={lst:nesterov}]
def nesterov_momentum(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta_max, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    z = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for k in range(1, n_iters + 1):
        beta_k = min((k - 1) / (k + 2), beta_max)  # Increasing momentum schedule
        lookahead = x + beta_k * z
        g = grad_fn(lookahead)                       # Gradient at lookahead point
        z = beta_k * z - alpha * g
        x = x + z
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Adam Optimiser},label={lst:adam}]
def adam_optimiser(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta1, beta2, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    m_vec, v_vec = np.zeros_like(x), np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for t in range(1, n_iters + 1):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        m_vec = beta1 * m_vec + (1 - beta1) * g          # First moment (EMA)
        v_vec = beta2 * v_vec + (1 - beta2) * g**2       # Second moment (EMA)
        m_hat = m_vec / (1 - beta1**t)                    # Bias correction
        v_hat = v_vec / (1 - beta2**t)
        x = x - alpha * m_hat / (np.sqrt(v_hat) + eps)
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Newton's Method with Tikhonov Regularisation},label={lst:newton}]
def newtons_method(grad_fn, hess_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, n_iters, damping=1e-8):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, update_norms = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        H = hess_fn(x) if callable(hess_fn) else hess_fn
        H_reg = H + damping * np.eye(len(x))  # Tikhonov regularisation
        try:
            p = np.linalg.solve(H_reg, g)     # Solve H_reg * p = g
        except np.linalg.LinAlgError:
            p = g                              # Fallback to gradient step
        update_norms.append(np.linalg.norm(alpha * p))
        x = x - alpha * p
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(update_norms)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Frank--Wolfe (Conditional Gradient) Algorithm},label={lst:fw}]
def frank_wolfe(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, bounds, beta, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, z_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        # Linear minimisation oracle (LMO) for box constraints
        z = np.array([bounds[i][0] if g[i] > 0 else bounds[i][1]
                       for i in range(len(g))])
        z_hist.append(z.copy())
        x = beta * x + (1 - beta) * z     # Convex combination: maintains feasibility
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(z_hist)
\end{lstlisting}
