\begin{abstract}
This report presents a comprehensive comparative study of optimisation algorithms for data analysis, applied to three benchmark functions of increasing difficulty: a well-conditioned linear regression quadratic loss (condition number $\kappa \approx 1.12$), a mildly anisotropic toy neural network loss ($\kappa \approx 6.90$), and the highly ill-conditioned Rosenbrock function ($\kappa \approx 2508$). Six categories of methods are investigated: (Q1) adaptive step-size methods --- Polyak, Adagrad, RMSprop, and Heavy Ball; (Q2) momentum-based methods --- Nesterov acceleration and Adam --- together with mini-batch stochastic gradient descent; (Q3) Newton's method with Hessian-based quadratic approximations; (Q4) derivative-free approaches including forward and centred finite differences, Nesterov random search, Nelder--Mead simplex, and grid search; (Q5) constrained optimisation via projected gradient descent and quadratic penalty methods, analysed through the KKT framework; and (Q6) the Frank--Wolfe conditional gradient algorithm with duality gap analysis. Convergence rates are quantified analytically: gradient descent achieves $((\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1))^k$ per step; Nesterov achieves the optimal $(1-1/\sqrt{\kappa})^k$ rate for strongly convex problems ($25\times$ fewer iterations to $10^{-3}$ accuracy on Rosenbrock; $7\times$ lower final loss in 150 iterations); Newton achieves local quadratic convergence and exact one-step convergence on quadratics (Benchmark A). The Polyak step diverges when $f^{\star}$ is misspecified: $f_{\text{Polyak}} \approx 24.07$ vs $f_{\text{GD}} \approx 0.72$ on Benchmark B ($33\times$ higher), and $f_{\text{Polyak}} \approx 2.72$ vs $f_{\text{GD}} \approx 0.48$ on Benchmark A ($5.6\times$ higher). Centred finite differences achieve $O(\delta^2)$ accuracy versus $O(\delta)$ for forward differences, providing a 600-fold improvement at $\delta = 0.05$. Projected gradient descent enforces hard feasibility from the first iteration, while penalty methods with insufficient weight fail to fully enforce constraints. All results are supported by 42 figures and 12 tables.
\end{abstract}

\subsection{Benchmark A: Linear Regression Quadratic Loss}
\label{sec:bench_A}

A synthetic dataset with $m = 1000$ samples and $d = 2$ parameters is generated as follows:
\begin{equation}
\theta^{\star} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
x^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_2), \quad
y^{(i)} = (\theta^{\star})^T x^{(i)} + \varepsilon^{(i)}, \quad
\varepsilon^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1).
\end{equation}
The loss function is the mean squared error:
\begin{equation}
J(\theta) = \frac{1}{2m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \left(\theta^T x^{(i)} - y^{(i)}\right)^2
= \frac{1}{2m}\|X\theta - y\|_2^2.
\end{equation}
This is a $\mu$-strongly convex and $L$-smooth quadratic with constant Hessian $H = \frac{1}{m}X^T X$. The gradient is $\nabla J(\theta) = \frac{1}{m}X^T(X\theta - y)$. The ordinary least-squares solution is $\theta_{\text{OLS}} = H^{-1}\bigl(\frac{1}{m}X^Ty\bigr) \approx (3.028, 4.017)^T$.

\textbf{Smoothness and conditioning.} Numerically, the Hessian eigenvalues are $\mu = 0.9247$ and $L = 1.0325$, giving a \emph{condition number} $\kappa = L/\mu \approx 1.12$. This is an exceptionally well-conditioned problem: gradient descent with the optimal step $\alpha^* = 2/(L+\mu) \approx 0.99$ converges at rate $((\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1))^k \approx 0.057^k$. Even with the conservative step $\alpha = 0.08$ used in experiments, the rate is $\max(|1-\alpha L|, |1-\alpha\mu|) = \max(0.917, 0.926) \approx 0.926$ per step, and after 120 iterations: $0.926^{120} \approx 1.5 \times 10^{-4}$. This $10^4$-fold reduction in error is more than sufficient to converge to the noise floor $J^{\star} \approx 0.4826$, explaining why all methods easily solve Benchmark A. Starting point: $\theta_0 = (0, 0)^T$.

\subsection{Benchmark B: Toy Neural Network Loss}
\label{sec:bench_B}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bench_B}
f(x_1, x_2) = (x_1 - 1)^2 + 5(x_2 - 2)^2 + \sin(x_1).
\end{equation}
This function combines a quadratic bowl with a sinusoidal perturbation along $x_1$. The gradient and Hessian are:
\begin{align}
\nabla f(x) &= \bigl[2(x_1 - 1) + \cos(x_1),\; 10(x_2 - 2)\bigr]^T, \\
\nabla^2 f(x) &= \operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(2 - \sin(x_1),\; 10\bigr).
\end{align}
The global minimum is at $x^{\star} \approx (0.5824,\, 2.0000)^T$ with $f^{\star} \approx 0.7244$, determined by solving $2(x_1-1)+\cos(x_1) = 0$ numerically. At the optimum, the Hessian has eigenvalues $\mu \approx 1.450$ and $L = 10$, giving $\kappa \approx 6.90$. The sinusoidal perturbation introduces mild nonlinearity, making this a non-quadratic but nearly convex problem. Starting point: $x_0 = (-1, 4)^T$.

\subsection{Benchmark C: Rosenbrock Function}
\label{sec:bench_C}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rosenbrock}
f(x_1, x_2) = (1 - x_1)^2 + 100(x_2 - x_1^2)^2.
\end{equation}
The global minimum is at $(1, 1)^T$ with $f^{\star} = 0$. The Hessian is:
\begin{equation}
\nabla^2 f(x) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 + 1200x_1^2 - 400x_2 & -400x_1 \\ -400x_1 & 200 \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
At the optimum $(1,1)^T$, the Hessian eigenvalues are approximately $0.40$ and $1002$, giving $\kappa \approx 2508$. At the starting point $(-1, 1)^T$, the eigenvalues are similarly $0.40$ and $1002$, with maximum stable gradient descent step $\alpha \leq 1/L \approx 0.001$. This extreme ill-conditioning, combined with the narrow curved valley defined by $x_2 = x_1^2$, makes Rosenbrock one of the most widely used test functions for assessing algorithm robustness. Starting point: $x_0 = (-1, 1)^T$.

\textbf{Valley geometry.} The Rosenbrock valley can be understood geometrically as a two-scale problem: (1) a \emph{transverse} direction orthogonal to the valley $x_2 = x_1^2$, where the Hessian eigenvalue is $\approx 1002$ and the function is steeply curved; and (2) a \emph{longitudinal} direction along the valley toward $(1,1)$, where the eigenvalue is $\approx 0.40$ and the function is nearly flat. The gradient direction is primarily transverse (large gradient component), so gradient descent spends most of its budget descending toward the valley floor. Once on the floor, the gradient along the valley is small ($\|\nabla f\| \approx 0.40 \|x - x^{\star}\|$), requiring many tiny steps to progress. Mathematically: the valley floor is the constraint surface $\{x: h(x) = x_2 - x_1^2 = 0\}$, and motion along the floor is governed by the projected Hessian $P^T H P$ where $P$ is the tangent space of $\{h=0\}$ --- this projected matrix has eigenvalue $\approx 0.40$, corresponding to the slow longitudinal rate.

\section{Question 1: Adaptive Step-Size Methods \hfill [30 marks]}
\label{sec:q1}

\subsection{Theoretical Background}

Gradient descent with a fixed step $\alpha$ satisfies, for a $\mu$-strongly convex and $L$-smooth function:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gd_rate}
f(x_{k+1}) - f^{\star} \leq \left(1 - \frac{2\alpha\mu L}{\mu + L}\right)^{k+1} (f(x_0) - f^{\star}).
\end{equation}
The challenge is that an appropriate $\alpha$ requires knowing $L$ (or $\mu$), which may not be available. Adaptive methods address this by computing effective step sizes from gradient information.

\begin{remark}[Information-Theoretic Lower Bounds for First-Order Methods]
Nesterov established that \emph{no} first-order method (using only gradient information) can improve upon the following lower bounds for $\mu$-strongly convex, $L$-smooth objectives:
\begin{equation}
f(x_k) - f^{\star} \geq \left(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa}+1}\right)^{2k} (f(x_0) - f^{\star}).
\end{equation}
This means gradient descent's $O(\kappa)$ bound is \emph{suboptimal}: it can be improved. Nesterov's accelerated gradient method (Q2) achieves the lower bound, with rate $(1-1/\sqrt{\kappa})^k \approx ((\sqrt{\kappa}-1)/(\sqrt{\kappa}+1))^k$ per step, making it the optimal first-order method for this problem class. The adaptive methods in Q1 (Polyak when exact, Heavy Ball for quadratics) also approach this lower bound under favourable conditions, but none provides the same guarantee for general smooth strongly convex functions.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Connection to Line Search Methods}

Before discussing adaptive step sizes, it is worth noting that an alternative approach to step size selection is \emph{backtracking line search} (Armijo condition): given descent direction $-g_k$, find the largest $\alpha_k$ satisfying:
\begin{equation}
f(x_k - \alpha_k g_k) \leq f(x_k) - c\,\alpha_k\|g_k\|^2, \quad c \in (0, 1).
\end{equation}
This requires additional function evaluations per step but guarantees descent and adapts to local Lipschitz constants without knowing $L$ globally. The adaptive methods in Q1 take a different approach: instead of searching for an appropriate $\alpha$, they \emph{compute} it from gradient history, avoiding function evaluations beyond the gradient itself.

\subsection{Algorithm Formulations}

Four adaptive methods are compared against a constant step-size gradient descent baseline across all three benchmarks for $N = 120$ iterations.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Polyak Step Size \normalfont{[1]}}
\label{alg:polyak}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, optimal value $f^{\star}$, tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $\alpha_k \gets \dfrac{f(x_k) - f^{\star}}{\|g_k\|^2 + \varepsilon}$ \Comment{Optimal step if $f^\star$ known exactly}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha_k\, g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{proposition}[Polyak Convergence]
If $f$ is convex with minimum $f^{\star}$ known exactly and $\nabla f$ is $L$-Lipschitz, the Polyak step satisfies:
\begin{equation}
\|x_{k+1} - x^{\star}\|^2 \leq \|x_k - x^{\star}\|^2 - \frac{(f(x_k) - f^{\star})^2}{\|g_k\|^2},
\end{equation}
guaranteeing monotone decrease in distance to the optimum. The step size $\alpha_k = (f(x_k) - f^{\star})/\|g_k\|^2$ is the exact optimal step for minimising $\|x_{k+1} - x^{\star}\|^2$ along the gradient direction. \emph{If $\hat{f}^{\star} < f_{\text{true}}^{\star}$} (misspecification), the numerator $f(x_k) - \hat{f}^{\star}$ remains positive at the true optimum while $\|g_k\| \to 0$, causing $\alpha_k \to \infty$ and divergence.
\end{proposition}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Adagrad \normalfont{[2]}}
\label{alg:adagrad}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, base learning rate $\alpha_0 > 0$, $\varepsilon > 0$, iterations $N$
\State $G_0 \gets \mathbf{0} \in \mathbb{R}^d$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $G_{k+1} \gets G_k + g_k \odot g_k$ \Comment{Per-coordinate cumulative squared gradient}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \dfrac{\alpha_0}{\sqrt{G_{k+1}} + \varepsilon} \odot g_k$ \Comment{$\odot$ denotes elementwise product}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Adagrad's effective step in coordinate $i$ decays as $\alpha_0 / \sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{k} (g_j)_i^2}$, which is $O(1/\sqrt{k})$. This monotone decay prevents the step from ever growing, which ensures stability but can cause premature slowdown on non-stationary or long-horizon problems.

\begin{remark}[Adagrad and Sparse Gradients]
Adagrad was designed for \emph{online learning with sparse gradients} (e.g., NLP word embeddings), where coordinate $i$ receives a non-zero gradient only when feature $i$ is present in the current example. For sparse features, the cumulative sum $G_k^{(i)} = \sum_{j \leq k} (g_j)_i^2$ grows slowly, keeping the effective step $\alpha_0/\sqrt{G_k^{(i)}}$ large for rare features. For frequent features, $G_k^{(i)}$ grows quickly, automatically reducing the step to prevent oscillation. In the dense-gradient setting of this assignment (all coordinates receive non-zero gradients every step), this advantage is lost and Adagrad's monotone decay causes premature slowdown --- explaining why RMSprop and Adam (with exponential forgetting) generally outperform Adagrad on dense problems such as Benchmark C.
\end{remark}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{RMSprop \normalfont{[3]}}
\label{alg:rmsprop}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, learning rate $\alpha_0$, decay $\beta \in (0,1)$, $\varepsilon > 0$, iterations $N$
\State $v_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $v_{k+1} \gets \beta\, v_k + (1 - \beta)\, g_k \odot g_k$ \Comment{Exponential moving average of $g^2$}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \dfrac{\alpha_0}{\sqrt{v_{k+1}} + \varepsilon} \odot g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

RMSprop replaces Adagrad's cumulative accumulation with an exponential moving average (decay factor $\beta$), retaining only recent curvature information. The effective window length is approximately $1/(1-\beta)$ iterations, so $\beta = 0.9$ averages the last $\sim$10 squared gradients.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Polyak Momentum / Heavy Ball \normalfont{[4]}}
\label{alg:heavyball}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha > 0$, momentum $\beta \in [0, 1)$, iterations $N$
\State $z_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets \beta\, z_k + \alpha\, \nabla f(x_k)$ \Comment{Accumulated momentum term}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - z_{k+1}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

For a $\mu$-strongly convex quadratic with optimal parameters $\alpha^* = 4/(\sqrt{L}+\sqrt{\mu})^2$ and $\beta^* = ((\sqrt{L}-\sqrt{\mu})/(\sqrt{L}+\sqrt{\mu}))^2$, the Heavy Ball method achieves a convergence rate of $(({\sqrt{\kappa}-1})/({\sqrt{\kappa}+1}))^k$ per step, matching Nesterov's optimal rate for quadratics. However, this optimality does not extend to nonconvex problems.

\begin{remark}[Optimal Heavy Ball Parameters per Benchmark]
The theoretically optimal parameters differ substantially from the experimentally used values. For Benchmark A ($\mu=0.925, L=1.033, \kappa=1.12$): $\alpha^* = 4/(\sqrt{1.033}+\sqrt{0.925})^2 \approx 1.02$, $\beta^* \approx 7.5\times10^{-4}$ (near-zero momentum, since near-unit conditioning needs no correction). The experiments use $\alpha=0.045$, a $23\times$ smaller step, with $\beta=0.88$; the high momentum compensates for the small step but at the cost of overshoot. For Benchmark C ($\mu=0.40, L=1002, \kappa=2508$): $\alpha^* \approx 0.00384$, $\beta^* \approx (49.08/51.08)^2 \approx 0.924$. Experiments use $\alpha=0.0008$ ($4.8\times$ smaller) and $\beta=0.86$ (close to optimal). The sub-optimal step accounts for Rosenbrock's non-constant Hessian (the theoretical $L=1002$ is only valid near the optimum; globally the step must be more conservative). This explains why Heavy Ball performs well but not at its theoretical optimum on Benchmark C.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Polyak Divergence under Misspecification]
Let $\hat{f}^{\star} < f^{\star}_{\text{true}}$ (underestimate). Near a non-degenerate minimiser $x^{\star}$ where $\nabla^2 f(x^{\star}) \succ 0$ with eigenvalues $0 < \mu \leq L$, the Polyak step with misspecified target satisfies:
\begin{equation}
\alpha_k = \frac{f(x_k) - \hat{f}^{\star}}{\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2 + \varepsilon} \;\xrightarrow{x_k \to x^{\star}}\; \frac{f^{\star}_{\text{true}} - \hat{f}^{\star}}{\varepsilon} = \Theta(1/\varepsilon) \to \infty
\quad \text{as } \varepsilon \to 0.
\end{equation}
The effective step grows without bound since the denominator $\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2 \to 0$ while the numerator $f(x_k) - \hat{f}^{\star} \to f^{\star} - \hat{f}^{\star} > 0$. For small $\varepsilon$, the step exceeds the stability threshold $2/(L + \mu)$, causing $\|x_{k+1} - x^{\star}\| > \|x_k - x^{\star}\|$ and eventual divergence. Empirically: Benchmark A has $f^{\star} \approx 0.4826$ and $\hat{f}^{\star} = 0$, so at convergence $\alpha_k \approx 0.4826/\varepsilon = 0.4826/10^{-4} = 4826$, far exceeding the stable range $\alpha \leq 0.97$. Benchmark B has $f^{\star} \approx 0.7244$, giving $\alpha_k \approx 7244$. Both predict large divergence, consistent with the empirical final values $f \approx 2.72$ and $f \approx 24.07$ respectively.
\end{proposition}

\textbf{Key finding:} The Polyak step size is highly sensitive to the accuracy of $f^{\star}$. When the specified $\hat{f}^{\star} = 0$ deviates from the true minimum (Benchmarks A and B), the method diverges. On Benchmark C where $\hat{f}^{\star} = 0$ is exact, Polyak achieves the best convergence among all methods, confirming its theoretical optimality under correct specification.

\section{Question 2: Momentum and Stochastic Methods \hfill [30 marks]}
\label{sec:q2}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Theoretical Background}

Gradient descent converges at a linear rate bounded by $(\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1)$ per step. For $\kappa \approx 2508$ (Rosenbrock), this gives a rate of $\approx 0.999$, requiring thousands of iterations to reduce the error by a factor of 10. Nesterov's accelerated gradient achieves the optimal rate of $(1 - 1/\sqrt{\kappa})^k$ for smooth strongly convex problems, requiring only $O(\sqrt{\kappa})$ iterations instead of $O(\kappa)$. This is the fundamental motivation for momentum-based methods.

\subsection{Algorithm Formulations}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Nesterov Momentum / Acceleration \normalfont{[5]}}
\label{alg:nesterov}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha > 0$, max momentum $\beta_{\max} \in [0,1)$, iterations $N$
\State $z_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 1, 2, \ldots, N$}
    \State $\beta_k \gets \min\!\left(\dfrac{k-1}{k+2},\ \beta_{\max}\right)$ \Comment{Momentum schedule: $\beta_k \to 1$ as $k \to \infty$}
    \State $\tilde{x}_k \gets x_k + \beta_k\, z_k$ \Comment{Lookahead point (extrapolation)}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets \beta_k\, z_k - \alpha\, \nabla f(\tilde{x}_k)$ \Comment{Momentum update using lookahead gradient}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k + z_{k+1}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The key distinction from Heavy Ball is the \emph{lookahead step}: the gradient is evaluated at the extrapolated point $\tilde{x}_k = x_k + \beta_k z_k$ rather than at $x_k$ itself. This ``corrective lookahead'' allows the algorithm to anticipate and partially cancel overshoot before it occurs, which is the mechanism behind Nesterov's acceleration guarantee.

\begin{proposition}[Nesterov Convergence Rates]
For an $L$-smooth convex function with the schedule $\beta_k = (k-1)/(k+2)$ and step $\alpha = 1/L$:
\begin{equation}
f(x_k) - f^{\star} \leq \frac{2L\|x_0 - x^{\star}\|^2}{(k+1)^2} = O\!\left(\frac{1}{k^2}\right).
\end{equation}
For a $\mu$-strongly convex and $L$-smooth function with the optimal constant momentum $\beta^{\star} = (\sqrt{\kappa}-1)/(\sqrt{\kappa}+1)$:
\begin{equation}
f(x_k) - f^{\star} \leq \left(1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\right)^k (f(x_0) - f^{\star}).
\end{equation}
Both rates improve upon gradient descent's $O(1/k)$ or $(1-1/\kappa)^k$ bounds, and the strongly convex rate is information-theoretically optimal among first-order methods.
\end{proposition}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Adam Optimiser \normalfont{[6]}}
\label{alg:adam}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, learning rate $\alpha > 0$, moments $\beta_1, \beta_2 \in [0,1)$, $\varepsilon > 0$, iterations $N$
\State $m_0 \gets \mathbf{0},\; v_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$t = 1, 2, \ldots, N$}
    \State $g_t \gets \nabla f(x_t)$
    \State $m_t \gets \beta_1\, m_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_1)\, g_t$ \Comment{First moment: EMA of gradients}
    \State $v_t \gets \beta_2\, v_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_2)\, g_t \odot g_t$ \Comment{Second moment: EMA of $g^2$}
    \State $\hat{m}_t \gets m_t / (1 - \beta_1^t)$ \Comment{Bias correction: removes initialisation bias}
    \State $\hat{v}_t \gets v_t / (1 - \beta_2^t)$
    \State $x_{t+1} \gets x_t - \alpha\, \hat{m}_t / (\sqrt{\hat{v}_t} + \varepsilon)$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Adam combines gradient momentum (via $m_t$, analogous to Heavy Ball) with per-coordinate adaptive scaling (via $v_t$, analogous to RMSprop). The bias correction in $\hat{m}_t$ and $\hat{v}_t$ corrects for the zero initialisation of the moment vectors, which otherwise biases early steps towards zero. Typical parameters are $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$, $\varepsilon = 10^{-8}$.

A key stability property of Adam is that the effective step size in each coordinate is approximately \emph{bounded by $\alpha$}: since $\hat{m}_t^{(i)}$ is an EMA of gradients while $\sqrt{\hat{v}_t^{(i)}}$ is an EMA of gradient magnitudes, $|\hat{m}_t^{(i)}| \leq \sqrt{\hat{v}_t^{(i)}}$ holds approximately (with equality when the gradient is constant in sign), giving $|\hat{m}_t^{(i)}/(\sqrt{\hat{v}_t^{(i)}} + \varepsilon)| \lesssim 1$ and effective step $\lesssim \alpha$. In practice: when gradients are consistent in sign, $\hat{m}_t^{(i)}/\sqrt{\hat{v}_t^{(i)}} \approx \text{sign}(g_t^{(i)})$, so Adam behaves like a \emph{sign gradient method with momentum}, explaining its robustness to gradient scale and near-constant per-coordinate progress in the early phase --- unlike Polyak, Adam cannot diverge due to a growing step size.

\begin{remark}[Adam Convergence Limitations]
While Adam works exceptionally well in practice for training neural networks, Reddi et al.\ [14] demonstrated that Adam can fail to converge on simple convex online learning problems when the effective step size does not decay sufficiently fast. The proposed fix, AMSGrad, uses $\max(\hat{v}_1, \ldots, \hat{v}_t)$ instead of $\hat{v}_t$ in the denominator, guaranteeing convergence but often performing similarly to Adam empirically. In the deterministic setting of this assignment (no stochastic gradients), Adam is equivalent to a momentum method with per-coordinate scaling, and does converge to the exact minimum when the learning rate is sufficiently small.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Nesterov Schedule and Strong Convexity]
The implementation uses the schedule $\beta_k = \min((k-1)/(k+2), \beta_{\max})$. This is a \emph{momentum warmup}: $\beta_k$ starts at 0 (steepest descent) and increases toward $\beta_{\max}$. For the strongly convex Proposition~\ref{prop:nesterov_rates}, the optimal \emph{fixed} momentum is $\beta^* = (\sqrt{\kappa}-1)/(\sqrt{\kappa}+1)$, which for Benchmark C ($\kappa=2508$) gives $\beta^* \approx 49.08/51.08 \approx 0.961$. The experimental cap $\beta_{\max} = 0.90 < 0.961$ is therefore suboptimal for Benchmark C: the optimal rate $(1-1/\sqrt{\kappa})^k \approx 0.980^k$ requires $\beta^* = 0.961$, while $\beta_{\max} = 0.90$ achieves a slightly worse effective rate. This explains the gap between the theoretical $25\times$ iteration speedup and the empirical $7\times$ speedup at 150 iterations. The warmup schedule also trades away some acceleration in early iterations (where $\beta_k < \beta_{\max}$) for more stable initialisation.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Mini-Batch Stochastic Gradient Descent}
\label{sec:sgd}

Mini-batch SGD on Benchmark A uses learning rate $\alpha = 0.06$, batch sizes $b \in \{5, 40\}$, 50 epochs ($m=1000$ data points), with data indices randomly permuted at each epoch. In terms of computational budget: $b=5$ performs $50 \times 200 = 10{,}000$ gradient steps with $50{,}000$ total sample-gradient evaluations; $b=40$ performs $50 \times 25 = 1{,}250$ steps with the same $50{,}000$ sample evaluations; full-batch GD for 50 steps uses $50 \times 1{,}000 = 50{,}000$ evaluations. All three share the same \emph{total computational cost}, so any differences in convergence reflect algorithm efficiency rather than computational advantage. The gradient estimate for a mini-batch $\mathcal{B}$ is:
\begin{equation}
g_{\mathcal{B}}(\theta) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{B}} (x^{(i)T}\theta - y^{(i)}) x^{(i)},
\end{equation}
which satisfies $\mathbb{E}[g_{\mathcal{B}}(\theta)] = \nabla J(\theta)$ (unbiased), with variance $\propto \sigma^2 / |\mathcal{B}|$ where $\sigma^2$ is the per-sample gradient variance.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Mini-Batch SGD}
\label{alg:sgd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Data $(X, y)$, initial $\theta_0$, learning rate $\alpha$, batch size $b$, epochs $E$
\For{$e = 1, 2, \ldots, E$}
    \State Randomly permute data indices
    \For{each mini-batch $\mathcal{B}$ of size $b$}
        \State $g \gets \frac{1}{b} X_{\mathcal{B}}^T(X_{\mathcal{B}}\theta - y_{\mathcal{B}})$
        \State $\theta \gets \theta - \alpha\, g$
    \EndFor
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/q2_sgd_batch.pdf}
\caption{Q2: Mini-batch SGD with different batch sizes on Benchmark A. With $b = 5$, each epoch performs $1000/5 = 200$ gradient updates and converges faster in terms of epoch count due to the higher update frequency. However, the smaller batch means higher gradient variance, producing loss oscillations near the minimum. With $b = 40$, each epoch has $1000/40 = 25$ updates with lower variance, yielding smoother but slower per-epoch progress. The variance of the stochastic gradient is $\text{Var}[g_{\mathcal{B}}] \propto 1/b$, explaining the $\sqrt{8}$-fold difference in noise level between $b=5$ and $b=40$. Both batch sizes converge to the same noise floor since the fixed step size $\alpha=0.06$ does not decay.}
\label{fig:q2_sgd_batch}
\end{figure}

\begin{remark}[Variance-Reduced SGD]
Standard mini-batch SGD with a fixed learning rate converges to a \emph{neighbourhood} of the optimum (the noise floor) rather than to the exact minimiser, because the gradient variance $\text{Var}[g_{\mathcal{B}}] > 0$ prevents the updates from vanishing. This limitation can be overcome by \emph{variance reduction methods} such as SVRG (Stochastic Variance Reduced Gradient) [16] or SAG (Stochastic Average Gradient), which periodically recompute the full gradient to correct the stochastic gradient estimate. SVRG achieves a convergence rate of $O((n + \kappa) \log(1/\varepsilon))$ on finite-sum objectives, matching gradient descent's accuracy with near-linear time complexity.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Learning Rate Decay Schedules]
A practical alternative to variance reduction is \emph{learning rate decay}: reducing the step size $\alpha_t \to 0$ at a schedule that guarantees both vanishing bias (noise floor goes to zero) and vanishing variance. The classical result for SGD on strongly convex problems requires $\alpha_t = O(1/t)$ (Robbins--Monro conditions: $\sum_t \alpha_t = \infty$ and $\sum_t \alpha_t^2 < \infty$), which achieves the optimal $O(1/t)$ convergence rate in expectation. In practice, common schedules include: (i) \emph{step decay} --- multiply $\alpha$ by $\gamma \in (0,1)$ every $k$ epochs; (ii) \emph{cosine annealing} --- $\alpha_t = \alpha_{\min} + \frac{1}{2}(\alpha_{\max}-\alpha_{\min})(1 + \cos(\pi t/T))$; and (iii) \emph{polynomial decay} --- $\alpha_t = \alpha_0/(1 + \rho t)$. For the fixed $\alpha = 0.06$ used in the experiments, the noise floor is $O(\alpha \sigma^2 / b)$ rather than zero, which is the cost of prioritising implementation simplicity over asymptotic exactness.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Local Approximations of $g(x) = x^4$}

At a point $x_0$, the $k$-th order Taylor expansion of a smooth function $g$ is:
\begin{equation}
g(x) \approx \sum_{j=0}^{k} \frac{g^{(j)}(x_0)}{j!} (x - x_0)^j.
\end{equation}

\textbf{First-order (tangent line) approximation} at $x_0 = 0.25$:
\begin{equation}
\hat{g}_1(x) = g(x_0) + g'(x_0)(x - x_0) = (0.25)^4 + 4(0.25)^3(x - 0.25)
\approx 0.0039 + 0.0625(x - 0.25).
\end{equation}

\textbf{Second-order (parabolic) approximation:}
\begin{equation}
\hat{g}_2(x) = \hat{g}_1(x) + \tfrac{1}{2}g''(x_0)(x - x_0)^2
= 0.0039 + 0.0625(x - 0.25) + 0.375(x - 0.25)^2.
\end{equation}
since $g''(x) = 12x^2$ gives $g''(0.25) = 12(0.0625) = 0.75$.

The Newton step minimises $\hat{g}_2$ by setting $\hat{g}_2'(x) = 0$:
\begin{equation}
x_{k+1} = x_k - \frac{g'(x_k)}{g''(x_k)}.
\end{equation}
For $g(x) = x^4$, this gives $x_{k+1} = x_k - \frac{4x_k^3}{12x_k^2} = x_k - \frac{x_k}{3} = \frac{2}{3}x_k$, which converges \emph{linearly} to 0 with rate $2/3$. This is slower than quadratic convergence because $g'(x) = 4x^3 = 0$ has a root of multiplicity $m = 3$: Newton's method achieves quadratic convergence only at \emph{simple} roots ($m=1$). For a root of multiplicity $m$, the convergence rate is $(1 - 1/m)^k = (2/3)^k$. This illustrates a fundamental limitation: Newton's method applied to minimisation (i.e., root-finding for $g'$) achieves quadratic convergence only when the minimum is \emph{non-degenerate} ($g''(x^*) \neq 0$), which fails for $g(x) = x^4$ where $g''(0) = g'''(0) = 0$.

\begin{remark}[Backtracking Line Search]
To avoid the manual step-size tuning required by damped Newton ($\alpha = 0.22$ for Benchmark C), Newton's method is typically combined with an \emph{Armijo backtracking line search}: start with $\alpha = 1$ and multiply by $\tau \in (0,1)$ until the sufficient decrease condition $f(x_k - \alpha p_k) \leq f(x_k) - c\alpha\, \nabla f(x_k)^T p_k$ is satisfied (for $c \in (0, 0.5)$). This automatically selects the largest stable step, achieving quadratic convergence near the optimum (where $\alpha = 1$ passes the line search) and guaranteed descent far from it.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Newton's Method with Damping}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Newton's Method with Damping and Hessian Regularisation}
\label{alg:newton}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha > 0$, damping $\lambda \geq 0$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $H_k \gets \nabla^2 f(x_k) + \lambda I$ \Comment{Tikhonov regularisation ensures $H_k \succ 0$}
    \State Solve $H_k p_k = g_k$ for $p_k$ (Newton direction)
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, p_k$ \Comment{Damped Newton step; $\alpha=1$ is full Newton}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Newton's method has \emph{local quadratic convergence}: in a neighbourhood of a non-degenerate minimiser where $\nabla^2 f \succ 0$, the iterate satisfies:
\begin{equation}
\|x_{k+1} - x^{\star}\| \leq C\|x_k - x^{\star}\|^2,
\end{equation}
for some constant $C > 0$. This superlinear convergence rate means the number of accurate digits roughly doubles each iteration near the optimum, contrasted with gradient descent's linear convergence rate.

\begin{remark}[Newton Decrement as Stopping Criterion]
A principled stopping criterion for Newton's method is the \emph{Newton decrement}:
\begin{equation}
\lambda(x) = \bigl(\nabla f(x)^T [\nabla^2 f(x)]^{-1} \nabla f(x)\bigr)^{1/2} = \|p\|_{H},
\end{equation}
where $p = H^{-1}\nabla f(x)$ is the Newton direction. The quantity $\frac{1}{2}\lambda(x)^2$ is a computable upper bound on $f(x) - f^{\star}$: specifically, for $L$-smooth strongly convex $f$, $f(x) - f^{\star} \leq \frac{1}{2}\lambda(x)^2$. Unlike the gradient norm $\|\nabla f(x)\|$, the Newton decrement is scale-invariant (unaffected by linear changes of variable) and provides a meaningful suboptimality estimate when the Hessian is ill-conditioned. Newton's method is typically terminated when $\lambda(x)^2/2 < \varepsilon$ for a desired tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$, which corresponds to the \emph{quadratic phase} where the gradient norm is guaranteed to decrease quadratically at each subsequent iteration.
\end{remark}

Settings: GD uses $N_{\text{GD}} = 80$ iterations; Newton uses $N_{\text{N}} = 20$ iterations with Hessian regularisation $\lambda = 10^{-8}$ (negligible numerically).

\subsection{Convergence Comparison}

\begin{proposition}[Newton's Method on Quadratics: Exact One-Step Convergence]
For a quadratic objective $J(\theta) = \frac{1}{2}\theta^T H\theta - b^T\theta$ with $H \succ 0$, the full Newton step ($\alpha = 1$) from any starting point $\theta_0$ converges to the minimiser $\theta^{\star} = H^{-1}b$ in \emph{exactly one step}:
\begin{equation}
\theta_1 = \theta_0 - H^{-1}\nabla J(\theta_0) = \theta_0 - H^{-1}(H\theta_0 - b) = \theta_0 - \theta_0 + H^{-1}b = \theta^{\star}.
\end{equation}
This is independent of the condition number $\kappa$: unlike gradient descent, whose convergence rate $((κ-1)/(κ+1))^k$ degrades with $\kappa$, Newton's method is \emph{condition-number blind} on quadratics. For non-quadratic functions, the local quadratic model $f(x) \approx f(x_k) + \nabla f(x_k)^T(x-x_k) + \frac{1}{2}(x-x_k)^T H_k (x-x_k)$ is only exact when $f$ is quadratic; departures from quadraticity introduce error and require damping ($\alpha < 1$) to maintain descent.
\end{proposition}

\subsection{Comparative Analysis}

Newton's method is transformative for quadratic problems (single-step convergence) and highly effective for near-quadratic problems (B: $\sim$5 steps). For Rosenbrock, damping is essential: the rapidly varying Hessian means the quadratic model is accurate only locally, and undamped Newton steps would diverge. The computational cost per step is $O(d^3)$ for the Hessian solve (via LU decomposition), compared to $O(d)$ for gradient descent, but the superlinear convergence rate means far fewer steps are needed.

\begin{remark}[Quasi-Newton Methods]
For large-scale problems where the full Hessian computation and $O(d^3)$ solve are prohibitive, \emph{quasi-Newton methods} such as BFGS and L-BFGS approximate the Hessian (or its inverse) from successive gradient differences using the secant condition: $B_{k+1}(x_{k+1} - x_k) = \nabla f(x_{k+1}) - \nabla f(x_k)$. L-BFGS stores only the last $m$ gradient differences ($m \approx 10$--20), giving $O(md)$ memory and $O(md)$ per-step cost with superlinear (but not quadratic) convergence. This makes quasi-Newton methods the practical choice for high-dimensional smooth problems, bridging the gap between first-order ($O(d)$ cost, linear rate) and Newton's method ($O(d^3)$ cost, quadratic rate).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Part A: Finite Difference and Nesterov Random Search (Benchmark B)}

\subsubsection{Finite Difference Gradient Approximation}

When analytic gradients are unavailable, the partial derivative can be approximated using the \emph{forward} finite difference:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x) \approx \frac{f(x + \delta e_i) - f(x)}{\delta}, \quad \text{error} = O(\delta).
\end{equation}
A more accurate alternative is the \emph{centred} finite difference (using $2d$ function evaluations):
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x) \approx \frac{f(x + \delta e_i) - f(x - \delta e_i)}{2\delta}, \quad \text{error} = O(\delta^2).
\end{equation}
The centred approximation is $O(\delta)$ more accurate than forward differences for the same $\delta$, at the cost of one additional function evaluation per coordinate. Empirically, at $\delta = 0.05$ on Benchmark B at $x = (-0.5, 3.0)$: the forward FD gradient error is $\|\hat{g}_{\text{fwd}} - g\|_2 \approx 0.258$, while the centred FD error is $\|\hat{g}_{\text{cen}} - g\|_2 \approx 0.0004$ --- a 600-fold improvement. At $\delta = 0.8$: forward error $\approx 4.10$, centred error $\approx 0.091$.

The total error for forward FD combines truncation $O(\delta)$ and floating-point cancellation $O(\varepsilon_{\text{mach}}/\delta)$, yielding an optimal $\delta^{\star}_{\text{fwd}} \sim \sqrt{\varepsilon_{\text{mach}}} \approx 10^{-8}$. For centred FD, the truncation error is $O(\delta^2)$, giving the optimal $\delta^{\star}_{\text{cen}} \sim \varepsilon_{\text{mach}}^{1/3} \approx 10^{-5}$, which is more practical. The experiments test $\delta = 0.05$ (close to optimal for this function scale) and $\delta = 0.8$ (dominated by truncation error).

\subsubsection{Nesterov Random Search}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Nesterov Random Search (Zeroth-Order) \normalfont{[8]}}
\label{alg:nrs}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha > 0$, perturbation $\delta > 0$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State Sample $u_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)$; normalise $\hat{u}_k \gets u_k / \|u_k\|$
    \State $\hat{g}_k \gets \dfrac{f(x_k + \delta \hat{u}_k) - f(x_k)}{\delta}\, \hat{u}_k$ \Comment{Random directional gradient estimate}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \hat{g}_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

This zeroth-order method estimates a directional derivative along a uniformly random unit direction $\hat{u}_k$, requiring only \emph{two} function evaluations per iteration regardless of dimension $d$. The key identity is $\mathbb{E}_u[\hat{g}_k] = \nabla_\delta f(x_k)$ (a smoothed gradient with smoothing radius $\delta$). Nesterov and Spokoiny [8] show this method converges at rate $O(d/\sqrt{k})$ for $L$-smooth non-convex problems:
\begin{equation}
\min_{0 \leq k \leq N} \mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2] \leq \frac{2L(f(x_0) - f^{\star}) + \delta^2 L^2 N}{(N+1)^{1/2}} \cdot \frac{d}{N^{1/2}}.
\end{equation}
For $d = 2$ (as in these experiments), the $O(d/\sqrt{k})$ rate is only $2\times$ worse than the $O(1/\sqrt{k})$ rate of full gradient descent, explaining the close match observed in practice. However, this rate degrades as $d$ increases: for $d = 100$, random search would require $100\times$ more iterations for the same accuracy, making it impractical in high dimensions.

\subsection{Part B: Derivative-Free Methods (Benchmark C)}

\subsubsection{Nelder--Mead Simplex Method}

The Nelder--Mead algorithm [9] maintains a simplex of $n+1 = 3$ vertices in $\mathbb{R}^2$ and iteratively transforms it through four operations, using standard coefficients $(\alpha, \chi, \gamma, \sigma) = (1, 2, 1/2, 1/2)$:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Reflection} ($\alpha = 1$): Reflect the worst vertex $x_{n+1}$ through the centroid $\bar{x}$ of the remaining vertices: $x_r = \bar{x} + \alpha(\bar{x} - x_{n+1}) = 2\bar{x} - x_{n+1}$. \emph{If} $f(x_1) \leq f(x_r) < f(x_n)$: accept $x_r$ and continue.
\item \textbf{Expansion} ($\chi = 2$): \emph{If} reflection improved beyond the best ($f(x_r) < f(x_1)$), try $x_e = \bar{x} + \chi(\bar{x} - x_{n+1})$; accept whichever of $x_e$, $x_r$ is better.
\item \textbf{Contraction} ($\gamma = 1/2$): \emph{If} $f(x_r) \geq f(x_n)$: try outside contraction $x_c = \bar{x} + \gamma(x_r - \bar{x})$ or inside contraction $x_c = \bar{x} + \gamma(x_{n+1} - \bar{x})$ depending on $f(x_r)$ vs $f(x_{n+1})$.
\item \textbf{Shrink} ($\sigma = 1/2$): If contraction fails, contract all non-best vertices toward the best: $x_i \gets x_1 + \sigma(x_i - x_1)$ for $i = 2, \ldots, n+1$.
\end{enumerate}
Settings: initial simplex step $= 0.35$, $N = 160$ iterations. No gradient information is required.

\begin{remark}[Nelder--Mead Failure Modes]
Despite its widespread use, Nelder--Mead lacks global convergence guarantees and can fail in several ways: (1) \emph{Simplex degeneracy} --- the simplex collapses to a lower-dimensional affine subspace, losing its ability to probe all directions; (2) \emph{Cycling} --- the reflection/contraction sequence repeats without progress, particularly on non-smooth or flat objectives; (3) \emph{Non-convergence to a stationary point} --- McKinnon [17] constructed a smooth, strictly convex function in $\mathbb{R}^2$ where Nelder--Mead converges to a non-stationary point. The empirical effectiveness on smooth, low-dimensional problems (as observed here) stems from the simplex implicitly encoding directional information about the Hessian without ever computing it. For $d \geq 6$, Nelder--Mead's performance degrades rapidly, and derivative-free methods based on quadratic model interpolation (e.g., BOBYQA, COBYLA) are preferred.
\end{remark}

\subsubsection{Grid Search}

Grid search evaluates the objective on a uniform $55 \times 55 = 3025$ point grid over $x_1 \in [-2, 2]$, $x_2 \in [-1, 3]$, with grid spacing $\Delta x \approx 0.073$ in each coordinate.

\begin{remark}[Space-Filling Alternatives to Uniform Grid Search]
Uniform grid search has two major drawbacks: its $O(N^d)$ scaling and its tendency to concentrate evaluations along coordinate-aligned lines (many grid points may yield the same marginal distribution in each coordinate). \emph{Latin Hypercube Sampling} (LHS) addresses both: it divides each coordinate axis into $N$ equal intervals and samples exactly one point per interval per coordinate, achieving better coverage of the $d$-dimensional space with the same $N$ evaluations. For $d = 2$ with $N = 55$ samples, LHS achieves similar coverage to the $55 \times 55$ grid (3025 evaluations) but uses only 55 evaluations --- a $55\times$ reduction. For $d \geq 3$, LHS becomes essential: a $10^3$ uniform grid in $d=3$ requires $10^9$ evaluations, while LHS achieves comparable coverage with $10^3$ evaluations. LHS results can then be used as warm starts for local optimisers.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Problem Formulation and KKT Conditions}

The constrained problem is:
\begin{equation}
\min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^2} f(x) = (x_1-1)^2 + 5(x_2-2)^2 + \sin(x_1)
\quad \text{subject to} \quad x_1 \geq 0.5.
\end{equation}
This is equivalent to $\min_{x} f(x)$ s.t.\ $g(x) = 0.5 - x_1 \leq 0$. The KKT necessary conditions for a local minimum $x^{\star}$ are:
\begin{align}
\nabla f(x^{\star}) + \lambda^{\star} \nabla g(x^{\star}) &= 0, \label{eq:kkt_stationarity}\\
g(x^{\star}) &\leq 0, \quad \lambda^{\star} \geq 0, \quad \lambda^{\star} g(x^{\star}) = 0. \label{eq:kkt_comp_slack}
\end{align}
The unconstrained minimiser of Benchmark B is $x^{\star}_{\text{unc}} \approx (0.582, 2.0)$, which satisfies $x^{\star}_{1, \text{unc}} = 0.582 \geq 0.5$. Therefore, the constraint $x_1 \geq 0.5$ is \emph{inactive} at the unconstrained solution ($g(x^{\star}) = 0.5 - 0.582 < 0$). By complementary slackness (Eq.~\ref{eq:kkt_comp_slack}): $\lambda^{\star} g(x^{\star}) = 0$ implies either $\lambda^{\star} = 0$ or $g(x^{\star}) = 0$. Since $g(x^{\star}) < 0$, we must have $\lambda^{\star} = 0$. Substituting into the stationarity condition (Eq.~\ref{eq:kkt_stationarity}): $\nabla f(x^{\star}) + 0 \cdot \nabla g(x^{\star}) = \nabla f(x^{\star}) = 0$, which holds at the unconstrained minimiser. Therefore, the constrained and unconstrained minima coincide: $x^{\star}_{\text{con}} = (0.582, 2.0)$, $f^{\star} = 0.7244$.

The optimisation starts from the infeasible point $x_0 = (0.2, 4.0)$, which violates the constraint ($x_1 = 0.2 < 0.5$).

\begin{remark}[Active vs Inactive Constraints]
Whether a constraint is active or inactive at the solution determines the complexity of the constrained problem. For this problem, the unconstrained optimum $x^{\star}_{\text{unc}} = (0.582, 2.0)$ satisfies $x_1^{\star} = 0.582 > 0.5$, so the constraint is \emph{inactive} and both constrained and unconstrained minima coincide. If instead the unconstrained minimum had $x_1^{\star} < 0.5$ (e.g., if $f(x) = (x_1 - 0.3)^2 + 5(x_2-2)^2 + \sin(x_1)$ with $x_1^{\star} \approx 0.07$), the constraint would be \emph{active}: the constrained minimum would be on the boundary $x_1 = 0.5$, the KKT multiplier $\lambda^{\star} > 0$, and the methods would converge to different values than unconstrained GD. In this more interesting case, the stationarity condition gives: $\nabla f(x^{\star}) + \lambda^{\star} \nabla g(x^{\star}) = 0 \Rightarrow \lambda^{\star} = \partial_{x_1} f(0.5, x_2^{\star})$, which must be solved simultaneously with the boundary constraint $x_1 = 0.5$.
\end{remark}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Projected Gradient Descent}
\label{alg:pgd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha > 0$, projection $\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \nabla f(x_k)$ \Comment{Unconstrained gradient step}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets \Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(z_{k+1})$ \Comment{Project onto $\mathcal{X}$: $(x_1, x_2) \mapsto (\max(0.5, x_1), x_2)$}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Projected gradient descent is guaranteed to reduce the objective at every step when $\mathcal{X}$ is convex and $\alpha \leq 1/L$, satisfying the descent condition $f(x_{k+1}) \leq f(x_k)$ with $\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}$ being the Euclidean projection. For a half-space constraint $x_1 \geq 0.5$, the projection is a simple clipping operation: $\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(z) = (\max(0.5, z_1), z_2)$. For a $\mu$-strongly convex, $L$-smooth objective restricted to a convex set, projected GD satisfies:
\begin{equation}
f(x_{k+1}) - f^{\star} \leq \left(1 - 2\alpha\frac{\mu L}{\mu + L}\right)(f(x_k) - f^{\star}),
\end{equation}
with the same linear convergence rate as unconstrained GD. With $\alpha = 0.08$, $\mu = 1.45$, $L = 10$ (Benchmark B), the per-step rate is $1 - 2 \times 0.08 \times 1.45 \times 10 / (1.45 + 10) \approx 0.797$, giving $(0.797)^{100} \approx 10^{-10}$ after 100 iterations --- sufficient for full convergence to $f^\star$.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Quadratic Penalty Method}
\label{alg:penalty}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha > 0$, penalty weight $\lambda > 0$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $F_{\lambda}(x) \gets f(x) + \lambda \max(0,\, 0.5 - x_1)$ \Comment{Penalised objective; one-sided L1/hinge penalty}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \nabla_x F_{\lambda}(x_k)$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The gradient of the penalised objective is:
\begin{equation}
\nabla F_{\lambda}(x) = \nabla f(x) + \begin{cases} (-\lambda, 0)^T & \text{if } x_1 < 0.5 \\ (0, 0)^T & \text{if } x_1 \geq 0.5 \end{cases}.
\end{equation}
As $\lambda \to \infty$, the penalty forces $x_1 \to 0.5$ from the infeasible side, and the penalised solution converges to the constrained solution. However, large $\lambda$ increases the condition number of $F_\lambda$, necessitating smaller step sizes.

Settings: Projected GD ($\alpha = 0.08$), Penalty with $\lambda \in \{0.15, 1.8, 4.5\}$ ($\alpha = 0.05$ for $\lambda \leq 1.8$; $\alpha = 0.03$ for $\lambda = 4.5$), unconstrained GD ($\alpha = 0.07$). All run for 100 iterations.

\textbf{Projected GD} [12] enforces feasibility as a \emph{hard constraint}: every iterate $x_k \in \mathcal{X}$. This is the fundamental advantage for safety-critical applications where constraint violation is unacceptable. The cost is requiring a projection oracle, which for a half-space is trivial ($O(1)$) but can be $O(d^3)$ for general polytopes (e.g., polytope defined by $m$ inequalities).

\textbf{Penalty methods} enforce feasibility \emph{softly} via the modified objective $F_\lambda = f + \lambda \cdot \text{violation}$. The advantage is simplicity: no projection oracle is needed, only the gradient of $F_\lambda$. The trade-off: small $\lambda$ allows residual violation; large $\lambda$ creates an ill-conditioned objective (the gradient of the penalty term dominates), requiring smaller step sizes (here $\alpha = 0.03$ for $\lambda = 4.5$, vs $\alpha = 0.05$ for smaller $\lambda$). In the augmented Lagrangian method, this ill-conditioning is addressed by updating $\lambda$ adaptively while adding a quadratic penalty term.

\textbf{Connection to KKT:} When the constraint is inactive at the optimum (as in this problem), both methods converge to the same solution as unconstrained gradient descent. The methods differ primarily in their approach to handling the infeasible starting point and their treatment of the constraint boundary. Figure~\ref{fig:q5_penalty_land} visualises how the penalty weight reshapes the objective landscape.

\begin{remark}[Augmented Lagrangian Method]
The penalty method's limitation (ill-conditioning as $\lambda \to \infty$) can be addressed by the \emph{Augmented Lagrangian} method, which combines the Lagrange multiplier $\mu$ with a quadratic penalty:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_\rho(x, \mu) = f(x) + \mu\, g(x) + \frac{\rho}{2} g(x)^2, \quad g(x) = \max(0, 0.5 - x_1).
\end{equation}
The multiplier $\mu$ is updated as $\mu_{k+1} = \mu_k + \rho\, g(x_k^{\star})$ after each inner minimisation. This approach achieves exact constraint satisfaction with a finite penalty parameter $\rho$, avoiding the ill-conditioning associated with sending $\lambda \to \infty$ in the pure penalty method.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Feasible Set and Linear Programme}

Feasible set: $\mathcal{X} = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^2 : 0.5 \leq x_1 \leq 5,\; -5 \leq x_2 \leq 10\}$ (rectangular box with four vertices).

\textbf{Linear Programme:} Minimise $f(x) = a^T x = x_1 + 2x_2$ over $\mathcal{X}$. Since $f$ is linear, $\nabla f = a = (1, 2)^T$ is constant and strictly positive in both coordinates. By the \emph{Fundamental Theorem of Linear Programming}, the minimum of a linear objective over a bounded convex polytope is attained at a vertex. For a box constraint, the minimum in coordinate $i$ is at the lower bound when $a_i > 0$:
\begin{equation}
x^{\star} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 \\ -5 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
f^{\star} = 1 \times 0.5 + 2 \times (-5) = -9.5.
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/q6_lp.pdf}
\caption{Q6-I: Feasible region $\mathcal{X}$ (blue rectangle) and linear objective contours $f(x) = x_1 + 2x_2 = c$. The contour lines are parallel (constant gradient $a$), and the minimum occurs at the lower-left vertex $(0.5, -5)$ where both coordinates are at their lower bounds. This illustrates the fundamental LP property: the optimum of any linear objective over a bounded polytope lies at a vertex. For a box with $d$ coordinates and two bounds per coordinate, there are $2^d$ vertices to check, but the LP oracle used by Frank--Wolfe can identify the optimal vertex in $O(d)$ by inspecting each gradient sign independently.}
\label{fig:q6_lp}

\subsection{Frank--Wolfe Algorithm (Conditional Gradient)}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Frank--Wolfe (Conditional Gradient) \normalfont{[10]}}
\label{alg:fw}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0 \in \mathcal{X}$, mixing parameter $\beta \in [0,1)$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_k \gets \arg\min_{x \in \mathcal{X}}\, \nabla f(x_k)^T x$ \Comment{Linear minimisation oracle (LP subproblem)}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets \beta\, x_k + (1 - \beta)\, z_k$ \Comment{Convex combination stays in $\mathcal{X}$}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

A key property is that since $x_k, z_k \in \mathcal{X}$ and $\mathcal{X}$ is convex, every iterate $x_{k+1}$ is a convex combination of feasible points and thus automatically feasible: \emph{no projection is required}. For box constraints, the LP oracle solves in $O(d)$ by:
\begin{equation}
(z_k)_i = \begin{cases} l_i & \text{if } (\nabla f(x_k))_i > 0 \\ u_i & \text{if } (\nabla f(x_k))_i \leq 0 \end{cases}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Convergence:} For a convex, $L$-smooth objective with diameter $D = \max_{x,y \in \mathcal{X}} \|x-y\|$ and the decay schedule $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$, Frank--Wolfe achieves:
\begin{equation}
f(x_k) - f^{\star} \leq \frac{2LD^2}{k+2} = O\left(\frac{1}{k}\right).
\end{equation}
This $O(1/k)$ rate is slower than projected gradient descent's $O(1/k)$ (with better constants) but avoids the expensive projection oracle. For the box constraint used here: $D^2 = (5-0.5)^2 + (10-(-5))^2 = 20.25 + 225 = 245.25$, giving the bound $f(x_k) - f^\star \leq 2 \times 2 \times 245.25/(k+2) = 981/(k+2)$.

The standard Frank--Wolfe convergence proof uses the decaying schedule $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$ (equivalently $\beta_k = 1 - 2/(k+2) = k/(k+2) \to 1$). The experiments here use a \emph{fixed} $\beta$, equivalent to a constant step $\gamma = 1-\beta$: for $\beta = 0.985$, the effective step $\gamma = 0.015$ is roughly equivalent to the schedule at iteration $k \approx 2/\gamma - 2 \approx 131$. A fixed $\beta$ is simpler to tune but may not converge to the exact optimum: the $O(1/k)$ bound requires $\gamma_k \to 0$, and fixed $\gamma$ introduces a residual gap $O(\gamma LD^2)$ analogous to the SGD noise floor with a fixed learning rate.

\begin{remark}[Sparse Solutions and Atomic Decomposition]
A fundamental advantage of Frank--Wolfe in high-dimensional sparse optimisation (e.g., constrained to the $\ell_1$ ball) is that each iterate $x_k$ is a convex combination of at most $k+1$ extreme points (atoms) of $\mathcal{X}$. This gives Frank--Wolfe a natural \emph{sparsity-inducing} property: the LP oracle selects atoms (vertices) that are aligned with the gradient, and the convex combination progressively focuses on the most relevant ones. For the $\ell_1$ ball $\{x : \|x\|_1 \leq 1\}$, atoms are the $\pm e_i$ standard basis vectors, so FW automatically produces sparse solutions with at most $k$ non-zero coordinates after $k$ iterations.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Away-Step Frank--Wolfe for Linear Convergence on Polytopes]
The standard Frank--Wolfe algorithm achieves only $O(1/k)$ convergence even for strongly convex objectives, because the \emph{zig-zag} phenomenon prevents it from exploiting strong convexity. The \emph{Away-Step Frank--Wolfe} (AFW) variant [15] augments each update with an ``away step'' that removes weight from the atom most misaligned with the gradient:
\begin{equation}
x_{k+1} = x_k + \gamma_k^{\text{FW}}(z_k - x_k) \quad \text{or} \quad x_{k+1} = x_k - \gamma_k^{\text{A}}(s_k - x_k),
\end{equation}
where $s_k = \arg\max_{v \in \mathcal{S}(x_k)} \nabla f(x_k)^T v$ is the ``away atom'' in the current active set $\mathcal{S}(x_k)$. AFW achieves \emph{linear} convergence $O(\rho^k)$ on strongly convex objectives over polytopes, matching the rate of projected gradient descent but without requiring a projection oracle. The price is maintaining and updating the active set of atoms throughout the optimisation.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Interior vs Boundary Behaviour}

\textbf{Interior optimum:} When the unconstrained minimum lies inside $\mathcal{X}$, Frank--Wolfe converges to it but exhibits a characteristic zig-zag: as $x_k$ approaches the interior, the gradient $\nabla f(x_k) \to 0$, causing small perturbations to determine the LP vertex $z_k$, which then oscillates between different vertices. The convex combination $x_{k+1} = \beta x_k + (1-\beta)z_k$ averages out these oscillations, but this averaging slows convergence. The $O(1/k)$ convergence rate reflects this inefficiency near interior minima.

\begin{remark}[$O(1/k)$ Despite Strong Convexity at Interior Optima]
For the interior case $f(x) = (x_1-1)^2 + (x_2-5)^2$ with $\mu = 2$ (strongly convex), projected gradient descent would achieve linear convergence $O(\rho^k)$ with $\rho = 1 - 2\alpha\mu/(1+\kappa)$. Frank--Wolfe, however, is provably limited to $O(1/k)$ even for strongly convex $f$ when the optimum is in the interior of $\mathcal{X}$. This is because the Frank--Wolfe update $x_{k+1} = \beta x_k + (1-\beta) z_k$ is constrained to convex combinations of $x_k$ and an extreme point $z_k \in \text{vert}(\mathcal{X})$. When $x^{\star}$ is in the interior, $z_k$ oscillates between vertices as the gradient direction rotates near the optimum, and the algorithm cannot ``stay'' near $x^{\star}$ --- each step pulls $x_k$ toward a vertex, counteracting the progress made. This is the fundamental limitation motivating the Away-Step FW variant, which achieves linear convergence by explicitly moving away from the current active vertex.
\end{remark}

\textbf{Boundary optimum:} Frank--Wolfe is particularly natural when the constrained minimum lies on the boundary, because the LP oracle $z_k$ consistently selects the vertex closest to the gradient direction, which corresponds to the binding constraint face. The iterates naturally approach the boundary without explicit projection, demonstrating Frank--Wolfe's fundamental advantage: \emph{constraint satisfaction by convex combination rather than projection}. The residual gap between the achieved value ($f = 0.364$) and $f^{\star} = 0.25$ arises because the fixed $\beta = 0.93$ does not satisfy the standard $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$ decay schedule required for the $O(1/k)$ convergence guarantee.

\begin{remark}[Frank--Wolfe Duality Gap]
A natural convergence criterion for Frank--Wolfe is the \emph{Frank--Wolfe gap} (or duality gap):
\begin{equation}
g_k = \nabla f(x_k)^T(x_k - z_k),
\end{equation}
which satisfies $g_k \geq f(x_k) - f^{\star} \geq 0$ for convex $f$. When $g_k = 0$, the KKT conditions for the LP subproblem are satisfied, confirming optimality. The gap provides a cheap upper bound on suboptimality without requiring $f^{\star}$. Figure~\ref{fig:q6_gap} shows empirically that both the objective value and the duality gap follow the theoretical $O(1/k)$ rate closely, validating the convergence theory.
\end{remark}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

This study has demonstrated that no single optimisation algorithm dominates across all problem structures. The comprehensive comparison in Table~\ref{tab:all_methods} and the benchmark properties in Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks} together confirm that algorithm selection must be guided by the problem's condition number, availability of derivative information, constraint structure, and computational budget.

\textbf{Impact of condition number (Tables~\ref{tab:benchmarks}, \ref{tab:q1_final}--\ref{tab:q2_final}).} The condition number $\kappa$ is the primary determinant of first-order algorithm performance. Benchmark A ($\kappa = 1.12$) is easily solved by all methods. Benchmark B ($\kappa = 6.90$) requires more iterations but remains tractable. Benchmark C ($\kappa = 2508$) exposes the fundamental limitation: gradient descent's optimal-step convergence rate $((\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1)) \approx 0.9992$ per step requires $\sim$8600 iterations to reduce error by $10^{3}$, while Nesterov's optimal rate $(1-1/\sqrt{\kappa}) \approx 0.980$ requires only $\sim$343 iterations for the same reduction --- a $\mathbf{25\times}$ iteration-count advantage (Table~\ref{tab:iteration_counts}). In the 150-iteration experiment, this translates to a $7\times$ lower final loss ($f_{\text{Nest}} \approx 0.479$ vs $f_{\text{GD}} \approx 3.388$). Newton's method resolves this entirely through curvature normalisation, rendering $\kappa$ irrelevant near the optimum.

\textbf{Adaptive step sizes (Q1, Section~\ref{sec:q1}).} The Polyak step is theoretically optimal ($\alpha_k = (f(x_k) - f^\star) / \|\nabla f\|^2$) and achieves the best empirical result on Rosenbrock ($f = 0.009$ vs next-best Nesterov $f = 0.479$), but catastrophically fails when $f^\star$ is misspecified ($f = 2.72$ on Benchmark A, $f = 24.07$ on Benchmark B). This fragility limits its practical applicability. Adagrad and Heavy Ball provide robust convergence across all benchmarks without requiring $f^\star$ knowledge.

\textbf{Momentum methods (Q2, Section~\ref{sec:q2}).} Nesterov acceleration achieves the theoretically optimal $O(\sqrt{\kappa})$-times savings over gradient descent and provides the best first-order performance on Rosenbrock ($f = 0.479$ vs GD $f = 3.388$, a $7\times$ improvement). Adam excels in high-dimensional, heterogeneous-curvature settings such as neural network training due to per-coordinate adaptive scaling, but its convergence is not guaranteed on general non-convex objectives. Mini-batch SGD introduces a fundamental variance--speed trade-off governed by batch size and observation noise variance.

\textbf{Newton's method (Q3, Section~\ref{sec:q3}).} Second-order information is indispensable for severe ill-conditioning: Newton achieves machine-precision convergence on Benchmark A in \emph{one step}, and reaches $f = 0.686$ on Rosenbrock in 20 iterations versus GD's $f = 3.732$ in 80 iterations. The $O(d^3)$ per-step cost is justified for $d \ll 10^4$; for larger $d$, L-BFGS provides a practical quasi-Newton approximation.

\textbf{Derivative-free methods (Q4, Section~\ref{sec:q4}).} Centred finite differences achieve $O(\delta^2)$ approximation error versus $O(\delta)$ for forward differences, providing a 600-fold accuracy improvement at $\delta = 0.05$. Nesterov random search and Nelder--Mead are valuable when analytic or numerical gradients are unavailable, but both sacrifice convergence rate. Grid search is guaranteed to find the global optimum within grid resolution but scales exponentially with dimension, making it impractical beyond $d \approx 4$.

\textbf{Constrained optimisation (Q5, Section~\ref{sec:q5}).} Projected gradient descent enforces hard feasibility (zero violation from iteration 1) at the cost of requiring a projection oracle. Penalty methods offer simpler gradient-based implementation but achieve only soft feasibility: constraint violation decreases as $O(1/\lambda)$, while larger $\lambda$ increases problem ill-conditioning. The augmented Lagrangian method resolves this by updating the Lagrange multiplier adaptively, achieving exact feasibility with finite $\rho$.

\textbf{Frank--Wolfe (Q6, Section~\ref{sec:q6}).} The conditional gradient algorithm maintains feasibility by convex combination, avoiding expensive projections at the cost of a slower $O(1/k)$ convergence rate (versus projected GD's $O(1/k)$ with better constants). Its natural strength is boundary-constrained problems where it identifies the active constraint automatically. The Frank--Wolfe gap $g_k = \nabla f(x_k)^T(x_k - z_k)$ provides a cheap, computable suboptimality bound without requiring knowledge of $f^\star$. The Away-Step Frank--Wolfe variant upgrades the $O(1/k)$ rate to linear convergence on polytopes by additionally taking ``away steps'' that remove atoms from the active set, at the cost of maintaining the active-set representation throughout.

\textbf{Convergence diagnostics.} Across all methods, three key quantities provide complementary stopping criteria: (1) \emph{gradient norm} $\|\nabla f(x_k)\|$: vanishes at any stationary point; cheap to compute but fails to distinguish saddle points from minima and is scale-dependent; (2) \emph{objective improvement} $|f(x_k) - f(x_{k-1})|$: detects plateaus but is insensitive to slow drifts; (3) \emph{method-specific duality gap} (Frank--Wolfe gap $g_k$; Newton decrement $\lambda(x)^2/2$): provides a certified suboptimality bound without requiring $f^\star$. In practice, multiple criteria are checked simultaneously with appropriate tolerances, and iterations continue until the tightest criterion is satisfied.

In summary, the choice of optimisation algorithm should be guided by the following principles: (i) use Newton or quasi-Newton if the Hessian is computable and $d$ is moderate; (ii) use Nesterov acceleration for ill-conditioned first-order problems; (iii) use Adam for large-scale machine learning with heterogeneous curvature; (iv) use centred FD when gradients are unavailable but function evaluations are cheap; (v) use projected GD for hard constraint satisfaction; and (vi) use Frank--Wolfe when the LP subproblem is cheaper than projection.

def gradient_descent(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        x = x - alpha * grad_fn(x)
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Polyak Step Size}]
def polyak_step(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, f_star, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        alpha_k = (loss_fn(x) - f_star) / (np.dot(g, g) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(alpha_k)
        x = x - alpha_k * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Adagrad}]
def adagrad(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha0, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    G = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        G += g**2
        eff_alpha = alpha0 / (np.sqrt(G) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(np.mean(eff_alpha))
        x = x - eff_alpha * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={RMSprop}]
def rmsprop(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha0, beta, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    v = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        v = beta * v + (1 - beta) * g**2
        eff_alpha = alpha0 / (np.sqrt(v) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(np.mean(eff_alpha))
        x = x - eff_alpha * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Heavy Ball / Polyak Momentum}]
def heavy_ball(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    z = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        z = beta * z + alpha * g
        x = x - z
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Nesterov Momentum}]
def nesterov_momentum(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta_max, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    z = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for k in range(1, n_iters + 1):
        beta_k = min((k - 1) / (k + 2), beta_max)
        lookahead = x + beta_k * z
        g = grad_fn(lookahead)
        z = beta_k * z - alpha * g
        x = x + z
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Adam Optimiser}]
def adam_optimiser(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta1, beta2, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    m_vec, v_vec = np.zeros_like(x), np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for t in range(1, n_iters + 1):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        m_vec = beta1 * m_vec + (1 - beta1) * g
        v_vec = beta2 * v_vec + (1 - beta2) * g**2
        m_hat = m_vec / (1 - beta1**t)
        v_hat = v_vec / (1 - beta2**t)
        x = x - alpha * m_hat / (np.sqrt(v_hat) + eps)
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Newton's Method with Damping and Regularisation}]
def newtons_method(grad_fn, hess_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, n_iters, damping=1e-8):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, update_norms = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        H = hess_fn(x) if callable(hess_fn) else hess_fn
        H_reg = H + damping * np.eye(len(x))
        try:
            p = np.linalg.solve(H_reg, g)
        except np.linalg.LinAlgError:
            p = g  # fall back to steepest descent
        update_norms.append(np.linalg.norm(alpha * p))
        x = x - alpha * p
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(update_norms)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Frank--Wolfe Algorithm}]
def frank_wolfe(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, bounds, beta, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, z_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        z = np.array([bounds[i][0] if g[i] > 0 else bounds[i][1]
                       for i in range(len(g))])
        z_hist.append(z.copy())
        x = beta * x + (1 - beta) * z
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(z_hist)
\end{lstlisting}
