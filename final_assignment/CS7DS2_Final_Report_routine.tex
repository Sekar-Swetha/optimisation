\begin{abstract}
The efficiency of an optimisation algorithm is governed by the interplay between the
algorithm's update rule and the geometric structure of the objective --- condition number,
curvature, and constraint set.  This report provides a systematic comparative study of
first-order, second-order, derivative-free, and constrained optimisation algorithms across
three benchmarks of increasing difficulty: a well-conditioned linear regression quadratic
($\kappa \approx 1.12$, Benchmark A), a moderately anisotropic toy neural-network loss
($\kappa \sim 3.5$--$6.9$, Benchmark B), and the notoriously ill-conditioned Rosenbrock
function ($\kappa \approx 2508$, Benchmark C).  Six categories of methods are investigated:
(Q1)~\emph{adaptive step-size methods} (Polyak, Adagrad, RMSprop, Heavy Ball) ---
Polyak achieves the lowest first-order loss on Rosenbrock ($f \approx 0.024$ vs GD's
$f = 3.51$) when $f^{\star}$ is known, but diverges when it is misspecified;
(Q2)~\emph{momentum and stochastic methods} (Nesterov, Adam, mini-batch SGD) ---
Nesterov's $O(1/k^2)$ optimal rate reduces Rosenbrock loss seven-fold relative to GD;
(Q3)~\emph{Newton's method} --- one step solves the quadratic exactly, and 20 damped
steps reach $f = 0.686$ on Rosenbrock vs GD's $f = 3.732$ in 80 steps;
(Q4)~\emph{derivative-free methods} (finite differences, Nesterov random search,
Nelder--Mead, grid search) --- illustrating the cost of foregoing gradient information;
(Q5)~\emph{constrained optimisation} (projected GD, penalty methods) --- projected GD
guarantees feasibility at every iterate while penalty methods approximate it softly;
(Q6)~\emph{Frank--Wolfe} (conditional gradient) --- a projection-free oracle achieving
$O(LD^2/k)$ convergence on box-constrained problems.
All claims are supported by reproducible Python experiments (\texttt{numpy.random.seed(42)})
with convergence curves, contour trajectories, and cross-method comparisons on all three
benchmarks.
\end{abstract}

\subsection{Benchmark A: Linear Regression Quadratic Loss}
A synthetic dataset with $m = 1000$ samples and two parameters is generated:
\begin{equation}
\theta^{\star} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
x^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_2), \quad
y^{(i)} = \theta^{\star T} x^{(i)} + \varepsilon^{(i)}, \quad
\varepsilon^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1).
\end{equation}
The loss function is the mean squared error:
\begin{equation}
J(\theta) = \frac{1}{2m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \left(\theta^T x^{(i)} - y^{(i)}\right)^2
= \frac{1}{2m}\|X\theta - y\|^2.
\end{equation}
This is a \emph{strictly convex quadratic} with constant Hessian $H = \frac{1}{m}X^T X \succ 0$.
The gradient is $\nabla J(\theta) = \frac{1}{m}X^T(X\theta - y)$.
The exact minimiser (OLS solution) is $\hat{\theta} = (X^TX)^{-1}X^Ty \approx [3.028, 4.017]^T$.
Due to observation noise, the minimum loss is $J^{\star} \approx 0.4826$, not zero.
With $m = 1000$ samples and $X \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)$, the law of large numbers gives
$H \approx I$; numerically, $\lambda_{\min}(H) \approx 0.925$, $\lambda_{\max}(H) \approx 1.033$,
so $\kappa \approx 1.12$ --- a \emph{very well-conditioned} problem where any step
$\alpha \leq 1/\lambda_{\max} \approx 0.97$ is stable.
Starting point: $\theta_0 = [0, 0]^T$.

\subsection{Benchmark B: Toy Neural Network Quadratic Loss}
\begin{equation}
f(x_1, x_2) = (x_1 - 1)^2 + 5(x_2 - 2)^2 + \sin(x_1).
\end{equation}
The gradient is $\nabla f = [2(x_1 - 1) + \cos(x_1),\ 10(x_2 - 2)]^T$ and the Hessian is
$H(x) = \operatorname{diag}(2 - \sin(x_1),\ 10)$. Since $|\sin(x_1)| \leq 1$, the Hessian is
positive definite everywhere (smallest eigenvalue $\geq 1$, largest eigenvalue $= 10$).
The condition number varies: $\kappa(H) = 10/(2-\sin(x_1)) \in [10/3,\ 10]$, creating
\emph{moderate anisotropy}. At the minimum $x^{\star} \approx (0.582, 2.0)$,
$\sin(0.582) \approx 0.549$, giving $\kappa \approx 10/1.45 \approx 6.9$.
Starting point: $x_0 = [-1, 4]^T$, where $\sin(-1) \approx -0.841$ and $\kappa \approx 3.5$.
The function value at the minimum is $f^{\star} \approx 0.7244$.

\subsection{Benchmark C: Rosenbrock Function}
\begin{equation}
f(x_1, x_2) = (1 - x_1)^2 + 100(x_2 - x_1^2)^2.
\end{equation}
The global minimum is at $(1, 1)$ with $f^{\star} = 0$. The gradient is:
\begin{equation}
\nabla f = \begin{bmatrix}
-2(1-x_1) - 400 x_1(x_2 - x_1^2) \\
200(x_2 - x_1^2)
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The Hessian at the optimum $(1,1)$ is:
$H = \begin{bmatrix}802 & -400 \\ -400 & 200\end{bmatrix}$,
with eigenvalues approximately $\{0.4,\ 1001.6\}$, giving condition number
$\kappa \approx 2508$. This \emph{extreme ill-conditioning}, combined with the narrow
curved valley along $x_2 = x_1^2$, makes Rosenbrock one of the most challenging standard test
functions. Starting point: $x_0 = [-1, 1]^T$.

\begin{remark}[Why Rosenbrock is difficult]
The Rosenbrock function $(1-x_1)^2 + 100(x_2 - x_1^2)^2$ has two sources of difficulty:
(1) \textbf{High ill-conditioning}: the Hessian eigenvalues at the minimum
$\approx \{0.4, 1001.6\}$ create a condition number $\kappa \approx 2508$, which forces
gradient descent to use step $\alpha < 2/L \approx 0.002$ for stability while making
negligible progress in the eigenvector direction corresponding to $\lambda_{\min} \approx 0.4$.
(2) \textbf{Curved valley}: the minimum lies along the curve $x_2 = x_1^2$. The gradient
is almost perpendicular to this curve, so gradient steps mostly project onto the valley
walls rather than along it. Methods that exploit curvature (Newton, Polyak with exact
$f^{\star}$) can detect the valley floor direction and take larger steps along it.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Theoretical Background}

The fundamental bottleneck of constant-step gradient descent (GD) is its sensitivity to
the condition number $\kappa = L/\mu$.  For $x_{k+1} = x_k - \alpha \nabla f(x_k)$ on an
$L$-smooth, $\mu$-strongly convex function, $L$-smoothness gives the descent lemma
$f(x_{k+1}) \leq f(x_k) - \alpha\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2 + \frac{\alpha^2 L}{2}\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2$,
while strong convexity gives $\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2 \geq 2\mu[f(x_k) - f^{\star}]$.
Choosing $\alpha = 1/L$ and combining:
\begin{equation}
f(x_{k+1}) - f^{\star} \leq \left(1 - \frac{\mu}{L}\right)[f(x_k) - f^{\star}]
= \left(1 - \frac{1}{\kappa}\right)[f(x_k) - f^{\star}].
\end{equation}
The optimal fixed step $\alpha = 2/(\mu + L)$ tightens this to:
\begin{equation}
f(x_k) - f^{\star} \leq \left(\frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1}\right)^{2k} [f(x_0) - f^{\star}],
\quad \kappa = L/\mu.
\end{equation}
For Rosenbrock with $\kappa \approx 2508$ at the optimum, this factor is $\approx 1 - 0.0008$,
so GD requires $\sim 8600$ iterations to reduce the excess loss by a factor of $e^7$.
This motivates the adaptive methods studied here, which adjust the step to local curvature
without requiring knowledge of $\mu$ and $L$ a priori.

\subsection{Algorithm Formulations}

Four adaptive methods are compared against a constant step-size gradient descent baseline
across all three benchmarks for 120 iterations (pseudocode in
Algorithms~\ref{alg:polyak}--\ref{alg:heavyball}; hyperparameters in
Table~\ref{tab:q1_params}).

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Polyak Step Size}
\label{alg:polyak}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, optimal value $f^{\star}$, tolerance $\varepsilon$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $\alpha_k \gets \dfrac{f(x_k) - f^{\star}}{\|g_k\|^2 + \varepsilon}$ \Comment{Step size adapts to distance from optimum}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha_k\, g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The Polyak step is theoretically motivated by the subgradient inequality: for convex $f$,
$f(x^{\star}) \geq f(x_k) + \langle g_k, x^{\star} - x_k\rangle$, which gives
$\langle g_k, x_k - x^{\star}\rangle \geq f(x_k) - f^{\star}$.
Minimising $\|x_{k+1} - x^{\star}\|^2 = \|x_k - \alpha_k g_k - x^{\star}\|^2$
over $\alpha_k$ and applying the above bound:
\begin{equation}
\|x_{k+1} - x^{\star}\|^2 \leq \|x_k - x^{\star}\|^2 - \frac{(f(x_k)-f^{\star})^2}{\|g_k\|^2}
\quad \text{(when $f^{\star}$ is exact)}.
\end{equation}
The squared-distance to the optimum is thus non-increasing, and the Robbins--Siegmund lemma
implies convergence. When $f^{\star}$ is misspecified (too small), the numerator
fails to vanish as $x_k \to x^{\star}_{\text{true}}$, causing $\alpha_k \to \infty$.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Adagrad}
\label{alg:adagrad}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, base learning rate $\alpha_0$, tolerance $\varepsilon$, iterations $N$
\State $G_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $G_{k+1} \gets G_k + g_k \odot g_k$ \Comment{Per-coordinate cumulative squared gradient}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \dfrac{\alpha_0}{\sqrt{G_{k+1}} + \varepsilon} \odot g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The effective per-coordinate step for Adagrad is
$\alpha_k^{(i)} = \alpha_0 / \sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{k} (g_j^{(i)})^2}$.
Coordinates with consistently large gradients receive smaller steps; rare or small-gradient
coordinates retain larger steps. This is equivalent to \emph{diagonal preconditioning}:
if $D_k = \operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{G_k} + \varepsilon)^{-1}$, then
$x_{k+1} = x_k - \alpha_0 D_k g_k$, which approximates Newton's step when
$D_k \approx [\nabla^2 f(x_k)]^{-1}$ (true when the Hessian is approximately diagonal
with its diagonal proportional to the accumulated squared gradients).
This makes Adagrad well-suited to sparse or anisotropic problems. However, the
monotonically \emph{decreasing} step can cause premature slowdown on dense problems
(because $G_k \to \infty$ coordinate-wise), which RMSprop addresses.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{RMSprop}
\label{alg:rmsprop}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, learning rate $\alpha_0$, decay $\beta$, tolerance $\varepsilon$, iterations $N$
\State $v_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $v_{k+1} \gets \beta\, v_k + (1 - \beta)\, g_k \odot g_k$ \Comment{Exponential moving average of $g^2$}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \dfrac{\alpha_0}{\sqrt{v_{k+1}} + \varepsilon} \odot g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

RMSprop replaces Adagrad's \emph{cumulative} sum with an \emph{exponential moving average}
of squared gradients (decay factor $\beta = 0.9$), giving the denominator an effective memory
of approximately $1/(1-\beta) = 10$ recent gradient magnitudes. This prevents the step size
from decaying to zero on non-stationary or dense problems.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Polyak Momentum / Heavy Ball}
\label{alg:heavyball}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, momentum $\beta$, iterations $N$
\State $z_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets \beta\, z_k + \alpha\, \nabla f(x_k)$ \Comment{Accumulate gradient with momentum}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - z_{k+1}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The Heavy Ball method builds momentum by accumulating past gradients with exponential
decay $\beta$. In consistent gradient directions, the momentum $z_k$ grows, accelerating
convergence. In oscillating directions (e.g.\ across a valley), it averages out, reducing
zig-zagging. For a quadratic with condition number $\kappa$, the optimal $(\alpha, \beta)$
give convergence factor $\left(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa}+1}\right)^2$,
dramatically faster than $\left(\frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1}\right)$ for GD.

\subsection{Comparative Analysis}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Convergence speed:} When $f^{\star}$ is known exactly, Polyak converges fastest
  (Rosenbrock: $f \approx 0.024$ at final iteration, reaching as low as $f \approx 0.007$ at
  intermediate steps, vs GD's $f = 3.51$ in 120 iterations --- a $\sim$150$\times$ improvement).
  Otherwise, Adagrad and Heavy Ball match or exceed the GD baseline. The momentum in Heavy
  Ball provides approximately $O\bigl((\sqrt{\kappa}-1)/(\sqrt{\kappa}+1)\bigr)^{2k}$ vs
  GD's $O\bigl((\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1)\bigr)^k$ convergence factor for quadratics
  (for $\kappa \gg 1$, this is roughly $O(e^{-4k/\sqrt{\kappa}})$ vs $O(e^{-2k/\kappa})$).
\item \textbf{Smoothness:} RMSprop produces the smoothest convergence curves due to its
  exponential moving average (effective memory $\approx 1/(1-\beta) = 10$ steps).
  Heavy Ball exhibits characteristic overshoot followed by damped oscillations.
\item \textbf{Stability:} GD baseline is most stable but slowest. Polyak is \emph{unstable}
  when $f^{\star}$ is misspecified --- a critical practical limitation. Adagrad is
  uniformly stable due to its monotonically decreasing step size, at the cost of
  eventual stagnation for large-scale problems.
\item \textbf{Adaptivity to ill-conditioning:} Adagrad and RMSprop rescale per-coordinate,
  effectively pre-conditioning the gradient by the diagonal of a running Hessian estimate.
  For Rosenbrock, the Hessian diagonal $h_{11} = 2 + 1200x_1^2 - 400x_2$ varies by
  factors of $\sim 100$ along the trajectory, making per-coordinate scaling especially
  valuable.
\item \textbf{Practical implication:} Since exact $f^{\star}$ is rarely available,
  RMSprop and Adagrad are safer defaults for problems with dense, anisotropic gradients.
  Heavy Ball with tuned $(\alpha, \beta)$ is competitive and does not require $f^{\star}$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Algorithm Formulations}

Three methods are compared against the GD baseline on all three benchmarks
(pseudocode in Algorithms~\ref{alg:nesterov}--\ref{alg:sgd}; hyperparameters
in Table~\ref{tab:q2_params}).

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Nesterov Momentum / Acceleration}
\label{alg:nesterov}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, max momentum $\beta_{\max}$, iterations $N$
\State $z_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 1, 2, \ldots, N$}
    \State $\beta_k \gets \min\!\left(\frac{k-1}{k+2},\ \beta_{\max}\right)$ \Comment{Increasing momentum schedule}
    \State $\tilde{x}_k \gets x_k + \beta_k\, z_k$ \Comment{Lookahead point}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets \beta_k\, z_k - \alpha\, \nabla f(\tilde{x}_k)$ \Comment{Update velocity at lookahead}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k + z_{k+1}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The key distinction from Heavy Ball is the \emph{lookahead}: the gradient is evaluated at
$\tilde{x}_k = x_k + \beta_k z_k$ rather than $x_k$. This allows Nesterov acceleration to
\emph{correct} its trajectory before overshooting. The adaptive schedule
$\beta_k = (k-1)/(k+2)$ increases momentum gradually, achieving an optimal $O(1/k^2)$
convergence rate on smooth convex functions:

\begin{theorem}[Nesterov Convergence, informal]
\label{thm:nesterov}
For an $L$-smooth convex function $f$ and the Nesterov step $\alpha = 1/L$:
\begin{equation}
f(x_k) - f^{\star} \leq \frac{2L\|x_0 - x^{\star}\|^2}{(k+1)^2}.
\end{equation}
This is optimal among first-order methods (matching the information-theoretic lower bound).
\end{theorem}

Compared to GD's $O(1/k)$ rate (or the linear rate $O(\rho^k)$ for strongly convex $f$),
the $O(1/k^2)$ rate represents a qualitative improvement that manifests as substantially
faster convergence on moderately ill-conditioned problems.

\begin{remark}[Information-theoretic optimality]
Nesterov (1983) established that \emph{no} first-order method (using only gradient
evaluations $\nabla f(x_0), \ldots, \nabla f(x_{k-1})$) can achieve better than
$O(1/k^2)$ on $L$-smooth convex functions in the worst case.  The lower bound is attained
by the adversarial quadratic $f(x) = \frac{L}{2}(x_1^2 + \sum_{i=1}^{k-1}(x_i - x_{i+1})^2 - x_k^2)$
which is designed so that any first-order method needs $\Omega(1/k^2)$ excess loss after
$k$ steps starting from the origin.  Nesterov acceleration matches this lower bound,
making it the \emph{optimal} first-order algorithm for this problem class.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Why $\beta_k = (k-1)/(k+2)$ is optimal]
The momentum schedule $\beta_k = (k-1)/(k+2)$ is derived from the Lyapunov function
$\Phi_k = (k+1)^2[f(x_k) - f^{\star}] + 2L\|z_k\|^2$ where $z_k$ is the dual variable.
A direct computation shows $\Phi_{k+1} \leq \Phi_k$ when this exact schedule is used,
yielding the telescoping bound $\Phi_k \leq \Phi_0$ and hence
$f(x_k) - f^{\star} \leq \Phi_0 / (k+1)^2 = O(1/k^2)$.
Any fixed momentum $\beta < 1$ (as in Heavy Ball) cannot achieve this rate on general
non-quadratic functions because it does not allow the corrective ``look-ahead'' to cancel
the accumulated momentum error.
\end{remark}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Adam Optimiser}
\label{alg:adam}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, learning rate $\alpha$, moments $\beta_1, \beta_2$, tolerance $\varepsilon$, iterations $N$
\State $m_0 \gets \mathbf{0},\ v_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$t = 1, 2, \ldots, N$}
    \State $g_t \gets \nabla f(x_t)$
    \State $m_t \gets \beta_1\, m_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_1)\, g_t$ \Comment{First moment (mean)}
    \State $v_t \gets \beta_2\, v_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_2)\, g_t \odot g_t$ \Comment{Second moment (uncentred variance)}
    \State $\hat{m}_t \gets m_t\, /\, (1 - \beta_1^t)$ \Comment{Bias correction}
    \State $\hat{v}_t \gets v_t\, /\, (1 - \beta_2^t)$
    \State $x_{t+1} \gets x_t - \alpha\, \hat{m}_t\, /\, (\sqrt{\hat{v}_t} + \varepsilon)$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Adam combines two ideas: (i) first-moment tracking ($m_t$) gives momentum similar to
Heavy Ball; (ii) second-moment tracking ($v_t$) gives per-coordinate adaptive scaling
similar to RMSprop. The bias-correction factors $(1 - \beta_1^t)^{-1}$ and
$(1 - \beta_2^t)^{-1}$ compensate for the initialisation at zero, which would otherwise
bias the early iterates. As $t \to \infty$, the bias corrections approach 1 and Adam
reduces to a momentum-RMSprop hybrid.

\begin{remark}[Adam's convergence bound]
While Nesterov achieves the optimal $O(1/k^2)$ rate on smooth convex functions, the
best-known theoretical bound for Adam on general convex problems is $O(1/\sqrt{T})$
(Reddi et al., 2018 showed that the original Adam can diverge on convex problems with
certain step schedule choices; AMSGrad, a corrected variant, achieves $O(1/\sqrt{T})$
rigorously). In practice, Adam often converges faster than this bound suggests, especially
in non-convex settings such as deep learning, where the adaptive per-coordinate scaling
handles the heterogeneous curvature across many layers. However, on the smooth, strongly
convex Rosenbrock function, Nesterov's superior $O(1/k^2)$ rate gives it a clear
empirical advantage (Rosenbrock: $f = 0.479$ vs Adam's $f = 1.578$ after 150 iterations).
\end{remark}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Mini-Batch SGD}
\label{alg:sgd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Data $(X, y)$, initial $\theta_0$, learning rate $\alpha$, batch size $b$, epochs $E$
\For{$e = 1, 2, \ldots, E$}
    \State Randomly permute data indices $\pi$
    \For{each mini-batch $\mathcal{B} = \{\pi_{ib+1}, \ldots, \pi_{(i+1)b}\}$}
        \State $g \gets \frac{1}{b} X_{\mathcal{B}}^T(X_{\mathcal{B}}\theta - y_{\mathcal{B}})$
        \State $\theta \gets \theta - \alpha\, g$
    \EndFor
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Nesterov vs Heavy Ball:} Both use momentum, but Nesterov's lookahead

  self-correction before overshooting. This gives Nesterov its $O(1/k^2)$ theoretical
  guarantee, whereas Heavy Ball is optimal only for quadratics and can diverge on
  nonlinear problems. On Rosenbrock, Nesterov achieves $f = 0.479$ vs Heavy Ball's
  $f = 1.400$ (both 120--150 iterations), confirming the theoretical advantage.
\item \textbf{Adam vs RMSprop:} Adam adds first-moment tracking ($m_t$) to RMSprop's
  adaptive scaling ($v_t$). The first moment provides momentum that accelerates convergence
  in the early phase; the bias correction terms prevent underestimation of step size
  in early iterations when $m_t, v_t$ are small. In practice, Adam requires less step
  size tuning than RMSprop for similar performance.
\item \textbf{Batch size trade-off (SGD):} The gradient variance of a mini-batch estimator
  scales as $\sigma^2_{\text{SGD}} \approx \sigma^2_{\text{full}} / b$, where $b$ is
  the batch size. Smaller $b$ gives more updates per epoch (200 vs 25 for $b=5$ vs $b=40$)
  but higher per-update variance. Near the minimum, this variance dominates convergence,
  which explains why both batch sizes approach similar final loss values after 50 epochs.
\item \textbf{Noise effect on SGD:} With $\sigma = 6$ (factor-6 noise), the irreducible
  floor rises by factor $36$: $J^{\star}(\sigma=6) = 36 \times J^{\star}(\sigma=1)
  \approx 36 \times 0.48 \approx 17.4$. Under this high noise, the gradient signal-to-noise
  ratio drops, and the batch size effect (which matters only for reducing sampling variance)
  becomes negligible relative to the dominant observation noise variance.
\item \textbf{Key result:} Nesterov outperforms Adam on Rosenbrock by $3.3\times$
  ($f = 0.479$ vs $f = 1.578$). On smooth convex problems, Adam's adaptive scaling
  introduces a bias that limits its convergence rate to $O(1/\sqrt{k})$ in the worst case,
  whereas Nesterov achieves the optimal $O(1/k^2)$ rate.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Local Approximations of $g(x) = x^4$}

Whereas first-order methods use only the gradient to construct a linear model of $f$,
Newton's method fits a full second-order (quadratic) model at each iterate and minimises
it exactly.  The quality of this local model determines Newton's convergence rate: when
$f$ is precisely quadratic, the model is exact and one step reaches the minimum; when
curvature varies, the model error limits convergence but second-order information still
confers a substantial speed advantage over first-order methods.

Figure~\ref{fig:q3_approx} illustrates the first- and second-order approximations at $x_0 = 0.25$.
At $x_0 = 0.25$, we compute:
$g(x_0) = 0.25^4 = 0.00391$,\;
$g'(x_0) = 4 \cdot 0.25^3 = 0.0625$,\;
$g''(x_0) = 12 \cdot 0.25^2 = 0.750$.

\textbf{First-order (Taylor) approximation:}
\begin{equation}
g(x) \approx g(x_0) + g'(x_0)(x - x_0) = 0.00391 + 0.0625\,(x - 0.25).
\end{equation}

\textbf{Second-order approximation:}
\begin{equation}
g(x) \approx 0.00391 + 0.0625\,(x - 0.25) + 0.375\,(x - 0.25)^2.
\end{equation}

The Newton update minimises the second-order approximation by setting its derivative to zero:
\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{dx}\left[g(x_0) + g'(x_0)(x - x_0) + \tfrac{1}{2}g''(x_0)(x-x_0)^2\right] = 0
\implies x_{k+1} = x_k - \frac{g'(x_k)}{g''(x_k)}.
\end{equation}
This corresponds to stepping to the stationary point of the local quadratic model, yielding a
step that is automatically scaled by the local curvature $g''(x_k)$.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/q3_approximation.pdf}
\caption{Q3: Local approximations of $g(x) = x^4$ at $x_0 = 0.25$. The first-order
approximation (tangent line) captures only the local slope and diverges rapidly. The
second-order approximation (parabola, $g'' = 0.750$) captures local curvature and fits
well in a neighbourhood of $x_0$. The minimum of this parabola at
$x_{\min} = x_0 - g'(x_0)/g''(x_0) = 0.25 - 0.0625/0.75 \approx 0.167$ provides
a much better estimate of the function minimum than a pure gradient step.}
\label{fig:q3_approx}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Newton's Method Applied to Benchmark Functions}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:newton} gives the pseudocode for damped Newton's method; hyperparameters
are in Table~\ref{tab:q3_params}. Results are reported in Table~\ref{tab:q3_final} and
Figures~\ref{fig:q3_fval_A}--\ref{fig:q3_fval_C}.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Newton's Method with Damping}
\label{alg:newton}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, Hessian regularisation $\lambda$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $H_k \gets \nabla^2 f(x_k) + \lambda I$ \Comment{Regularised Hessian (Tikhonov)}
    \State $p_k \gets H_k^{-1}\, g_k$ \Comment{Newton direction}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, p_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Settings: GD uses 80 iterations; Newton uses only 20 iterations with Hessian damping
$\lambda = 10^{-8}$ (for numerical stability). The damped Newton step $\alpha < 1$ guards
against instability when the Hessian changes rapidly (Benchmark C).

\begin{proposition}[Newton's method is exact for quadratics]
For a strictly convex quadratic $J(\theta) = \frac{1}{2}\theta^T H\theta - b^T\theta + c$
with constant Hessian $H \succ 0$, the Newton step $\theta_1 = \theta_0 - H^{-1}\nabla J(\theta_0)$
yields the global minimiser $\theta^{\star} = H^{-1}b$ in a single step, regardless of $\theta_0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$\nabla J(\theta_0) = H\theta_0 - b$, so
$\theta_1 = \theta_0 - H^{-1}(H\theta_0 - b) = H^{-1}b = \theta^{\star}$.
\end{proof}

GD requires 80 iterations to reach comparable accuracy, demonstrating the power of
second-order information on quadratic problems.

\begin{theorem}[Newton's quadratic convergence]
\label{thm:newton}
Let $f$ be twice continuously differentiable, $\nabla^2 f$ Lipschitz-continuous with
constant $M$ in a neighbourhood of the minimiser $x^{\star}$, and suppose
$\nabla^2 f(x^{\star}) \succ 0$ with smallest eigenvalue $\mu > 0$.  For the full Newton
step ($\alpha = 1$) and $x_0$ sufficiently close to $x^{\star}$:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:newton_quad}
  \|x_{k+1} - x^{\star}\| \;\leq\; \frac{M}{2\mu}\,\|x_k - x^{\star}\|^2.
\end{equation}
This \emph{quadratic convergence} means the number of correct decimal digits roughly
doubles each iteration.  For damped Newton ($\alpha < 1$) or far from the minimum,
\eqref{eq:newton_quad} does not apply; a global phase with linear-rate convergence
(Armijo backtracking guarantees $f(x_{k+1}) \leq f(x_k) - c\alpha_k\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2$)
precedes the local quadratic phase.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Comparative Summary}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Quadratic functions:} Newton is exact in one step (Theorem~\ref{thm:newton}
  with $M = 0$). This is an extreme advantage over all first-order methods.
\item \textbf{Near-quadratic functions (Benchmark B):} Newton converges in $O(5)$ steps vs
  GD's $O(80)$ steps --- a $16\times$ reduction.
\item \textbf{Highly nonlinear functions (Rosenbrock):} Newton still substantially outperforms
  GD but requires damping ($\alpha < 1$) to maintain stability, losing the full quadratic
  convergence rate (the global phase dominates in 20 iterations).
\item \textbf{Computational cost:} Each Newton step requires forming and inverting the Hessian:
  $O(n^2)$ memory and $O(n^3)$ arithmetic (or $O(n^2)$ with structured factorisation). For
  large-scale problems, quasi-Newton methods (BFGS, L-BFGS) approximate the Hessian without
  full computation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Quasi-Newton Methods: Bridging First- and Second-Order}

Full Newton requires computing and factorising the $n \times n$ Hessian at every step,
costing $O(n^3)$ per iteration. \emph{Quasi-Newton} methods maintain an approximation
$B_k \approx \nabla^2 f(x_k)$ (or its inverse $H_k \approx [\nabla^2 f(x_k)]^{-1}$)
that is updated cheaply from gradient differences using the \emph{secant condition}:
\begin{equation}
B_{k+1}(x_{k+1} - x_k) = \nabla f(x_{k+1}) - \nabla f(x_k).
\end{equation}
The BFGS update formula maintains positive definiteness of $B_k$ via a rank-2 correction:
\begin{equation}
B_{k+1} = B_k - \frac{B_k s_k s_k^T B_k}{s_k^T B_k s_k} + \frac{y_k y_k^T}{y_k^T s_k},
\quad s_k = x_{k+1} - x_k,\quad y_k = \nabla f(x_{k+1}) - \nabla f(x_k).
\end{equation}
This update costs $O(n^2)$ per step rather than $O(n^3)$. In terms of convergence,
BFGS achieves \emph{super-linear} convergence ($\|e_{k+1}\| / \|e_k\| \to 0$),
which is strictly faster than linear (GD) but slower than Newton's quadratic rate.
For the 2D problems in this report, the $O(n^3)$ cost of Newton is negligible ---
quasi-Newton methods become essential for $n \gtrsim 10^4$ parameters, such as
training large neural networks or fitting high-dimensional statistical models.
L-BFGS (limited-memory BFGS) retains only the last $m \in \{5,\ldots,20\}$ gradient
differences, reducing storage from $O(n^2)$ to $O(mn)$ while preserving the
super-linear convergence rate in practice.

\begin{proposition}[BFGS super-linear convergence]
\label{prop:bfgs}
Let $f$ be twice continuously differentiable with $\nabla^2 f$ Lipschitz-continuous in a
neighbourhood of a strong local minimum $x^{\star}$ (where $\nabla^2 f(x^{\star}) \succ 0$).
Starting sufficiently close to $x^{\star}$, BFGS satisfies
\[
  \lim_{k\to\infty} \frac{\|x_{k+1} - x^{\star}\|}{\|x_k - x^{\star}\|} = 0,
\]
i.e.\ \emph{super-linear} convergence, without requiring Hessian computation.  The
Dennis--Mor\'e condition governing this rate is
$\lim_{k\to\infty}\|(\nabla^2 f(x^{\star}) - B_k)s_k\| / \|s_k\| = 0$: the Hessian
approximation need not converge globally, only along the step directions.
\end{proposition}
\begin{remark}
For the 2D problems here ($n=2$) the $O(n^3)$ cost of full Newton is negligible.
BFGS becomes essential at $n \gtrsim 10^4$ (e.g.\ deep neural network training), where
the $O(n^2)$ per-step cost and $O(n)$ L-BFGS storage are critical advantages.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Part A: Finite Difference and Nesterov Random Search (Benchmark B)}

\subsubsection{Theoretical Framework}

\textbf{Finite difference gradient approximation.}
The forward finite-difference approximation replaces each partial derivative with:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x) \approx \frac{f(x + \delta e_i) - f(x)}{\delta}.
\end{equation}
The approximation error has two competing components:
\begin{equation}
\left|\hat{g}_i - \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x)\right|
\leq \underbrace{\frac{L_2 \,\delta}{2}}_{\text{truncation error}}\;
+ \underbrace{\frac{2\varepsilon_{\text{mach}} \|f\|}{\delta}}_{\text{rounding error}},
\end{equation}
where $L_2 = \max_x \|\nabla^2 f(x)\|$ bounds the second derivative and
$\varepsilon_{\text{mach}} \approx 10^{-16}$ is machine epsilon.

\begin{proposition}[Optimal FD step size]
\label{prop:fd_opt}
Minimising the total FD error bound $E(\delta) = \tfrac{L_2 \delta}{2} + 2\varepsilon_{\rm mach}\|f\|/\delta$
over $\delta > 0$ gives the optimal step
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:delta_opt}
  \delta_{\rm opt} = \sqrt{\frac{4\varepsilon_{\rm mach}\|f\|}{L_2}} \approx \sqrt{\varepsilon_{\rm mach}} \approx 10^{-8},
\end{equation}
yielding a minimum error of $E(\delta_{\rm opt}) = \sqrt{4 L_2 \varepsilon_{\rm mach}\|f\|} = O(\varepsilon_{\rm mach}^{1/2})$.
Central differences halve the truncation term ($L_3\delta^2/6$ instead of $L_2\delta/2$),
giving $\delta_{\rm opt}^{\rm central} \approx \varepsilon_{\rm mach}^{1/3} \approx 10^{-5}$.
\end{proposition}
The assignment tests $\delta = 0.05$ (near-optimal given function scale, close to
$\delta_{\rm opt}$ in \eqref{eq:delta_opt}) and $\delta = 0.8$
(large truncation error dominates, gradient quality is poor).

An important consequence: when $\delta = 0.8$, the FD gradient direction may be
\emph{non-descent} for the true $f$. The FD-gradient method with large $\delta$
therefore converges to a \emph{perturbed stationary point} of the smoothed function
$f_\delta(x) = \mathbb{E}_{\xi \sim \text{Unif}(B(0,\delta))}[f(x + \xi)]$, which
differs from the true minimiser by $O(\delta^2)$. For $\delta = 0.8$, this error
floor $O(\delta^2) \approx 0.64$ prevents the method from converging close to $f^{\star}$.

\subsubsection{Nesterov Random Search}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:nrs} gives the pseudocode for Nesterov random search.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Nesterov Random Search (Zeroth-Order)}
\label{alg:nrs}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, perturbation $\delta$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State Sample $u_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_n)$; normalise: $u_k \gets u_k / \|u_k\|$
    \State $\hat{g}_k \gets \dfrac{f(x_k + \delta u_k) - f(x_k)}{\delta}\, u_k$ \Comment{Directional derivative estimate}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \hat{g}_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

This zeroth-order method estimates the gradient by probing along a single random direction,
requiring only \emph{two} function evaluations per iteration regardless of dimension $n$.
The expected value of $\hat{g}_k$ is a smoothed gradient: $\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_k] = \nabla f_\delta(x_k)$
where $f_\delta$ is the Gaussian-smoothed version of $f$. However, only one direction is
probed per step, so the variance of $\hat{g}_k$ is high --- convergence requires $O(n)$
times more iterations than gradient-based methods in dimension $n$.

\subsection{Part B: Derivative-Free Methods (Benchmark C)}

\subsubsection{Nelder--Mead Simplex Method}

The Nelder--Mead algorithm maintains a simplex of $n+1 = 3$ vertices in $\mathbb{R}^2$
and iteratively transforms it through four geometric operations:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Reflection} ($\alpha_r = 1.0$): Move the \emph{worst} vertex $x_{n+1}$ (highest
  $f$ value) through the centroid $\bar{x}$ of the remaining vertices:
  $x_r = \bar{x} + \alpha_r(\bar{x} - x_{n+1})$.
\item \textbf{Expansion} ($\gamma = 2.0$): If the reflected point is the \emph{new best},
  try an even larger step: $x_e = \bar{x} + \gamma(\bar{x} - x_{n+1})$.
\item \textbf{Contraction} ($\rho = 0.5$): If reflection \emph{worsened} relative to
  the second-worst vertex, contract the worst vertex towards the centroid.
\item \textbf{Shrink} ($\sigma = 0.5$): If contraction also fails, shrink \emph{all}
  vertices towards the current best: $x_i \leftarrow x_1 + \sigma(x_i - x_1)$ for $i > 1$.
\end{enumerate}
No gradient information is required. The simplex adaptively elongates along promising
directions (frequent reflection/expansion) and contracts in poorly-explored directions
(shrink). Settings: initial simplex step $= 0.35$, 160 iterations (Figure~\ref{fig:q4_nm}).

For Rosenbrock, the initial simplex step of 0.35 creates a simplex large enough to
``sense'' the valley geometry. The algorithm first contracts into the valley (early
contraction operations), then elongates along the valley floor (reflection/expansion),
mimicking the gradient-descent trajectory but without requiring $\nabla f$.

\subsection{Problem Setup and KKT Conditions}

Minimise Benchmark B with the constraint $x_1 \geq 0.5$, starting from the infeasible
point $x_0 = (0.2, 4.0)$:
\begin{equation}
\min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^2} f(x_1, x_2) = (x_1 - 1)^2 + 5(x_2 - 2)^2 + \sin(x_1)
\quad \text{subject to } g(x) = 0.5 - x_1 \leq 0.
\end{equation}
The Karush--Kuhn--Tucker (KKT) conditions at the constrained minimiser $x^{\star}$ are:
\begin{align}
\nabla f(x^{\star}) + \mu^{\star} \nabla g(x^{\star}) &= 0, \label{eq:kkt_stationary} \\
g(x^{\star}) \leq 0 &\quad\text{(primal feasibility)}, \\
\mu^{\star} \geq 0 &\quad\text{(dual feasibility)}, \\
\mu^{\star} g(x^{\star}) = 0 &\quad\text{(complementary slackness)}.
\end{align}
Since the unconstrained minimum $x^{\star}_{\text{unc}} \approx (0.582, 2.0)$ satisfies
$0.582 > 0.5$, it lies in the interior of the feasible set and is also the constrained
minimum. Complementary slackness gives $\mu^{\star} = 0$ (inactive constraint), so
the stationarity condition~\eqref{eq:kkt_stationary} reduces to the unconstrained
$\nabla f(x^{\star}) = 0$.

Two algorithms are studied: Algorithm~\ref{alg:pgd} (Projected GD) and
Algorithm~\ref{alg:penalty} (Penalty Method). Results are summarised in
Table~\ref{tab:q5_final}; Figures~\ref{fig:q5_fval}, \ref{fig:q5_contour},
and~\ref{fig:q5_violation} show convergence, trajectories, and constraint violation.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Projected Gradient Descent}
\label{alg:pgd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, projection $\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \nabla f(x_k)$ \Comment{Unconstrained gradient step}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets \Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(z_{k+1})$ \Comment{Project: $x_1 \leftarrow \max(0.5,\, x_1)$}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The projection $\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(z) = (\max(0.5, z_1), z_2)$ is a $O(1)$ operation for
this half-space constraint. More general constraints (e.g.\ the positive semidefinite cone)
have expensive projections, making the projection oracle the bottleneck.

\begin{proposition}[Projected GD convergence]
\label{prop:pgd_conv}
Let $f$ be $L$-smooth and $\mu$-strongly convex, and let $\mathcal{X}$ be a closed convex
set with unconstrained minimiser $x^{\star} \in \mathcal{X}$.  With step size
$\alpha \leq 1/L$, projected gradient descent satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:pgd_rate}
  \|x_{k+1} - x^{\star}\|^2
  \;\leq\;
  \bigl(1 - \alpha\mu\bigr)\,\|x_k - x^{\star}\|^2,
\end{equation}
yielding linear convergence at rate $(1-\mu/L)^k$ for $\alpha = 1/L$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Sketch]
By the non-expansiveness of $\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}$ and $\mu$-strong convexity:
$\|x_{k+1}-x^{\star}\|^2 = \|\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_k - \alpha g_k) - \Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x^{\star})\|^2
\leq \|x_k - \alpha g_k - x^{\star}\|^2
\leq (1-\alpha\mu)\|x_k-x^{\star}\|^2 - \alpha(1-\alpha L/2)\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2$.
Dropping the non-positive gradient norm term gives \eqref{eq:pgd_rate}.
\end{proof}
\begin{remark}
When $x^{\star}$ lies strictly inside $\mathcal{X}$ (constraint inactive), the projection
never fires after the first step and projected GD reduces to unconstrained GD.  This
is precisely the situation in Benchmark~B: the unconstrained minimiser $(0.582, 2.00)$
satisfies $x_1 > 0.5$, so all methods reach the same objective value
$f^{\star} \approx 0.7244$ (see Table~\ref{tab:q5_final}).
\end{remark}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Penalty Method}
\label{alg:penalty}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, penalty weight $\lambda$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $F_{\lambda}(x) \gets f(x) + \lambda \max(0,\, 0.5 - x_1)$ \Comment{$L_1$ exterior penalty}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \nabla_x F_{\lambda}(x_k)$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The penalty gradient $\nabla_x F_\lambda$ is:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial F_\lambda}{\partial x_1} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1} - \lambda \cdot \mathbf{1}[x_1 < 0.5],
\qquad
\frac{\partial F_\lambda}{\partial x_2} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2}.
\end{equation}
The $L_1$ penalty is non-smooth at $x_1 = 0.5$, which can cause oscillation in the
iterates near the constraint boundary. The true constrained minimiser is recovered
only as $\lambda \to \infty$.

\textbf{Settings:} Projected GD ($\alpha = 0.08$), Penalty with $\lambda \in \{0.15, 1.8, 4.5\}$
($\alpha = 0.05$ for $\lambda \leq 1.8$; $\alpha = 0.03$ for $\lambda = 4.5$),
Unconstrained GD ($\alpha = 0.07$). All run for 100 iterations.

\textbf{Projected GD} enforces feasibility immediately and permanently. The projection
oracle is $O(1)$ for the half-space $\{x_1 \geq 0.5\}$ and projects any gradient step
back to the feasible set. The fundamental advantage is that feasibility is a \emph{hard
guarantee}: the iterate $x_k$ satisfies the constraint at every iteration by construction.

\textbf{Penalty methods} trade exact feasibility for algorithmic simplicity. The weight
$\lambda$ controls the \emph{speed} of achieving feasibility:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Small $\lambda = 0.15$:} At the starting point $x_0 = (0.2, 4.0)$, the
  violation $0.5 - 0.2 = 0.3$ incurs a penalty gradient of $-0.15$ in the $x_1$ direction,
  while the objective gradient $\partial f/\partial x_1 \approx -0.62$ at $x_0$ dominates.
  The net $x_1$-gradient is $-0.62 + 0.15 = -0.47$, still pointing towards the infeasible
  region in the first step. Feasibility is eventually achieved (around iteration 50) as the
  iterate moves closer to the true minimum and the penalty term grows relative to the
  objective gradient.
\item \textbf{Large $\lambda = 4.5$:} The penalty gradient $-4.5$ in the $x_1$ direction
  overwhelms the objective gradient $-0.62$, giving net gradient $-0.62 + 4.5 = +3.88$
  --- strongly pointing towards feasibility from the first iteration. This large gradient
  requires reducing $\alpha$ to $0.03$ to prevent instability. Feasibility is reached
  within $\sim$10 iterations.
\end{itemize}
In this particular problem, all penalty methods ultimately converge to the same solution
$f = 0.7244$ since the constraint is \emph{inactive} at the true minimum ($x_1^{\star} \approx 0.582 > 0.5$).
The distinction appears primarily in the \emph{path}: how quickly each method becomes feasible.
\begin{remark}[Active vs inactive constraints]
Because the unconstrained minimum $x^{\star}_{\text{unc}} = (0.582, 2.0)$ satisfies
$x_1^{\star} > 0.5$, the constraint is \emph{inactive} and all methods find the same
solution. The interesting case arises when the constraint is \emph{active}, e.g., if
the constraint were $x_1 \geq 0.7 > 0.582$. Then the constrained minimum would be
$(0.7, 2.0)$ with $f = 0.7^2 - 2 \times 0.7 + 1 + \sin(0.7) = 0.09 + 0.644 = 0.734$,
and penalty methods with small $\lambda$ would fail to converge to the correct solution ---
demonstrating the necessity of sufficiently large $\lambda$ for active constraints.
\end{remark}

A more principled approach is the \emph{augmented Lagrangian method} (ALM), which
adds both a penalty and a Lagrange multiplier estimate:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:alm_obj}
F_{\rho,\mu}(x) = f(x) + \mu \max(0,\, g(x)) + \frac{\rho}{2}[\max(0,\, g(x))]^2.
\end{equation}
The multiplier $\mu$ is updated between outer iterations as
$\mu^{(t+1)} \leftarrow \mu^{(t)} + \rho\, g(x^{(t)}_\star)$, where $x^{(t)}_\star$
minimises $F_{\rho,\mu^{(t)}}$.

\begin{proposition}[ALM convergence]
\label{prop:alm}
Under constraint qualification (LICQ) at $x^{\star}$, the ALM dual ascent update drives
$\mu^{(t)} \to \mu^{\star}$ at a linear rate $|\mu^{(t)} - \mu^{\star}| \leq C(1 - \rho/c)^t$
for some $c > 0$ depending on the problem.  At the true multiplier $\mu^{\star}$, the ALM
subproblem is equivalent to the constrained problem at finite $\rho$, whereas the pure
penalty method requires $\rho \to \infty$ to enforce the constraint exactly.  This is the
fundamental advantage of maintaining a dual estimate.
\end{proposition}
\begin{remark}
The penalty sub-problems in \eqref{eq:alm_obj} may be ill-conditioned for large $\rho$
(condition number grows as $O(\rho)$ for equality constraints), but the ALM keeps $\rho$
moderate while achieving exact feasibility through the multiplier update.
ADMM (Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers) is a popular distributed variant that
splits the primal update into two alternating steps, enabling parallel computation over
separable constraint structures, with applications in compressed sensing and distributed
machine learning.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Feasible Set and Linear Programme}

The Frank--Wolfe algorithm (also called the conditional gradient method) addresses
constrained optimisation over a compact convex set $\mathcal{X}$ by replacing the
expensive projection step of projected GD with a \emph{linear minimisation oracle}
(LMO): at each iteration it solves $z_k = \arg\min_{z \in \mathcal{X}} \langle \nabla f(x_k), z \rangle$.
For a box $\mathcal{X}$, the LMO admits an explicit $O(d)$ solution (clip each coordinate
to the bound in the gradient direction), making it far cheaper than projection onto
general convex sets (e.g.\ nuclear norm ball: $O(d^3)$).

Feasible set: $\mathcal{X} = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^2 : 0.5 \leq x_1 \leq 5,\ -5 \leq x_2 \leq 10\}$
(a box / rectangle in $\mathbb{R}^2$).

\textbf{Linear Programme:} For $f(x) = a^T x$ with $a = [1, 2]^T$, minimise over $\mathcal{X}$.
Since $f$ is linear, its minimum over a bounded polytope is attained at a \emph{vertex}.
Both components of $a$ are positive, so minimising $a^T x$ requires minimising each
coordinate at its lower bound:
\begin{equation}
x^{\star} = (0.5,\, -5), \qquad f^{\star} = a^T x^{\star} = 0.5 + 2(-5) = -9.5.
\end{equation}
This is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:q6_lp} and illustrates the \textbf{fundamental
theorem of linear programming}: the optimum of a linear objective over a bounded convex
polytope is attained at a vertex of the polytope.

\subsection{Frank--Wolfe Algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:fw} gives the pseudocode for the fixed-$\beta$ Frank--Wolfe variant.
Final positions and objective values are in Table~\ref{tab:q6_final}.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Frank--Wolfe (Conditional Gradient) --- Fixed-$\beta$ Variant}
\label{alg:fw}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0 \in \mathcal{X}$, mixing parameter $\beta \in (0,1)$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_k \gets \arg\min_{x \in \mathcal{X}}\, \nabla f(x_k)^T x$ \Comment{Linear minimisation oracle (LP)}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets \beta\, x_k + (1 - \beta)\, z_k$ \Comment{Fixed step: move $(1-\beta)$ fraction towards $z_k$}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{remark}[Step-size schedule]
The standard Frank--Wolfe algorithm uses the \emph{open-loop} (decaying) step schedule
$\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$, writing $x_{k+1} = (1-\gamma_k)x_k + \gamma_k z_k$.
The fixed-$\beta$ variant used here corresponds to a \emph{constant} step $\gamma = 1 - \beta$
at every iteration. Constant $\gamma$ is simpler to implement but produces sustained
oscillation near the optimum since $z_k$ keeps switching between vertices of $\mathcal{X}$.
The theoretical $O(1/k)$ guarantee applies only to the decaying schedule; the fixed
schedule may not converge for all functions. The results below illustrate this oscillation.
\end{remark}

The key feature of Frank--Wolfe is the \textbf{linear minimisation oracle}: rather than
projecting onto $\mathcal{X}$, it solves a \emph{linear programme} over $\mathcal{X}$ at
each iteration. A natural certificate of convergence is the \textbf{Frank--Wolfe gap}:
\begin{equation}
g_k = \langle \nabla f(x_k),\; x_k - z_k \rangle \geq 0,
\end{equation}
which satisfies $g_k \geq f(x_k) - f^{\star}$ (since $f(x^{\star}) \geq f(x_k) +
\langle \nabla f(x_k), x^{\star} - x_k\rangle \geq f(x_k) - g_k$ by convexity and the
definition of $z_k$). Thus $g_k = 0$ certifies that $x_k$ is optimal. The $O(1/k)$
convergence bound states $\min_{j \leq k} g_j \leq 2LD^2/(k+2)$. For box constraints, this decomposes per coordinate:
\begin{equation}
(z_k)_i = \begin{cases} l_i & \text{if } (\nabla f(x_k))_i > 0, \\ u_i & \text{if } (\nabla f(x_k))_i \leq 0, \end{cases}
\end{equation}
requiring only $O(d)$ operations. Since $x_{k+1} = \beta x_k + (1-\beta) z_k$ is a
convex combination of two feasible points, the iterates are \emph{automatically feasible}
--- no projection oracle is needed.

\begin{proposition}[Frank--Wolfe $O(1/k)$ bound]
\label{prop:fw_conv}
Let $f$ be $L$-smooth and convex on a convex compact set $\mathcal{X}$ with diameter
$D = \max_{x,y \in \mathcal{X}}\|x - y\|$.  The Frank--Wolfe algorithm with
decaying step $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:fw_rate}
  f(x_k) - f^{\star} \;\leq\; \frac{2LD^2}{k+2},
\end{equation}
and the Frank--Wolfe gap $g_k \geq f(x_k) - f^{\star}$ serves as a computable
certificate: $g_k = 0 \Rightarrow x_k = x^{\star}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Sketch]
With $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$ and $L$-smoothness of $f$:
$f(x_{k+1}) \leq f(x_k) + \gamma_k\langle \nabla f(x_k), z_k - x_k\rangle
+ \frac{L\gamma_k^2}{2}D^2
\leq f(x_k) - \gamma_k(f(x_k)-f^{\star}) + \frac{L\gamma_k^2}{2}D^2$.
Letting $h_k = f(x_k) - f^{\star}$ and unrolling the recursion gives \eqref{eq:fw_rate}.
\end{proof}
\begin{remark}
For the box $\mathcal{X} = [0.5,5]\times[-5,10]$ in Q6, $D = \sqrt{(5-0.5)^2+(10+5)^2}\approx 15.7$
and $L = 2$ for the quadratic objectives, giving the bound $f(x_{180}) - f^{\star} \leq 2{\cdot}2{\cdot}246/182 \approx 5.4$.
The empirical values ($f \approx 0.005$--$0.009$) are \emph{much} tighter because the fixed-$\beta$
variant (with $\beta = 0.985$) takes smaller but better-directed steps than the worst-case
analysis assumes.  The $O(1/k)$ rate is slower than projected GD's $O(\rho^k)$ linear
rate (Proposition~\ref{prop:pgd_conv}) for strongly convex objectives, but Frank--Wolfe
avoids projection entirely, which is critical for structured constraint sets
(nuclear norm ball, spectrahedra, flow polytopes) where projection is $O(d^3)$ but LP
minimisation is $O(d)$.
\end{remark}
For the box $\mathcal{X}$, $D = \sqrt{(5-0.5)^2 + (10-(-5))^2} \approx 15.7$.
This $O(1/k)$ rate is slower than projected GD's linear convergence ($O(\rho^k)$ for
strongly convex), but the projection-free oracle makes Frank--Wolfe attractive for
structured constraint sets (e.g.\ nuclear norm ball, spectrahedra, flow polytopes)
where projection is computationally expensive but LP minimisation is efficient.

\subsection{Interior vs Boundary Behaviour}

\textbf{Interior optimum:} Frank--Wolfe converges but exhibits a characteristic
``zig-zag'' pattern. The LP vertex $z_k$ jumps between extreme points of $\mathcal{X}$
at each iteration, and the convex combination gradually averages these jumps towards
the optimum. The convergence factor $(1-\beta)$ controls step aggression and oscillation:
smaller $(1-\beta)$ (larger $\beta$) damps oscillation at the cost of slower movement
per step. Table~\ref{tab:q6_beta} compares the two $\beta$ values: $\beta = 0.985$
gives $f = 0.0046$ vs $\beta = 0.90$ giving $f = 0.0089$, both well above $f^{\star} = 0$.
The smaller step ($\beta = 0.985$, step size $1.5\%$) wins because it reduces the
oscillation caused by $z_k$ jumping between widely-separated vertices.

\textbf{Boundary optimum:} When the unconstrained minimum lies outside $\mathcal{X}$,
Frank--Wolfe is naturally guided to the boundary. At every step, $\nabla f(x_k)$ points
towards the infeasible region; the LP oracle therefore consistently selects $z_k$ on the
constraint boundary, pulling the iterate towards the constrained optimum. The final
value $f = 0.364$ vs $f^{\star} = 0.25$ after 140 iterations shows that more iterations
are needed for the $O(1/k)$ rate to close the remaining gap.

\textbf{Comparison with Projected GD (Q5):} For the box constraint used in Q5, projected GD
converges much faster (achieves $f = 0.7244$ in $\approx 30$ iterations vs Frank--Wolfe's

appears for constraints where projection is expensive but LP oracle is cheap.

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{When to use Frank--Wolfe:} Large-scale problems with structured constraints
  (trace norm, flow polytopes, Birkhoff polytope) where LP is cheap but projection is not.
\item \textbf{When to use Projected GD:} Simple constraints (box, half-space, simplex) where
  projection is $O(d)$ or $O(d \log d)$ and a faster convergence rate is needed.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Method-Specific Insights}

\textbf{Adaptive step-size methods (Q1):} The Polyak step size is theoretically elegant
but practically fragile: it requires knowledge of $f^{\star}$. When $f^{\star}$ is exact
(Rosenbrock), it outperforms all other first-order methods, reaching $f \approx 0.007$--$0.024$
(oscillating near the minimum) vs GD's $f = 3.51$ in 120 iterations. RMSprop's exponential
forgetting makes it more robust than Adagrad's monotone decay for sustained convergence,
particularly on dense (non-sparse) gradient problems.

\textbf{Momentum methods (Q2):} Nesterov's acceleration ($O(1/k^2)$) consistently
outperforms GD, particularly on ill-conditioned problems. The 7-fold improvement on
Rosenbrock ($f = 0.479$ vs $3.39$) demonstrates the practical impact of the
$\kappa$-independent $O(1/k^2)$ bound. Adam's per-coordinate adaptivity is beneficial
in stochastic settings but underperforms Nesterov on deterministic smooth problems.

\textbf{Newton's method (Q3):} Second-order information transforms the problem: one step
for quadratics, $\sim$5 steps for near-quadratic (Benchmark B), and 20 damped steps for
Rosenbrock achieving $5.4\times$ better final value than 80 GD steps. The cost is
$O(n^2)$ memory and $O(n^3)$ per step, limiting applicability to $n \lesssim 10^4$.
BFGS achieves super-linear convergence via the Dennis--Mor\'e condition
(Proposition~\ref{prop:bfgs}) without Hessian computation; L-BFGS reduces storage
to $O(mn)$ while retaining this rate in practice.

\textbf{Derivative-free methods (Q4):} When gradients are unavailable, FD provides
near-exact gradient directions for small $\delta$ ($d+1$ function evaluations vs 1
gradient). The optimal FD step $\delta_{\rm opt} \approx \sqrt{\varepsilon_{\rm mach}} \approx 10^{-8}$
balances truncation and rounding error to achieve $O(\varepsilon_{\rm mach}^{1/2})$ total
error (Proposition~\ref{prop:fd_opt}). Nelder--Mead adapts well to low-dimensional non-smooth landscapes.
Grid search provides global guarantees but suffers from the $O(N^d)$ curse of dimensionality.

\textbf{Constrained optimisation (Q5):} Projected GD enforces feasibility as a hard
constraint at every step; penalty methods approximate feasibility via soft penalisation.
Large $\lambda$ enforces feasibility but increases the objective's condition number.
The augmented Lagrangian method achieves exact feasibility at finite $\lambda$.

\textbf{Frank--Wolfe (Q6):} Projection-free design via LP oracle; the formal $O(LD^2/k)$
rate (Proposition~\ref{prop:fw_conv}) is slower than projected GD's linear rate
(Proposition~\ref{prop:pgd_conv}) but the oracle efficiency makes FW preferred for
structured constraints where LP solving is cheap but projection is expensive. An additional
advantage is \emph{affine invariance}: FW's convergence does not worsen under affine
reparametrisation of the domain (unlike GD, whose rate degrades with condition number).
For the interior case with $L = 2$ and $D = 15.7$, the bound gives
$f(x_k) - f^{\star} \leq 2 \times 2 \times 15.7^2 / (180 + 2) \approx 5.4$ after 180
iterations --- looser than the observed $f = 0.005$, since the FW gap closes
faster in practice when the optimum is interior to $\mathcal{X}$.

\subsection{Practical Recommendations}

The results across all benchmarks suggest the following decision hierarchy for practioners:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Small-scale, well-conditioned, smooth problems ($n \leq 10^3$, $\kappa$ small):}
  Newton's method (Q3) requires the fewest iterations and achieves the highest accuracy;
  the $O(n^3)$ Hessian cost is negligible at this scale.
\item \textbf{Large-scale smooth problems ($n \gtrsim 10^4$, gradient available):}
  Nesterov acceleration (Q2) achieves the information-theoretically optimal $O(1/k^2)$
  rate without Hessian computation; L-BFGS approximates Newton's step at $O(mn)$ cost
  (see Section~\ref{sec:q3}) and retains super-linear convergence in practice.
\item \textbf{Ill-conditioned problems ($\kappa \gg 1$):} Adaptive per-coordinate methods
  (Adagrad, Adam) or Nesterov with a small step provide the most robust performance;
  constant-step GD is impractical when $\kappa \gtrsim 100$.
\item \textbf{$f^{\star}$ known exactly:} The Polyak step (Q1) is theoretically optimal
  and empirically the best first-order method on Rosenbrock ($\approx 150 \times$ better
  than GD); it is not recommended when $f^{\star}$ must be estimated.
\item \textbf{Gradient unavailable (black-box $f$):} Use finite differences (Q4) for
  smooth, moderate-$d$ problems ($d \lesssim 100$); Nelder--Mead simplex for low-dimensional
  ($d \leq 10$) or non-smooth landscapes; avoid grid search for $d > 3$.
\item \textbf{Linearly constrained problems with simple projection:} Projected GD (Q5)
  enforces hard feasibility at every step; it is the safest choice when the projection
  is $O(d)$ (e.g.\ box, simplex, $\ell_2$ ball).
\item \textbf{Structured constraints where projection is expensive:} Frank--Wolfe (Q6)
  replaces projection with an LP oracle; accept the slower $O(1/k)$ rate (vs linear for
  projected GD) in exchange for projection-free iterates and affine invariance.
\end{enumerate}

def gradient_descent(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        x = x - alpha * grad_fn(x)
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Polyak Step Size}]
def polyak_step(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, f_star, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        alpha_k = (loss_fn(x) - f_star) / (np.dot(g, g) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(alpha_k)
        x = x - alpha_k * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Adagrad}]
def adagrad(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha0, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    G = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        G += g**2
        eff_alpha = alpha0 / (np.sqrt(G) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(np.mean(eff_alpha))
        x = x - eff_alpha * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={RMSprop}]
def rmsprop(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha0, beta, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    v = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        v = beta * v + (1 - beta) * g**2
        eff_alpha = alpha0 / (np.sqrt(v) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(np.mean(eff_alpha))
        x = x - eff_alpha * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Heavy Ball / Polyak Momentum}]
def heavy_ball(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    z = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        z = beta * z + alpha * g
        x = x - z
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Nesterov Momentum}]
def nesterov_momentum(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta_max, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    z = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for k in range(1, n_iters + 1):
        beta_k = min((k - 1) / (k + 2), beta_max)
        lookahead = x + beta_k * z
        g = grad_fn(lookahead)
        z = beta_k * z - alpha * g
        x = x + z
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Adam Optimiser}]
def adam_optimiser(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta1, beta2, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    m_vec, v_vec = np.zeros_like(x), np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for t in range(1, n_iters + 1):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        m_vec = beta1 * m_vec + (1 - beta1) * g
        v_vec = beta2 * v_vec + (1 - beta2) * g**2
        m_hat = m_vec / (1 - beta1**t)
        v_hat = v_vec / (1 - beta2**t)
        x = x - alpha * m_hat / (np.sqrt(v_hat) + eps)
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Newton's Method with Damping}]
def newtons_method(grad_fn, hess_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, n_iters, damping=1e-8):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, update_norms = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        H = hess_fn(x) if callable(hess_fn) else hess_fn
        H_reg = H + damping * np.eye(len(x))
        try:
            p = np.linalg.solve(H_reg, g)
        except np.linalg.LinAlgError:
            p = g
        update_norms.append(np.linalg.norm(alpha * p))
        x = x - alpha * p
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(update_norms)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Finite-Difference Gradient}]
def fd_gradient(loss_fn, x, delta):
    n = len(x)
    g = np.zeros(n)
    f0 = loss_fn(x)
    for i in range(n):
        ei = np.zeros(n)
        ei[i] = 1.0
        g[i] = (loss_fn(x + delta * ei) - f0) / delta
    return g
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Nesterov Random Search (Zeroth-Order)}]
def nesterov_random_search(loss_fn, x0, alpha, delta, n_iters, seed=42):
    rng = np.random.RandomState(seed)
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        u = rng.randn(len(x))
        u = u / np.linalg.norm(u)
        df = (loss_fn(x + delta * u) - loss_fn(x)) / delta
        x = x - alpha * df * u
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Frank--Wolfe Algorithm}]
def frank_wolfe(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, bounds, beta, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, z_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        z = np.array([bounds[i][0] if g[i] > 0 else bounds[i][1]
                       for i in range(len(g))])
        z_hist.append(z.copy())
        x = beta * x + (1 - beta) * z
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(z_hist)
\end{lstlisting}
