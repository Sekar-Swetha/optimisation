\begin{abstract}
This report presents a rigorous comparative study of fifteen optimisation algorithms across six methodological categories applied to three benchmark functions spanning well-conditioned quadratic, mildly non-convex, and highly ill-conditioned regimes. The six questions investigate: (1)~adaptive step-size methods (Polyak, Adagrad, RMSprop, Heavy Ball), revealing that the Polyak step diverges when $f^{\star}$ is misspecified but achieves $f = 9.4 \times 10^{-3}$ on Rosenbrock when $f^{\star}=0$ is correct; (2)~momentum and stochastic methods, where Nesterov acceleration achieves the optimal $O(1/k^2)$ rate, reaching $f = 0.479$ on Rosenbrock versus $f = 3.388$ for GD, while mini-batch SGD reveals the batch-size/variance trade-off; (3)~Newton's method, which converges in a \emph{single iteration} for quadratic losses and reduces Rosenbrock loss from the GD value of~3.73 to~0.69 in only 20 iterations; (4)~derivative-free optimisation, where finite-difference quality is highly sensitive to perturbation $\delta$, and the Nesterov random search provides dimension-independent two-evaluation-per-step zeroth-order convergence; (5)~constrained optimisation, where projected gradient descent enforces hard feasibility from iteration~1 while penalty methods offer a softer, $\lambda$-dependent approximation with explicit KKT analysis; and (6)~the Frank--Wolfe algorithm, which achieves $O(1/k)$ convergence while avoiding projections entirely. Results confirm that curvature information provides the greatest acceleration on ill-conditioned problems, while adaptive step sizes offer the best practical trade-off between speed and hyperparameter robustness.
\end{abstract}

\section{Benchmark Problems and Experimental Setup}

All experiments use \texttt{np.random.seed(42)} for reproducibility. Three benchmark functions present distinct optimisation challenges, summarised in Table~\ref{tab:bench_props}.

\subsection{Benchmark A: Linear Regression Quadratic Loss}

A synthetic dataset with $m = 1000$ samples in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is generated:
\begin{equation}
\theta^{\star} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad x^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_2), \quad y^{(i)} = (\theta^{\star})^T x^{(i)} + \varepsilon^{(i)}, \quad \varepsilon^{(i)} \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, 1).
\end{equation}
The mean squared error loss is:
\begin{equation}
J(\theta) = \frac{1}{2m} \|X\theta - y\|^2, \qquad
\nabla J(\theta) = \frac{1}{m}X^T(X\theta - y), \qquad
H = \frac{1}{m}X^TX \succ 0.
\end{equation}
The Hessian $H$ is constant (independent of $\theta$), making this a strongly convex quadratic. By the law of large numbers, $H \to I_2$ as $m \to \infty$; with $m=1000$ and seed~42, $\lambda_{\max}(H) \approx 1.01$, $\lambda_{\min}(H) \approx 0.99$, giving condition number $\kappa \approx 1.02$. The irreducible noise floor is $J^{\star} = \frac{1}{2m}\|\varepsilon\|^2 \approx \frac{\sigma^2}{2} = 0.5$; numerically $J^{\star} \approx 0.4826$. Starting point: $\theta_0 = (0, 0)^T$.

\subsection{Benchmark B: Toy Neural Network Loss}

\begin{equation}
f(x_1, x_2) = (x_1 - 1)^2 + 5(x_2 - 2)^2 + \sin(x_1).
\label{eq:bench_B}
\end{equation}
The gradient and Hessian are:
\begin{equation}
\nabla f = \begin{bmatrix} 2(x_1 - 1) + \cos(x_1) \\ 10(x_2 - 2) \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
H(x) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 - \sin(x_1) & 0 \\ 0 & 10 \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The Hessian is diagonal with $\lambda_1 = 2 - \sin(x_1) \in [1, 3]$ (since $\sin(x_1) \in [-1,1]$) and $\lambda_2 = 10$, giving condition number $\kappa = 10/(2-\sin(x_1)) \in [10/3, 10]$ and smoothness $L = \sup_x \lambda_{\max}(H(x)) = 10$. Setting $\nabla f = 0$ yields $x_2^{\star} = 2$ and $2(x_1^{\star}-1) + \cos(x_1^{\star}) = 0$, solved numerically to $x_1^{\star} \approx 0.5828$, so $x^{\star} \approx (0.5828, 2.0)$ with $f^{\star} \approx 0.7244$. Starting point: $x_0 = (-1, 4)^T$.

\subsection{Benchmark C: Rosenbrock Function}

\begin{equation}
f(x_1, x_2) = (1 - x_1)^2 + 100(x_2 - x_1^2)^2.
\label{eq:bench_C}
\end{equation}
Global minimum at $(1, 1)$ with $f^{\star} = 0$. The Hessian is:
\begin{equation}
H(x) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 + 1200x_1^2 - 400x_2 & -400x_1 \\ -400x_1 & 200 \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
At $x_0 = (-1, 1)$: $H(-1,1) = \begin{pmatrix} 802 & 400 \\ 400 & 200 \end{pmatrix}$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \approx 1001.6$, $\lambda_2 \approx 0.4$, giving $\kappa \approx 2504$ and stability constraint $\alpha < 2/L \approx 0.002$. This extreme ill-conditioning arises from two geometrically distinct difficulties: (i)~\textbf{steep transverse direction} --- large curvature $\approx 1001.6$ across the valley $x_2 = x_1^2$ severely restricts the step size; (ii)~\textbf{flat longitudinal direction} --- curvature $\approx 0.4$ along the valley floor means very small gradient components drive progress toward the minimum, requiring many steps even without the step-size constraint. Gradient methods must use $\alpha \leq 1/L \approx 0.001$ to avoid oscillations across the valley, yet at this step size, longitudinal progress per step is only $\alpha \mu \|x_k - x^{\star}\| \approx 0.001 \times 0.4 \times \|x_k-x^{\star}\|$, extremely slow. Starting point: $x_0 = (-1, 1)^T$.

\subsection{Theoretical Background}

For an $L$-smooth, $\mu$-strongly convex function, gradient descent with step size $\alpha \in (0, 1/L]$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}
f(x_{k+1}) - f^{\star} \leq \left(1 - \frac{\mu}{L}\right)^k \bigl(f(x_0) - f^{\star}\bigr).
\label{eq:gd_rate}
\end{equation}
The contraction factor $(1 - \mu/L) = 1 - 1/\kappa$ is close to 1 for large $\kappa$, explaining why GD is slow on ill-conditioned problems. Adaptive methods attempt to overcome this by constructing per-coordinate or iteration-dependent step sizes that implicitly account for curvature.

\textbf{Quantitative convergence on Benchmark A.} With $\kappa \approx 1.02$ and step $\alpha = 0.08$, the per-step contraction of GD on a quadratic is $\rho = \max(|1-\alpha\lambda|) = \max(|1-0.08\times 0.99|,\, |1-0.08\times 1.01|) \approx 0.921$. The initial error $f(\theta_0) - f^{\star} = J(0) - J^{\star}$ with $\theta_0 = (0,0)$ and $\theta^{\star} = (3,4)$, $\sigma=1$ can be estimated as $J(0) \approx \frac{1}{2}(\|\theta^{\star}\|^2 + \sigma^2) = \frac{1}{2}(25+1) = 13$, so initial gap $\approx 12.5$. After $k=80$ steps: $\rho^{80} \times 12.5 \approx 0.921^{80} \times 12.5 \approx 0.001 \times 12.5 = 0.012$, confirming $f(\theta_{80}) \approx J^{\star} + 0.012 \approx 0.495$, close to the observed convergence. With the \emph{optimal} step $\alpha^{\star} = 2/(\mu+L) \approx 1.0$, the contraction would be $((\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1))^k \approx (0.01/2.01)^k \approx (0.005)^k$, converging in 3 steps to machine precision. The sub-optimal step $\alpha = 0.08$ thus adds an unnecessary factor of $\sim 80/3 \approx 27\times$ more iterations but is chosen conservatively to handle the three benchmarks with a consistent tuning protocol.

\begin{theorem}[Gradient Descent Convergence, \cite{nesterov2004introductory}]
Let $f$ be $L$-smooth and $\mu$-strongly convex with minimiser $x^{\star}$. Under GD with $\alpha = 1/L$:
\[
\|x_k - x^{\star}\|^2 \leq \left(1 - \frac{\mu}{L}\right)^k \|x_0 - x^{\star}\|^2.
\]
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Algorithm Formulations}

Four adaptive methods are compared against a constant step-size GD baseline across all three benchmarks for 120 iterations.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Polyak Step Size \cite{polyak1969minimization}}
\label{alg:polyak}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, optimal value $f^{\star}$, tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $\alpha_k \gets \dfrac{f(x_k) - f^{\star}}{\|g_k\|^2 + \varepsilon}$
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha_k\, g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{proposition}[Polyak Step Convergence]
If $f$ is convex and $f^{\star}$ is the true minimum, the Polyak step size satisfies $f(x_k) - f^{\star} \leq \frac{L\|x_0 - x^{\star}\|^2}{2(k+1)}$ for all $k \geq 0$. When $f^{\star}$ is incorrect, however, the step $\alpha_k = (f(x_k) - \tilde{f}^{\star})/\|g_k\|^2$ diverges as $\|g_k\| \to 0$ while $f(x_k) - \tilde{f}^{\star} \to f^{\star}_{\text{true}} - \tilde{f}^{\star} \neq 0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Adagrad \cite{duchi2011adaptive}}
\label{alg:adagrad}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $x_0$, base learning rate $\alpha_0$, tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$, iterations $N$
\State $G_0 \gets \mathbf{0} \in \mathbb{R}^d$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $G_{k+1} \gets G_k + g_k \odot g_k$ \Comment{Cumulative per-coordinate squared gradient}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \dfrac{\alpha_0}{\sqrt{G_{k+1}} + \varepsilon} \odot g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Adagrad's per-coordinate accumulator $G_k$ scales the learning rate inversely with the historical gradient magnitude in each dimension: coordinates with consistently large gradients receive smaller steps, while rarely-updated coordinates retain larger steps. This yields the online regret bound $R_T \leq \frac{1}{2\alpha_0}\sum_i \|g_{1:T,i}\|_2$, with $O(1/\sqrt{T})$ average regret for bounded gradients \cite{duchi2011adaptive}. The bound is tighter than the $O(\sqrt{T})$ worst-case when gradients are sparse: if coordinate $i$ only receives nonzero gradients in $s_i \ll T$ steps, its effective learning rate is $\alpha_0/\sqrt{s_i}$ rather than $\alpha_0/\sqrt{T}$, giving substantially faster convergence on sparse-gradient problems (e.g., word embeddings with rare words). In our dense-gradient benchmarks, this sparsity advantage does not appear. The critical drawback is that $G_k$ grows monotonically, causing the effective step to decay to zero irrespective of whether convergence has been achieved.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{RMSprop \cite{tieleman2012rmsprop}}
\label{alg:rmsprop}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $x_0$, learning rate $\alpha_0$, decay $\beta \in (0,1)$, tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$, iterations $N$
\State $v_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $v_{k+1} \gets \beta\, v_k + (1 - \beta)\, g_k \odot g_k$ \Comment{Exponential moving average of $g^2$}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \dfrac{\alpha_0}{\sqrt{v_{k+1}} + \varepsilon} \odot g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

RMSprop replaces Adagrad's cumulative sum with an exponential moving average (EMA) with decay $\beta = 0.9$. The EMA gives weight $\beta^j(1-\beta)$ to gradient $g_{k-j}$, effectively remembering only the last $\sim 1/(1-\beta) = 10$ iterations. This prevents the step size from decaying to zero, enabling RMSprop to adapt to non-stationary curvature.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Polyak Momentum / Heavy Ball \cite{polyak1964heavy}}
\label{alg:heavyball}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, momentum $\beta \in [0,1)$, iterations $N$
\State $z_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets \beta\, z_k + \alpha\, \nabla f(x_k)$ \Comment{Accumulated gradient (velocity)}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - z_{k+1}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The ``velocity'' $z_k$ accumulates gradient history with exponential decay $\beta^j$, allowing the iterate to build speed along consistent gradient directions. For strongly convex quadratics, the optimal Heavy Ball parameters are:
\begin{equation}
\alpha^{\star} = \frac{4}{(\sqrt{L}+\sqrt{\mu})^2}, \qquad \beta^{\star} = \left(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa}+1}\right)^2,
\label{eq:hb_optimal}
\end{equation}
achieving the optimal linear rate $(1 - 1/\sqrt{\kappa})^k$ versus GD's $(1 - 1/\kappa)^k$ \cite{polyak1964heavy}. Numerically, for the three benchmarks:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
\toprule
Benchmark & $\kappa$ & $\alpha^{\star}$ & $\beta^{\star}$ & Used $\alpha$ & Used $\beta$ \\
\midrule
A ($L{=}1.01, \mu{=}0.99$) & 1.02 & 1.000 & 0.0025 & 0.045 & 0.88 \\
B ($L{=}10, \mu{\approx}1.45$) & $\approx 6.9$ & 0.210 & 0.201 & 0.035 & 0.90 \\
C ($L{\approx}1002, \mu{\approx}0.4$) & $\approx 2504$ & 0.00384 & 0.924 & 0.0008 & 0.86 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
On Benchmark~A the optimal $\beta^{\star} = 0.0025$ is near zero (near-isotropic curvature), so the used $\beta = 0.88$ causes modest oscillation but converges nonetheless because the problem is so well-conditioned. On Benchmark~C the used $\alpha = 0.0008$ is more conservative than $\alpha^{\star} = 0.00384$ and $\beta = 0.86$ is close to $\beta^{\star} = 0.924$; this conservatism is appropriate since the quadratic theory does not apply to the non-convex Rosenbrock and more aggressive steps would cause divergence.

\subsection{Comparative Analysis and Key Findings}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Polyak step size} is theoretically optimal (achieving $f(x_k) - f^{\star} = O(1/k)$) when $f^{\star}$ is known exactly. In practice, the requirement of knowing $f^{\star}$ is a severe limitation; misspecification causes catastrophic divergence as shown on Benchmarks A and B.

\item \textbf{Adagrad} provides the most reliable performance across all benchmarks when $f^{\star}$ is unknown, matching the GD noise floor on the well-conditioned benchmarks. Its monotonically decreasing step size is a double-edged sword: it guarantees stability but prevents it from maintaining sufficient step sizes late in training on ill-conditioned problems.

\item \textbf{RMSprop} introduces forgetting ($\beta = 0.9$), which prevents step-size collapse but makes it sensitive to the choice of $\alpha_0$. On Rosenbrock, the required $\alpha_0 = 0.0035$ is so small that little progress is made, whereas a larger $\alpha_0$ would cause instability.

\item \textbf{Heavy Ball} achieves the best constant-step-size performance on the ill-conditioned Rosenbrock (Table~\ref{tab:q1_final}), due to momentum accumulation in the valley-floor direction. Its convergence is theoretically guaranteed for convex quadratics with optimal parameters, but not for non-convex functions.

\item \textbf{Practical implication:} In machine learning where $f^{\star}$ is unknown and the loss landscape is non-convex, Adagrad or RMSprop (or their combination in Adam, Question~2) are more robust choices than Polyak step size.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Theoretical Background}

Nesterov (1983) proved that for $L$-smooth convex functions, no first-order method can achieve a convergence rate better than $O(1/k^2)$, and his accelerated gradient method attains this lower bound exactly.

\begin{theorem}[Nesterov Optimal Rates, \cite{nesterov1983method}]
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,label=(\roman*)]
\item \textbf{Convex case:} Let $f$ be $L$-smooth and convex with minimiser $x^{\star}$. Nesterov's accelerated gradient method with appropriately scheduled momentum satisfies:
\[
f(x_k) - f^{\star} \leq \frac{2L\|x_0 - x^{\star}\|^2}{(k+1)^2}.
\]
This rate $O(1/k^2)$ is optimal among all first-order algorithms for $L$-smooth convex functions.
\item \textbf{Strongly convex case:} If additionally $f$ is $\mu$-strongly convex, the accelerated method achieves:
\[
f(x_k) - f^{\star} \leq \left(1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\right)^k (f(x_0) - f^{\star}),
\]
matching the Heavy Ball rate $O((1-1/\sqrt{\kappa})^k)$. This is a $\sqrt{\kappa}$-factor improvement over GD's $O((1-1/\kappa)^k)$. For $\kappa \approx 2504$: GD requires $\sim 2504\log(1/\varepsilon)$ iterations, Nesterov requires $\sim 50\log(1/\varepsilon)$ --- a $50\times$ speedup, matching the empirical $7\times$ improvement on Rosenbrock with only 150 iterations (far from asymptotic).
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

The key idea is the \emph{lookahead}: evaluating the gradient at a predicted future point $\tilde{x}_k = x_k + \beta_k z_k$ rather than at $x_k$ itself. This allows the gradient to ``look ahead'' along the momentum direction and correct for overshoot before it occurs.

\subsection{Algorithm Formulations}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Nesterov Momentum / Acceleration \cite{nesterov1983method}}
\label{alg:nesterov}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, max momentum $\beta_{\max}$, iterations $N$
\State $z_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 1, 2, \ldots, N$}
    \State $\beta_k \gets \min\!\left(\frac{k-1}{k+2},\ \beta_{\max}\right)$ \Comment{Adaptive momentum schedule}
    \State $\tilde{x}_k \gets x_k + \beta_k\, z_k$ \Comment{Lookahead point}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets \beta_k\, z_k - \alpha\, \nabla f(\tilde{x}_k)$ \Comment{Update velocity}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k + z_{k+1}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The schedule $\beta_k = \min\!\left((k-1)/(k+2),\ \beta_{\max}\right)$ starts at $\beta_1 = 0$ (pure gradient step) and increases toward $\beta_{\max}$, gradually building momentum as the iterate approaches the minimum. The capped schedule ensures stability: without $\beta_{\max}$, momentum could grow too quickly and cause oscillation on non-convex problems.

Crucially, Nesterov momentum differs from Heavy Ball in the \emph{point of gradient evaluation}:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\textbf{Heavy Ball:} & $x_{k+1} = x_k - \alpha\, \nabla f(x_k) + \beta(x_k - x_{k-1})$ \\
\textbf{Nesterov:} & $x_{k+1} = x_k - \alpha\, \nabla f(x_k + \beta_k(x_k - x_{k-1})) + \beta_k(x_k - x_{k-1})$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The lookahead gradient makes Nesterov provably optimal for convex functions, whereas Heavy Ball can oscillate on non-quadratic problems. Intuitively, the Heavy Ball update ``commits'' to the momentum direction before computing the gradient, so if momentum is too large, there is no mechanism to correct overshoot within the same step. Nesterov's lookahead evaluates the gradient \emph{after} taking the momentum step, allowing immediate correction. This distinction is critical on non-quadratic functions like Benchmark~B (where $\sin(x_1)$ creates local curvature variation) and Rosenbrock (where the valley curves sharply).

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Adam Optimiser \cite{kingma2014adam}}
\label{alg:adam}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $x_0$, learning rate $\alpha$, moments $\beta_1, \beta_2 \in (0,1)$, tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$, iterations $N$
\State $m_0 \gets \mathbf{0},\ v_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$t = 1, 2, \ldots, N$}
    \State $g_t \gets \nabla f(x_t)$
    \State $m_t \gets \beta_1\, m_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_1)\, g_t$ \Comment{Biased first moment (EMA of gradient)}
    \State $v_t \gets \beta_2\, v_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_2)\, g_t \odot g_t$ \Comment{Biased second moment}
    \State $\hat{m}_t \gets m_t / (1 - \beta_1^t)$ \Comment{Bias-corrected first moment}
    \State $\hat{v}_t \gets v_t / (1 - \beta_2^t)$ \Comment{Bias-corrected second moment}
    \State $x_{t+1} \gets x_t - \alpha\, \hat{m}_t / (\sqrt{\hat{v}_t} + \varepsilon)$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Adam combines momentum (via $m_t$, inheriting from Heavy Ball/Nesterov) with per-coordinate adaptive scaling (via $v_t$, inheriting from RMSprop), making it a synthesis of both approaches. The bias correction terms $1 - \beta_1^t$ and $1 - \beta_2^t$ are critical in early iterations: without them, $m_1 = (1-\beta_1)g_1$ is biased toward zero by factor $(1-\beta_1)$, which would over-shrink the effective learning rate at the start. The EMA time constants are $\tau_1 = 1/(1-\beta_1)$ and $\tau_2 = 1/(1-\beta_2)$: with typical values $\beta_1 = 0.9$ and $\beta_2 = 0.999$, the first moment ``remembers'' the last $\sim 10$ gradients while the second moment ``remembers'' the last $\sim 1000$, giving the gradient direction a short memory (allowing rapid directional changes) while the curvature estimate is stable over long horizons (avoiding oscillation in the effective learning rate). Note that Adam lacks general convergence guarantees in non-convex settings; the AMSGrad variant \cite{reddi2019convergence} fixes this by using a non-decreasing running maximum $\hat{v}_t = \max(\hat{v}_{t-1}, v_t/({1-\beta_2^t}))$, restoring the diminishing step size property needed for convergence.

\subsection{Mini-Batch Stochastic Gradient Descent}
\label{sec:q2_sgd}

Mini-batch SGD on Benchmark A uses a fixed learning rate $\alpha = 0.06$, batch sizes $b \in \{5, 40\}$, 50 epochs with data reshuffled each epoch (Algorithm~\ref{alg:sgd}).

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Mini-Batch SGD}
\label{alg:sgd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Data $(X, y)$, initial $\theta_0$, learning rate $\alpha$, batch size $b$, epochs $E$
\For{epoch $e = 1, 2, \ldots, E$}
    \State Randomly permute all $m$ data indices: $\pi \gets \text{Shuffle}(\{1,\ldots,m\})$
    \For{each mini-batch $\mathcal{B} = \{\pi_{ib+1}, \ldots, \pi_{(i+1)b}\}$ for $i = 0, 1, \ldots$}
        \State $\hat{g} \gets \dfrac{1}{b} X_{\mathcal{B}}^T(X_{\mathcal{B}}\theta - y_{\mathcal{B}})$ \Comment{Stochastic gradient}
        \State $\theta \gets \theta - \alpha\, \hat{g}$
    \EndFor
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The stochastic gradient $\hat{g}$ is an unbiased estimate of $\nabla J(\theta)$ ($\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}] = \nabla J$) with variance $\text{Var}(\hat{g}) = \sigma^2_g / b$, where $\sigma^2_g$ is the per-sample gradient variance and $b$ is the batch size. With $b = 5$: each epoch performs $m/b = 200$ updates; with $b = 40$: only 25 updates per epoch. For $\mu$-strongly convex $J$ with SGD run at fixed step size $\alpha$, the convergence floor satisfies:
\begin{equation}
\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[J(\theta_k) - J^{\star}] = \frac{\alpha\, \sigma^2_g}{2\mu\, b},
\label{eq:sgd_floor}
\end{equation}
so the noise floor is proportional to $\alpha/b$. To halve the floor, one must either halve $\alpha$ (slowing convergence by factor $\approx \sqrt{2}$) or double $b$ (doubling the per-update cost). For Benchmark A, $\mu \approx 0.99$, $\alpha = 0.06$: with $b=5$ the floor is $\approx 0.006\sigma^2_g/0.99 \approx 0.006\sigma^2_g$; with $b=40$ it is $\approx 0.00075\sigma^2_g$, a factor-of-8 reduction. The trade-off is: smaller batches make more frequent updates (faster convergence per epoch) but each update is noisier (higher floor around the minimum).

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/q2_sgd_batch.pdf}
\caption{Q2: Mini-batch SGD with different batch sizes on Benchmark~A. The smaller batch ($b=5$) converges faster per epoch due to $4\times$ more parameter updates, but exhibits sustained oscillation near the noise floor because each mini-batch gradient deviates significantly from the true gradient. The larger batch ($b=40$) converges more smoothly but achieves comparable accuracy slower in epoch-count terms. In wall-clock-time terms, smaller batches are typically faster until the irreducible stochastic noise floor is reached.}

\subsection{Effect of Increased Observation Noise}
\label{sec:q2_noisy_sgd}

The experiment is repeated with output noise scaled by 6 ($\sigma = 6$): $y_{\text{noisy}} = X\theta^{\star} + 6\varepsilon$.

\textbf{Irreducible noise floor analysis.} For the MSE loss with observation noise $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$, the best achievable expected loss (over all estimators) is:
\begin{equation}
J^{\star} = \frac{\sigma^2}{2},
\label{eq:noise_floor}
\end{equation}
because $\mathbb{E}[J(\hat\theta)] = \frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\|\varepsilon\|^2/m = \frac{\sigma^2}{2}$ at $\hat\theta = \theta^{\star}$. Scaling $\sigma$ by 6 multiplies $J^{\star}$ by $6^2 = 36$: from $\approx 0.5$ to $\approx 18$. Since mini-batch SGD converges to within $O(\alpha\sigma^2_g/b)$ of $J^{\star}$ (where $\sigma^2_g$ is per-sample gradient variance), and $\sigma^2_g \propto \sigma^2$, the convergence floor for both batch sizes rises proportionally to $\sigma^2$.

\subsection{Local Approximations of $g(x) = x^4$}

Given $g(x) = x^4$ and expansion point $x_0 = 0.25$: $g(x_0) = 0.25^4 = 3.906 \times 10^{-3}$, $g'(x_0) = 4 \times 0.25^3 = 0.0625$, $g''(x_0) = 12 \times 0.25^2 = 0.75$.

\textbf{First-order approximation:}
\begin{equation}
\hat{g}_1(x) = g(x_0) + g'(x_0)(x - x_0) = 3.906 \times 10^{-3} + 0.0625(x - 0.25).
\end{equation}

\textbf{Second-order approximation:}
\begin{equation}
\hat{g}_2(x) = g(x_0) + g'(x_0)(x - x_0) + \tfrac{1}{2}g''(x_0)(x - x_0)^2 = 3.906 \times 10^{-3} + 0.0625(x - 0.25) + 0.375(x - 0.25)^2.
\end{equation}

The Newton step minimises $\hat{g}_2$: setting $\hat{g}_2'(x) = 0$ gives $x_{k+1} = x_k - g'(x_k)/g''(x_k)$. This is the fundamental motivation for Newton's method: each iteration moves to the minimiser of the local quadratic model.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/q3_approximation.pdf}
\caption{Q3: Local approximations of $g(x) = x^4$ at $x_0 = 0.25$. The first-order approximation (tangent line) captures only local slope and diverges rapidly from $g(x)$ away from $x_0$. The second-order approximation (parabola) captures curvature and fits well in a neighbourhood of $x_0$. The minimum of the parabola at $x_{\min} = x_0 - g'(x_0)/g''(x_0) = 0.25 - 0.0625/0.75 \approx 0.167$ is the Newton update, providing a much better estimate of the true minimum (at $x^{\star} = 0$) than the gradient-step would.}
\label{fig:q3_approx}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Newton's Method with Hessian Regularisation}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Newton's Method with Damping}
\label{alg:newton}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha \in (0, 1]$, damping $\lambda > 0$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $H_k \gets \nabla^2 f(x_k) + \lambda I$ \Comment{Regularised Hessian (guarantees PD)}
    \State Solve $H_k\, p_k = g_k$ for $p_k$ \Comment{Newton direction: $p_k = H_k^{-1} g_k$}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, p_k$ \Comment{$\alpha = 1$: pure Newton; $\alpha < 1$: damped Newton}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The damping term $\lambda I$ with $\lambda = 10^{-8}$ ensures positive definiteness of $H_k$ when the Hessian has near-zero eigenvalues (as occurs on Rosenbrock away from the optimum). The damped Newton step ($\alpha < 1$) trades the quadratic convergence rate for stability when the quadratic model is inaccurate far from the optimum.

\begin{theorem}[Newton Quadratic Convergence, \cite{nocedal2006numerical}]
Let $f$ be twice continuously differentiable with $L$-Lipschitz Hessian. If $\nabla^2 f(x^{\star})$ is positive definite and $x_0$ is sufficiently close to $x^{\star}$, then the full Newton step ($\alpha = 1$) satisfies:
\[
\|x_{k+1} - x^{\star}\| \leq C\, \|x_k - x^{\star}\|^2
\]
for some constant $C > 0$. This \emph{quadratic convergence} means the number of correct decimal places doubles at every iteration.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proposition}[Exact Convergence on Quadratics]
For a strictly convex quadratic $f(\theta) = \frac{1}{2}\theta^T H \theta - b^T \theta$, Newton's method with $\alpha = 1$ converges in \emph{exactly one iteration}: $\theta_1 = \theta_0 - H^{-1}(H\theta_0 - b) = H^{-1}b = \theta^{\star}$.
\end{proposition}

This exact convergence in one step is observed on Benchmark A (Figure~\ref{fig:q3_fval_A}).

\textbf{Newton decrement.} A key quantity for monitoring Newton's progress is the \emph{Newton decrement}:
\begin{equation}
\lambda_N(x_k) = \sqrt{g_k^T H_k^{-1} g_k} = \|p_k\|_{H_k},
\label{eq:newton_decrement}
\end{equation}
which satisfies $f(x_k) - f^{\star} \leq \frac{\lambda_N(x_k)^2}{2}$ for strongly convex functions. The Newton decrement is therefore a computable, scale-invariant upper bound on the suboptimality, providing a principled stopping criterion: terminate when $\lambda_N(x_k) \leq \varepsilon$ (which guarantees $f(x_k) - f^{\star} \leq \varepsilon^2/2$). It is also affine-invariant: $\lambda_N$ does not change if $f$ is reparametrised by a linear transformation, unlike $\|\nabla f\|$ which scales with the problem.

\textbf{Worked example (Benchmark B at convergence).} At $x^{\star} \approx (0.5828, 2.0)$ for Benchmark B, $g = \nabla f(x^{\star}) \approx (0, 0)$ and $H = \text{diag}(2-\sin(0.5828), 10) \approx \text{diag}(1.453, 10)$. Starting from $x_0 = (-1, 4)$:
$g_0 \approx (2(-1-1)+\cos(-1),\; 10(4-2)) = (-3.460,\; 20)$, $H_0 \approx \text{diag}(2-\sin(-1), 10) = \text{diag}(2.841,\; 10)$.
Newton direction: $p_0 = H_0^{-1}g_0 \approx (-3.460/2.841,\; 20/10) = (-1.218,\; 2.0)$.
Newton decrement: $\lambda_N^2 = g_0^T p_0 = (-3.460)(-1.218) + 20 \times 2 = 4.214 + 40 = 44.21$, so $\lambda_N = 6.65$.
The upper bound $\lambda_N^2/2 = 22.1$ on $f(x_0) - f^{\star}$. At $x_0$: $f(-1,4) = 4 + 20 + \sin(-1) = 4 + 20 - 0.841 = 23.16 - 0.7244 \approx 22.4$, matching the bound.
After one damped Newton step ($\alpha = 0.85$): $x_1 = x_0 - 0.85 p_0 \approx (-1 + 1.035,\; 4 - 1.7) = (0.035,\; 2.3)$. By the second step, $\lambda_N \ll 1$, triggering the stopping criterion.

The update norm shown in Figure~\ref{fig:q3_update} is $\alpha\|p_k\| = \alpha\|H_k^{-1}g_k\|$ (unscaled), which approximates the Newton decrement up to a factor of $\alpha\sqrt{\lambda_{\min}(H_k)}$.

\section{Question 4: Derivative Approximation and Derivative-Free Optimisation \hfill [30 marks]}
\label{sec:q4}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Part A: Finite Difference and Nesterov Random Search (Benchmark B)}

\subsubsection{Finite Difference Gradient Approximation: Error Analysis}

The forward finite-difference approximation of partial derivatives is:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x) \approx \frac{f(x + \delta e_i) - f(x)}{\delta}.
\label{eq:fd}
\end{equation}
By Taylor expansion: $f(x + \delta e_i) = f(x) + \delta \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x) + \frac{\delta^2}{2}\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i^2}(x) + O(\delta^3)$, so the approximation error is:
\begin{equation}
\left|\frac{f(x+\delta e_i) - f(x)}{\delta} - \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x)\right| \leq \frac{L_H \delta}{2} + \frac{\epsilon_{\text{mach}}}{\delta},
\label{eq:fd_error}
\end{equation}
where $L_H$ bounds the second derivative and $\epsilon_{\text{mach}} \approx 10^{-16}$ is machine epsilon. The first term (truncation error) grows with $\delta$; the second term (floating-point error) grows as $\delta \to 0$. The optimal $\delta$ minimises the total error:
\begin{equation}
\delta^{\star} = \sqrt{\frac{2\epsilon_{\text{mach}}}{L_H}} \approx \sqrt{2 \times 10^{-16}} \approx 10^{-8}.
\end{equation}
In the experiments, $\delta = 0.05$ (``good'') is close to the regime where truncation error dominates but is small enough for accurate approximation on Benchmark B. $\delta = 0.8$ (``poor'') is far too large: the truncation error $L_H \times 0.8/2 \approx 0.4 L_H$ dominates and the approximated gradient points in a significantly wrong direction.

\textbf{Central finite differences.} An alternative with lower truncation error is:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x) \approx \frac{f(x + \delta e_i) - f(x - \delta e_i)}{2\delta}.
\label{eq:cfd}
\end{equation}
By Taylor expansion the odd terms cancel, giving $O(\delta^2)$ truncation error (vs.\ $O(\delta)$ for forward differences):
\begin{equation}
\left|\frac{f(x+\delta e_i) - f(x-\delta e_i)}{2\delta} - \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x)\right| \leq \frac{L_3 \delta^2}{6} + \frac{\epsilon_{\text{mach}}}{\delta},
\label{eq:cfd_error}
\end{equation}
where $L_3$ bounds the third derivative. The optimal central-difference step is $\delta^{\star}_c = (3\epsilon_{\text{mach}}/L_3)^{1/3} \approx 10^{-5}$, achieving error $O(\epsilon_{\text{mach}}^{2/3})$ versus $O(\epsilon_{\text{mach}}^{1/2})$ for forward differences. The cost of this improvement is $2d$ function evaluations per gradient (versus $d+1$ for forward differences), a factor-of-two increase in evaluation cost:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Scheme & Trunc.\ error & FE per gradient & Optimal $\delta^{\star}$ \\
\midrule
Forward FD & $O(\delta)$ & $d+1$ & $\approx 10^{-8}$ \\
Central FD & $O(\delta^2)$ & $2d$ & $\approx 10^{-5}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
For problems where gradient accuracy matters more than oracle budget (e.g., Benchmark C with $\kappa \approx 2504$ where gradient direction quality is critical), central differences are preferred. For high-dimensional problems ($d \gg 1$) where function evaluations are expensive, the $2d$ vs.\ $d+1$ cost difference is significant. \textbf{Automatic differentiation (AD)} provides an exact alternative: AD computes the exact gradient at $O(c \cdot d)$ cost (where $c$ is the cost of one forward pass, typically $c \approx 5$) with \emph{no} discretisation error, by applying the chain rule symbolically through the computation graph in reverse mode (backpropagation). AD is the standard approach in modern deep learning frameworks (PyTorch, JAX, TensorFlow) and eliminates the need to choose $\delta$. The relative merits are:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{FD:} Requires only function evaluation (black-box oracle); robust when the function is only available as a black box or simulator.
\item \textbf{AD:} Requires differentiable code but provides exact gradients at $O(d)$ cost with no $\delta$-tuning; preferred when the function is implemented as explicit code.
\item \textbf{NRS:} Requires only black-box evaluation; dimension-independent cost $O(1)$ per step at the expense of high gradient variance.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Nesterov Random Search}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Nesterov Random Search (Zeroth-Order) \cite{nesterov2017random}}
\label{alg:nrs}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, perturbation $\delta > 0$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State Sample $u_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, normalise: $u_k \gets u_k / \|u_k\|$ (uniform on unit sphere)
    \State Compute stochastic directional derivative: $\hat{d}_k \gets \dfrac{f(x_k + \delta u_k) - f(x_k)}{\delta}\, u_k$
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \hat{d}_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

This zeroth-order method requires only 2 function evaluations per iteration regardless of dimension $d$, in contrast to FD which requires $d+1$ evaluations. The estimator satisfies $\mathbb{E}[\hat{d}_k] \approx \nabla f(x_k)$ in the sense that $\mathbb{E}[\hat{d}_k] = \nabla f_\delta(x_k)$ where $f_\delta$ is the smoothed function; for small $\delta$, $f_\delta \approx f$. The main limitation is high variance: since a single random direction is sampled per step, convergence requires many more iterations than FD or exact GD.

\subsection{Part B: Derivative-Free Methods (Benchmark C)}

\subsubsection{Nelder--Mead Simplex Method}

The Nelder--Mead algorithm maintains a simplex of $n+1 = 3$ vertices in $\mathbb{R}^2$, iteratively transforming it through four operations:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Reflection} ($\alpha_r = 1$): Move the worst vertex through the centroid of the remaining vertices.
\item \textbf{Expansion} ($\gamma = 2$): If reflection improved substantially, try moving further in the same direction.
\item \textbf{Contraction} ($\rho = 0.5$): If reflection did not improve, contract toward the centroid.
\item \textbf{Shrink} ($\sigma = 0.5$): If all else fails, contract the entire simplex toward the best vertex.
\end{enumerate}
No gradient information is required. The simplex adapts its shape to the local landscape: elongating along promising directions and contracting when near the minimum. Settings: initial step $= 0.35$, iterations $= 160$.

\textbf{Dimensionality limitation.} In $d$ dimensions, the simplex has $d+1$ vertices. In high dimensions, the ratio of the simplex's ``volume'' to the search space grows exponentially small, making meaningful exploration infeasible. Moreover, convergence proofs exist only for $d=1$ (where Nelder--Mead reduces to golden section search); for $d \geq 2$, the method can stagnate on degenerate simplices or cycle without convergence \cite{nelder1965simplex}. This contrasts with FD gradient descent and Nesterov random search, which both retain meaningful convergence guarantees in arbitrary $d$.

\subsection{Problem Setup and KKT Analysis}

The constrained problem is:
\begin{equation}
\min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^2}\; f(x) \quad \text{subject to}\quad x_1 \geq 0.5,
\label{eq:constrained_problem}
\end{equation}
where $f$ is Benchmark~B (\ref{eq:bench_B}). The starting point $x_0 = (0.2, 4.0)$ is infeasible. Writing the constraint as $g(x) = 0.5 - x_1 \leq 0$, the KKT necessary conditions for a local minimiser $x^{\star}$ are:
\begin{align}
\nabla f(x^{\star}) - \mu^{\star} e_1 &= 0, \label{eq:kkt_stationarity} \\
\mu^{\star} &\geq 0, \label{eq:kkt_dual_feasibility} \\
0.5 - x_1^{\star} &\leq 0, \label{eq:kkt_primal_feasibility} \\
\mu^{\star}(0.5 - x_1^{\star}) &= 0, \label{eq:kkt_complementary_slackness}
\end{align}
where $e_1 = (1, 0)^T$. Since the unconstrained minimum of $f$ is at $x^{\star}_{\text{unc}} \approx (0.5828, 2.0)$ with $0.5828 > 0.5$, the constraint is \emph{inactive} at the constrained solution. By complementary slackness (\ref{eq:kkt_complementary_slackness}), $\mu^{\star} = 0$, and stationarity (\ref{eq:kkt_stationarity}) reduces to $\nabla f(x^{\star}) = 0$, i.e., the constrained and unconstrained minima coincide. All constrained methods should therefore converge to $(0.5828, 2.0)$ with $f^{\star} = 0.7244$, provided they maintain or achieve feasibility.

\subsection{Algorithm Formulations}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Projected Gradient Descent (PGD)}
\label{alg:pgd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, projection $\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \nabla f(x_k)$ \Comment{Unconstrained gradient step}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets \Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(z_{k+1}) = ({\max}(0.5, z_{k+1,1}),\; z_{k+1,2})$ \Comment{Project onto feasible set}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

For the half-space $\{x : x_1 \geq 0.5\}$, the projection is simply $x_1 \leftarrow \max(0.5, x_1)$, a $O(1)$ operation. Projected GD satisfies: for any $x \in \mathcal{X}$, $\|x_{k+1} - x\|^2 \leq \|x_k - x\|^2 - 2\alpha(f(x_k) - f(x)) + \alpha^2 L^2 \|g_k\|^2$, giving linear convergence rate for smooth, strongly convex $f$ restricted to $\mathcal{X}$.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Linear Penalty Method}
\label{alg:penalty}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, penalty weight $\lambda > 0$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $F_{\lambda}(x) \gets f(x) + \lambda \max(0,\, 0.5 - x_1)$ \Comment{Penalised objective (linear $\ell_1$ penalty)}
    \State $\nabla F_{\lambda}(x_k) \gets \nabla f(x_k) - \lambda \mathbf{1}[x_{k,1} < 0.5] \cdot e_1$ \Comment{Subgradient}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \nabla F_{\lambda}(x_k)$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The penalty $\lambda\max(0, 0.5 - x_1)$ adds a linear cost proportional to the constraint violation. As $\lambda \to \infty$, the penalised minimiser approaches the constrained minimiser. For finite $\lambda$, the penalised problem has solution $x^{\star}(\lambda)$ with $x^{\star}(\lambda) \to x^{\star}$ as $\lambda \to \infty$.

\begin{proposition}[Augmented Lagrangian Method]
Define the augmented Lagrangian:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_\rho(x, \mu) = f(x) + \mu (0.5 - x_1) + \frac{\rho}{2}\max(0,\, 0.5 - x_1)^2.
\label{eq:alm}
\end{equation}
The augmented Lagrangian method (ALM) alternates between:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item Minimise $x_{k+1} \gets \arg\min_x \mathcal{L}_\rho(x, \mu_k)$, and
\item Update the dual: $\mu_{k+1} \gets \mu_k + \rho\,\max(0,\, 0.5 - x_{k+1,1})$.
\end{enumerate}
For fixed $\rho > 0$, this converges to $\mu^{\star}$ and achieves primal feasibility $x_1 \to 0.5$ without requiring $\rho \to \infty$, thereby avoiding the ill-conditioning that makes pure penalty methods ($\rho = \lambda \to \infty$) numerically unstable.
\end{proposition}

Since the true KKT multiplier here is $\mu^{\star} = 0$ (constraint inactive), the dual update satisfies $\mu_k \to 0$ and the ALM reduces to minimising $f$ directly, confirming the primal/dual consistency of the problem setup.

Settings: Projected GD ($\alpha = 0.08$, 100 iterations); Penalty with $\lambda \in \{0.15, 1.8, 4.5\}$ ($\alpha = 0.05$ for $\lambda \leq 1.8$, $\alpha = 0.03$ for $\lambda = 4.5$, as larger penalties increase the effective Lipschitz constant and require smaller steps); Unconstrained GD ($\alpha = 0.07$, 100 iterations).

\textbf{Key comparison:}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Projected GD} guarantees feasibility at every iterate through the projection operator. This is the correct choice when constraint violations are inadmissible (safety-critical systems, physical constraints). The cost is requiring a tractable projection oracle. The computational complexity of projection varies dramatically by constraint set:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
\textbf{Constraint set $\mathcal{X}$} & \textbf{Projection cost} & \textbf{Algorithm} \\
\midrule
Half-space $\{x : a^Tx \leq b\}$ & $O(d)$ & Closed form \\
Box $\{x : l \leq x \leq u\}$ & $O(d)$ & Coordinate clipping \\
Probability simplex $\Delta_d$ & $O(d\log d)$ & Sort + threshold \\
$\ell_2$ ball $\{\|x\| \leq r\}$ & $O(d)$ & Scale if needed \\
Nuclear norm ball $\{\|X\|_* \leq \tau\}$ & $O(\min(m,n)^2 \max(m,n))$ & Full SVD \\
Positive semidefinite cone & $O(d^3)$ & Eigendecomposition \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
When projection is expensive (e.g., nuclear norm, SDP constraints), the LP oracle in Frank--Wolfe may be substantially cheaper, making FW the preferred method despite its slower convergence rate.
\item \textbf{Penalty methods} only require a modified gradient, making them easy to implement for any constraint type. The penalty parameter $\lambda$ must be tuned: too small fails to enforce feasibility; too large requires smaller step sizes for stability, increasing iteration count. The linear ($\ell_1$) penalty can cause chattering near the constraint boundary due to the non-smooth subgradient. A smoother quadratic penalty $\lambda(0.5-x_1)^2 \mathbf{1}[x_1 < 0.5]$ would avoid this at the cost of more iterations needed to enforce feasibility at a given $\lambda$. From a Lagrangian duality perspective, the penalty parameter $\lambda$ approximates the KKT multiplier $\mu^{\star}$: the minimiser of $F_{\lambda}(x)$ satisfies $\mu^{\star}(\lambda) \approx \lambda \cdot \mathbf{1}[x_1 < 0.5]$. As $\lambda \to \infty$, $\mu^{\star}(\lambda) \to \mu^{\star}_{\text{true}} = 0$ (since the constraint is inactive). The \emph{augmented Lagrangian method} (ALM) combines penalty with an explicit dual update $\mu_{k+1} = \mu_k + \lambda(0.5 - x_{k+1,1})$, achieving primal feasibility without requiring $\lambda \to \infty$ and thus avoiding the associated step-size restriction.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Linear Programme over $\mathcal{X}$}

The feasible set is $\mathcal{X} = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^2 : 0.5 \leq x_1 \leq 5,\ -5 \leq x_2 \leq 10\}$.

\textbf{LP formulation:} Minimise $f(x) = x_1 + 2x_2$ over $\mathcal{X}$.

Since $f$ is linear, the minimum over a bounded polytope is attained at a vertex. With both coefficients positive ($a = [1, 2]^T > 0$), the minimum is at the vertex minimising $a^T x$, which for a box constraint is the lower bound of each coordinate:
\begin{equation}
x^{\star} = (0.5,\ -5), \qquad f^{\star} = 0.5 + 2(-5) = -9.5.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Frank--Wolfe Algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Frank--Wolfe (Conditional Gradient Method, Fixed-Step Variant) \cite{frank1956algorithm}}
\label{alg:fw}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $x_0 \in \mathcal{X}$, mixing parameter $\beta \in (0,1)$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State Solve LP: $z_k \gets \arg\min_{z \in \mathcal{X}}\, \nabla f(x_k)^T z$ \Comment{Linear minimisation oracle}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets \beta\, x_k + (1 - \beta)\, z_k$ \Comment{Convex combination (fixed step)}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

For box constraints, the LP subproblem decomposes per coordinate in $O(d)$ operations:
\begin{equation}
(z_k)_i = \begin{cases} l_i & \text{if } (\nabla f(x_k))_i > 0 \\ u_i & \text{if } (\nabla f(x_k))_i \leq 0 \end{cases}
\label{eq:fw_lp}
\end{equation}
Each $x_{k+1}$ is a convex combination of $x_k$ and a vertex of $\mathcal{X}$, so $x_{k+1} \in \mathcal{X}$ by convexity --- no projection is needed. The standard Frank--Wolfe convergence theorem uses the open-loop step size $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$:

\begin{theorem}[Frank--Wolfe Convergence, \cite{frank1956algorithm}]
For $L$-smooth convex $f$ and compact convex $\mathcal{X}$ with diameter $D$, the standard Frank--Wolfe algorithm with $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$ satisfies:
\[
f(x_k) - f^{\star} \leq \frac{2LD^2}{k+2}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}
The implementation uses a \emph{fixed} mixing parameter $\beta$ (constant step $(1-\beta)$ toward $z_k$), a variant of standard FW. The standard open-loop schedule $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$ achieves the $O(1/k)$ guarantee; the fixed-$\beta$ variant converges geometrically for interior optima but does not carry a tight $O(1/k)$ guarantee. A practical certificate of optimality is the \textbf{Frank--Wolfe gap}:
\begin{equation}
g_k^{\text{FW}} \;=\; \nabla f(x_k)^T(x_k - z_k) \;\geq\; f(x_k) - f^{\star} \;\geq\; 0,
\label{eq:fw_gap}
\end{equation}
where the first inequality follows from $f(z_k) \geq f^{\star}$ and the linear lower bound $f(x_k) + \nabla f(x_k)^T(z_k - x_k) \leq f(z_k)$ when $f$ is convex. This gap is computable at no additional cost since $z_k$ is already known, and serves as a stopping criterion. For the standard FW with $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$, $g_k^{\text{FW}} \leq 2LD^2/(k+2)$, matching the $O(1/k)$ rate.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Interior vs.\ Boundary Behaviour}

\textbf{Interior optimum:} When the constrained optimum lies strictly inside $\mathcal{X}$, FW converges but exhibits the ``zig-zag'' pattern from the LP vertex oscillation. The iterate $x_k$ is always a convex combination of past LP vertices, so it can only approach the optimum asymptotically. The fixed-$\beta$ schedule provides linear convergence but with a constant offset due to the vertex oscillation; in contrast, the standard schedule $\gamma_k \to 0$ damps out the oscillation and achieves $O(1/k)$ guaranteed convergence. The \textbf{FW gap} can be computed at the final iterate to certify progress. For the interior case with $\beta = 0.985$ and final $x \approx (0.997, 5.067)$: $\nabla f(x) = 2(x-(1,5)) \approx (-0.006,\; 0.134)$. The LP oracle sets $z_1 = u_1 = 5$ (since $\nabla_1 < 0$, take upper bound) and $z_2 = l_2 = -5$ (since $\nabla_2 > 0$, take lower bound), giving $z = (5, -5)$. The FW gap is:
\[
g^{\text{FW}} = \nabla f(x)^T(x - z) = (-0.006)(0.997-5) + (0.134)(5.067+5) \approx 0.024 + 1.349 \approx 1.37.
\]
Since $g^{\text{FW}} = 1.37 \geq f(x) - f^{\star} = 0.0046$, the certificate is valid but loose: the iterate is very close to $f^{\star}$ yet the gap remains large because the LP vertex $(5,-5)$ is far from the optimum $(1,5)$. This looseness is characteristic of FW for \emph{interior} optima and motivates away-step variants.

\textbf{Boundary optimum:} FW is particularly natural for boundary optima since the LP oracle consistently selects the vertex nearest the true optimum, allowing the iterate to approach the boundary directly. Unlike projected gradient descent, no explicit projection is needed: feasibility is maintained automatically by taking convex combinations. However, convergence is slower ($O(1/k)$ vs.\ linear for PGD) because the FW step is fundamentally ``open-loop'' in the standard formulation.

\textbf{Improving FW convergence: away-step and pairwise variants.} A known limitation of standard FW is that iterates are convex combinations of vertices, which can prevent them from moving \emph{away} from vertices once they are in the convex hull interior. The \textbf{away-step FW} (Lacoste-Julien \& Jaggi, 2015) augments each step with an ``away step'' that removes weight from the vertex in the current support that most opposes descent, achieving \emph{linear convergence} on strongly convex objectives. The \textbf{pairwise FW} variant simultaneously moves weight from the worst vertex to the best LP vertex in one step. Both variants maintain feasibility by construction and are particularly effective for simplex-constrained problems where the active set of vertices is small.

\textbf{Advantages of Frank--Wolfe over projection-based methods:}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item No projection oracle required --- only an LP oracle over $\mathcal{X}$
\item Iterates are always feasible by construction
\item The LP oracle is computationally cheap for many structured constraint sets (box, simplex, nuclear norm ball)
\item Sparsity-promoting: LP vertex solutions tend to be sparse (e.g., simplex vertices are one-hot vectors), useful in matrix completion and nuclear norm problems
\end{itemize}

On Benchmark~C with $\kappa \approx 2504$, GD requires $O(\kappa \log(1/\varepsilon)) \approx 2504 \log(1/\varepsilon)$ iterations, while Nesterov requires $O(\sqrt{\kappa} \log(1/\varepsilon)) \approx 50 \log(1/\varepsilon)$ iterations --- a $50\times$ speedup. Yet both remain slow for high accuracy; Newton achieves the same accuracy in $O(\log\log(1/\varepsilon))$ iterations, showing that no amount of first-order optimisation can substitute for curvature information on severely ill-conditioned problems. The Rosenbrock results confirm this hierarchy: excluding the oracle-assisted Polyak ($f=0.009$, $f^{\star}=0$ known), the ranking by final objective value (lower is better) is Nesterov ($f=0.479$, 150 it.) $\prec$ Newton ($f=0.686$, 20 it.) $\prec$ Heavy Ball ($f=1.40$, 120 it.) $\prec$ Adam ($f=1.578$, 150 it.) $\prec$ GD ($f=3.51$, 120 it.). Note that Heavy Ball outperforms Adam here despite both being $O(1/k)$ in the worst case: the consistent gradient direction along the Rosenbrock valley allows Heavy Ball's momentum to accumulate effectively, whereas Adam's per-coordinate scaling is less suited to the tightly coupled anisotropy of the Rosenbrock Hessian (off-diagonal coupling between $x_1$ and $x_2$).

\textbf{The value of second-order information.} Newton's method breaks the condition-number barrier by using Hessian information: the Newton direction $p_k = H_k^{-1}g_k$ effectively pre-conditions the gradient, rescaling each direction by the inverse curvature. This yields quadratic convergence near the optimum ($O(\|x_k - x^{\star}\|^2)$ per step) and exact one-step convergence for quadratics. The cost --- computing and factoring an $n \times n$ Hessian ($O(n^3)$ per iteration) --- makes Newton impractical for large-scale machine learning ($n \sim 10^8$). \textbf{Quasi-Newton methods} (notably L-BFGS) approximate $H_k^{-1}$ from $m$ recent gradient-difference pairs $(s_j, y_j) = (x_{j+1}-x_j,\, g_{j+1}-g_j)$ using the BFGS update rule, achieving superlinear convergence with $O(mn)$ memory and $O(mn)$ cost per iteration. L-BFGS with $m \approx 20$ is the standard choice for large-scale machine learning problems where the loss landscape is smooth (e.g., logistic regression, neural networks with smooth activations).

\textbf{Adaptive step sizes improve robustness, not rate.} Adagrad, RMSprop, and Adam do not improve the asymptotic convergence rate for smooth convex functions (they remain $O(1/k)$ sublinear or $O(\rho^k)$ linear), but they improve practical performance by: (i) automatically adapting to heterogeneous curvature through per-coordinate scaling; and (ii) reducing the need for manual step-size tuning. The bias-variance trade-off between Adagrad (cumulative, decaying) and RMSprop (EMA, stable) offers a practical dial for non-stationary problems.

\textbf{Stochastic gradients introduce an irreducible noise floor.} Mini-batch SGD does not converge to the exact minimiser with a fixed step size; it converges to a neighbourhood of size $O(\alpha \sigma^2)$ where $\sigma^2$ is the gradient variance. Reducing batch size increases $\sigma^2$ (more iterations needed) but reduces per-iteration cost; the optimal batch size balances these effects. Under high observation noise, the irreducible floor $J^{\star} = \sigma^2/2$ dominates, making the convergence floor independent of batch size.

\textbf{Derivative-free methods require exponentially many evaluations for global optimality.} Finite-difference approximation maintains $O(d)$ evaluation cost (same order as exact GD) and achieves comparable accuracy when $\delta$ is chosen well ($\delta \approx 10^{-4}$ to $10^{-2}$ for typical functions). Nesterov random search provides $O(1)$ evaluations per step at the cost of much higher variance. Grid search is the only method that guarantees global optimality, but at $O(N^d)$ cost, it is impractical beyond $d \approx 3$.

\textbf{Constrained optimisation: projection vs.\ penalty.} Projected GD provides hard feasibility guarantees at the cost of a projection oracle; penalty methods offer simplicity but only soft feasibility. For problems where the unconstrained optimum is feasible (as in this assignment), both methods converge to the same point, but Projected GD does so while always remaining in $\mathcal{X}$. For safety-critical applications, hard feasibility is essential.

\textbf{Frank--Wolfe exploits constraint structure.} By reducing each iteration to an LP over $\mathcal{X}$, Frank--Wolfe avoids projection entirely. For constraint sets where the LP is cheap (box: $O(d)$; simplex: $O(d\log d)$) but projection is not, FW is the method of choice. The $O(1/k)$ rate is slower than projected GD ($O(\rho^k)$ linear) but may be acceptable in applications requiring sparse iterates (e.g., compressed sensing, matrix completion) since LP vertices tend to be sparse.

\subsection{Practical Recommendations}

Based on the experimental results across all three benchmarks, the following guidelines emerge:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Well-conditioned problems} ($\kappa < 10$, Benchmark~A): GD with a fixed step size is sufficient and simple. Adaptive methods provide negligible benefit.
\item \textbf{Moderately ill-conditioned problems} ($\kappa \in [10, 100]$, Benchmark~B): Adam or Nesterov momentum provide meaningful acceleration and are robust to hyperparameter choices.
\item \textbf{Severely ill-conditioned problems} ($\kappa > 100$, Benchmark~C): Newton's method (or quasi-Newton if $n$ is large) is the only practical approach. First-order methods can succeed with momentum (Nesterov reduces Rosenbrock loss $7\times$ vs.\ GD) but struggle to reach high accuracy.
\item \textbf{Gradient unavailable}: Use finite differences (if evaluations are cheap and $d$ is small) or Nesterov random search (if evaluations are expensive or $d$ is large). Nelder--Mead is effective for $d \leq 5$.
\item \textbf{Constrained problems}: Projected GD if the projection is cheap; Frank--Wolfe if the LP is cheap and/or sparsity is desired; penalty methods if the constraint is soft or for rapid prototyping.
\item \textbf{Stochastic settings}: Adam is the default choice for deep learning due to its robustness to hyperparameter choice. Learning rate schedules (warmup + cosine decay) further improve practical performance.
\end{enumerate}

def gradient_descent(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        x = x - alpha * grad_fn(x)
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Polyak Step Size}]
def polyak_step(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, f_star, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        alpha_k = (loss_fn(x) - f_star) / (np.dot(g, g) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(alpha_k)
        x = x - alpha_k * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Adagrad}]
def adagrad(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha0, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    G = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        G += g**2
        eff_alpha = alpha0 / (np.sqrt(G) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(np.mean(eff_alpha))
        x = x - eff_alpha * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={RMSprop}]
def rmsprop(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha0, beta, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    v = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        v = beta * v + (1 - beta) * g**2
        eff_alpha = alpha0 / (np.sqrt(v) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(np.mean(eff_alpha))
        x = x - eff_alpha * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Heavy Ball / Polyak Momentum}]
def heavy_ball(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    z = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        z = beta * z + alpha * g
        x = x - z
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Nesterov Momentum}]
def nesterov_momentum(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta_max, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    z = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for k in range(1, n_iters + 1):
        beta_k = min((k - 1) / (k + 2), beta_max)
        lookahead = x + beta_k * z
        g = grad_fn(lookahead)
        z = beta_k * z - alpha * g
        x = x + z
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Adam Optimiser}]
def adam_optimiser(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta1, beta2, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    m_vec, v_vec = np.zeros_like(x), np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for t in range(1, n_iters + 1):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        m_vec = beta1 * m_vec + (1 - beta1) * g
        v_vec = beta2 * v_vec + (1 - beta2) * g**2
        m_hat = m_vec / (1 - beta1**t)
        v_hat = v_vec / (1 - beta2**t)
        x = x - alpha * m_hat / (np.sqrt(v_hat) + eps)
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Newton's Method with Regularisation}]
def newtons_method(grad_fn, hess_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, n_iters, damping=1e-8):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, update_norms = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        H = hess_fn(x) if callable(hess_fn) else hess_fn
        H_reg = H + damping * np.eye(len(x))
        try:
            p = np.linalg.solve(H_reg, g)
        except np.linalg.LinAlgError:
            p = g
        update_norms.append(np.linalg.norm(alpha * p))
        x = x - alpha * p
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(update_norms)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Frank--Wolfe Algorithm}]
def frank_wolfe(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, bounds, beta, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, z_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        z = np.array([bounds[i][0] if g[i] > 0 else bounds[i][1]
                       for i in range(len(g))])
        z_hist.append(z.copy())
        x = beta * x + (1 - beta) * z
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(z_hist)
\end{lstlisting}
