\begin{abstract}
This report presents a comprehensive comparative study of optimisation algorithms applied across three benchmark problems of increasing difficulty: a linear regression quadratic loss (Benchmark A, condition number $\kappa \approx 1$), a toy neural network loss with sinusoidal perturbation (Benchmark B, $\kappa \approx 5$), and the Rosenbrock function (Benchmark C, $\kappa \approx 2000$). Six categories of methods are investigated: (1) adaptive step-size methods (Polyak, Adagrad, RMSprop, Heavy Ball), (2) momentum-based methods (Nesterov acceleration, Adam), (3) Newton's method with Hessian-based local quadratic approximations, (4) derivative-free approaches (forward finite differences, Nesterov random search, Nelder--Mead simplex, grid search), (5) constrained optimisation via projected gradient descent and penalty methods, and (6) the Frank--Wolfe conditional gradient algorithm for linear-constrained problems. Key findings include: the Polyak step size diverges under misspecified $f^\star$ but achieves best convergence on Rosenbrock ($f = 0.009$) when $f^\star = 0$ is exact; Nesterov momentum achieves $f = 0.479$ on Rosenbrock versus GD's $f = 3.39$ in the same 150 iterations; Newton's method solves the quadratic regression exactly in one step and reaches $f = 0.686$ on Rosenbrock in just 20 iterations versus GD's $f = 3.73$ in 80; and Frank--Wolfe with fixed mixing parameter exhibits $O(1/k)$ convergence with characteristic vertex-oscillation in the LP subproblem vertex selection.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================

Optimisation lies at the heart of modern data analysis and machine learning. The choice of algorithm determines not only the speed of convergence but also the quality of the solution found, the stability of training, and the computational resources required. This report systematically compares a representative selection of first-order and second-order methods, both gradient-based and derivative-free, applied to three benchmark problems of progressively increasing difficulty.

A fundamental challenge in optimisation is the \emph{ill-conditioning} of the objective: when the curvature varies dramatically across different directions, gradient-based methods are forced to take tiny steps to avoid oscillation in high-curvature directions, making progress slow along low-curvature directions. This is quantified by the \emph{condition number} $\kappa = L/\mu$, where $L$ is the Lipschitz constant of the gradient and $\mu$ is the strong convexity constant. Classical gradient descent with constant step size $\alpha = 1/L$ converges at linear rate $(1 - \mu/L)^k$, which deteriorates for large $\kappa$.

The methods studied in this report address ill-conditioning through complementary mechanisms: adaptive step sizes (Adagrad, RMSprop) normalise updates by historical gradient magnitudes; momentum methods (Heavy Ball, Nesterov, Adam) accumulate velocity to accelerate progress; Newton's method uses the full Hessian to remove curvature entirely; and derivative-free methods sidestep gradient computation altogether at the cost of slower per-iteration progress.

The report is structured to mirror the six assignment questions. Each section describes the algorithmic formulation, hyperparameter settings, experimental results across the three benchmarks, and a comparative analysis grounded in the theoretical properties of each method.

\section{Benchmark Problems and Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:benchmarks}
% ============================================================

All experiments use a fixed random seed (\texttt{np.random.seed(42)}) for reproducibility. Starting points are shared across questions for each benchmark.

\subsection{Benchmark A: Linear Regression Quadratic Loss}
A synthetic dataset with $m = 1000$ samples and $d = 2$ parameters is generated:
\begin{equation}
\theta^{\star} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad x^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_2), \quad y^{(i)} = (\theta^{\star})^T x^{(i)} + \varepsilon^{(i)}, \quad \varepsilon^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1).
\end{equation}
The loss function is the mean squared error:
\begin{equation}
J(\theta) = \frac{1}{2m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \bigl(\theta^T x^{(i)} - y^{(i)}\bigr)^2 = \frac{1}{2m}\|X\theta - y\|^2.
\end{equation}
This is a convex quadratic with constant Hessian $H = X^TX/m \in \mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}$. The gradient is $\nabla J(\theta) = X^T(X\theta - y)/m$ and the smoothness constant is $L = \lambda_{\max}(H)$, the strong convexity constant is $\mu = \lambda_{\min}(H)$. Since $x^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I_2)$ and $m = 1000 \gg d = 2$, we have $H \approx I_2$ (by the law of large numbers), giving $\kappa \approx 1$ --- the best-conditioned benchmark. Due to observation noise, the irreducible minimum is $J^{\star} = \sigma^2/2 \approx 0.4826$, not zero. Starting point: $\theta_0 = [0, 0]^T$.

\subsection{Benchmark B: Toy Neural Network Loss}
\begin{equation}
f(x_1, x_2) = (x_1 - 1)^2 + 5(x_2 - 2)^2 + \sin(x_1).
\end{equation}
The gradient is
\begin{equation}
\nabla f(x) = \bigl[2(x_1 - 1) + \cos(x_1),\; 10(x_2 - 2)\bigr]^T,
\end{equation}
and the Hessian is $H(x) = \operatorname{diag}(2 - \sin(x_1),\; 10)$. The sinusoidal term breaks pure quadratic structure, introducing mild nonlinearity. The eigenvalue ratio $\lambda_2/\lambda_1 = 10/(2-\sin(x_1))$ lies in $[10/3, 10/1] \approx [3.3, 10]$, creating moderate anisotropy with condition number $\kappa \approx 5$--$10$. The minimum satisfies $2(x_1^\star - 1) + \cos(x_1^\star) = 0$ and $x_2^\star = 2$, giving $x^\star \approx (0.582, 2.0)$ with $f^\star \approx 0.7244$. Starting point: $x_0 = [-1, 4]^T$.

\subsection{Benchmark C: Rosenbrock Function}
\begin{equation}
f(x_1, x_2) = (1 - x_1)^2 + 100(x_2 - x_1^2)^2.
\end{equation}
The global minimum is at $(1, 1)$ with $f^\star = 0$. The Hessian at the optimum has eigenvalues approximately $0.5$ and $1000$, giving condition number $\kappa \approx 2000$. This extreme ill-conditioning, combined with a narrow curved valley along $x_2 = x_1^2$, makes Rosenbrock the standard stress test for optimisation algorithms. Gradient descent with any fixed step size converges extremely slowly because the step required for stability along the steep direction is far too small to make progress along the flat valley floor. Starting point: $x_0 = [-1, 1]^T$.

\subsection{Benchmark D: Constrained Feasible Set}
For constrained problems (Questions 5 and 6):
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{X} = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^2 : 0.5 \leq x_1 \leq 5,\; -5 \leq x_2 \leq 10\}.
\end{equation}
This is a bounded box (polytope), for which projection and linear minimisation have closed-form $O(d)$ solutions.

\section{Question 1: Adaptive Step-Size Methods \hfill [30 marks]}
\label{sec:q1}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Theoretical Background}

For an $L$-smooth convex function (i.e., $\|\nabla f(x) - \nabla f(y)\| \leq L\|x - y\|$), gradient descent with constant step $\alpha \leq 1/L$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
f(x_k) - f^\star \leq \frac{\|x_0 - x^\star\|^2}{2\alpha k},
\end{equation}
giving a $O(1/k)$ convergence rate. For $\mu$-strongly convex functions ($f(y) \geq f(x) + \nabla f(x)^T(y-x) + \tfrac{\mu}{2}\|y-x\|^2$), the rate improves to linear: $f(x_k) - f^\star \leq (1-\mu\alpha)^k(f(x_0)-f^\star)$. The adaptive methods below attempt to improve upon the fixed-step baseline by automatically adjusting the learning rate per coordinate or per iteration.

\subsection{Algorithm Formulations}

Four adaptive methods are compared against a constant step-size gradient descent baseline across all three benchmarks for 120 iterations.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Polyak Step Size}
\label{alg:polyak}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, optimal value $f^{\star}$, tolerance $\varepsilon$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $\alpha_k \gets \dfrac{f(x_k) - f^{\star}}{\|g_k\|^2 + \varepsilon}$
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha_k\, g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The Polyak step size is theoretically motivated: it is the exact minimiser of $f$ along the gradient direction under the assumption that $f$ is convex and $f^\star$ is known. For strongly convex $f$, it achieves $O(1/k)$ convergence without knowledge of $L$ or $\mu$, provided $f^\star$ is exact. The step $\alpha_k = (f(x_k) - f^\star)/\|g_k\|^2$ normalises the progress-to-gradient ratio, automatically large near the start (large numerator) and shrinking towards the minimum.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Adagrad}
\label{alg:adagrad}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, base learning rate $\alpha_0$, tolerance $\varepsilon$, iterations $N$
\State $G_0 \gets \mathbf{0} \in \mathbb{R}^d$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $G_{k+1} \gets G_k + g_k \odot g_k$ \Comment{Element-wise squared gradient accumulation}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \dfrac{\alpha_0}{\sqrt{G_{k+1}} + \varepsilon} \odot g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Adagrad scales the learning rate for each coordinate $i$ by $1/\sqrt{\sum_k g_{k,i}^2}$. Coordinates that receive large gradients get smaller effective step sizes, and vice versa. This provides automatic normalisation for ill-conditioned problems. However, since $G_{k+1}$ is \emph{monotonically increasing}, the effective step $\alpha_0/(\sqrt{G_{k+1}}+\varepsilon)$ decreases monotonically to zero, which can cause premature convergence on non-stationary problems.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{RMSprop}
\label{alg:rmsprop}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, learning rate $\alpha_0$, decay $\beta \in (0,1)$, tolerance $\varepsilon$, iterations $N$
\State $v_0 \gets \mathbf{0} \in \mathbb{R}^d$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $v_{k+1} \gets \beta\, v_k + (1 - \beta)\, g_k \odot g_k$ \Comment{Exponential moving average of squared gradients}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \dfrac{\alpha_0}{\sqrt{v_{k+1}} + \varepsilon} \odot g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

RMSprop replaces Adagrad's accumulation with an exponential moving average with decay $\beta$, so only recent gradient magnitudes influence the effective step. This prevents the step from decaying to zero, making RMSprop more suitable for non-stationary objectives. The effective second moment estimate $v_k$ satisfies $v_k \approx (1-\beta)\sum_{j=0}^{k}\beta^{k-j} g_j^2$, exponentially downweighting older gradients.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Heavy Ball (Polyak Momentum)}
\label{alg:heavyball}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, momentum $\beta \in [0,1)$, iterations $N$
\State $z_0 \gets \mathbf{0} \in \mathbb{R}^d$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets \beta\, z_k + \alpha\, \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - z_{k+1}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The Heavy Ball method accumulates a velocity $z_k$ as a running sum of gradients with exponential downweighting. For a quadratic $f$ with Hessian eigenvalues in $[\mu, L]$, the optimal parameters are $\alpha^\star = 4/(\sqrt{L}+\sqrt{\mu})^2$ and $\beta^\star = ((\sqrt{L}-\sqrt{\mu})/(\sqrt{L}+\sqrt{\mu}))^2$, achieving convergence rate $((\sqrt{\kappa}-1)/(\sqrt{\kappa}+1))^2$ — a significant improvement over GD's rate $(\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1)$ for large $\kappa$.

\subsection{Comparative Analysis and Theoretical Implications}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Polyak step:} Theoretically optimal when $f^\star$ is known exactly, achieving convergence without knowledge of smoothness or strong convexity constants. The divergence under misspecified $f^\star$ is fundamental: the step $\alpha_k = (f(x_k) - f^\star_{\text{wrong}})/\|g_k\|^2$ attempts to jump to a minimum that does not exist, growing without bound as $\|g_k\| \to 0$.

\item \textbf{Adagrad:} Provides automatic per-coordinate normalisation, making it robust to ill-conditioning. Its monotonically decaying step guarantees convergence but limits adaptability. Best suited for sparse problems (e.g., NLP) where only a subset of coordinates receive non-zero gradients.

\item \textbf{RMSprop:} Overcomes Adagrad's monotone decay via exponential moving averages. The decay parameter $\beta = 0.9$ controls the effective time window: approximately $1/(1-0.9) = 10$ recent gradient magnitudes are averaged. More suitable for non-stationary problems than Adagrad, at the cost of an additional hyperparameter.

\item \textbf{Heavy Ball:} Provides momentum without per-coordinate adaptation. Matches the optimal rate for quadratic objectives but can exhibit oscillation on non-quadratic functions (observed on Rosenbrock). The momentum $\beta = 0.88$--$0.90$ allows significant step accumulation in consistent directions while eventually damping oscillations.

\item \textbf{Practical implication:} In practice, exact $f^\star$ is rarely known, limiting Polyak's applicability. RMSprop and Adagrad are more robust for general problems. Heavy Ball is competitive when the problem is nearly quadratic.
\end{itemize}

\section{Question 2: Momentum and Stochastic Methods \hfill [30 marks]}
\label{sec:q2}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Theoretical Background}

Nesterov's accelerated gradient descent achieves the \emph{optimal} $O(1/k^2)$ convergence rate for smooth convex minimisation, compared to gradient descent's $O(1/k)$. This is optimal in the sense that no first-order method can achieve a better worst-case rate on this problem class. The key innovation is computing the gradient at a \emph{lookahead} point $\tilde{x}_k = x_k + \beta_k(x_k - x_{k-1})$ rather than at $x_k$, allowing the algorithm to ``correct'' its momentum before overshooting.

Adam combines momentum (exponential moving average of gradients) with coordinate-wise adaptive scaling (exponential moving average of squared gradients), inheriting the strengths of both Heavy Ball and RMSprop. It is ubiquitous in deep learning despite lacking strong convergence guarantees for non-convex problems.

\subsection{Algorithm Formulations}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Nesterov Accelerated Gradient}
\label{alg:nesterov}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, maximum momentum $\beta_{\max}$, iterations $N$
\State $z_0 \gets \mathbf{0} \in \mathbb{R}^d$
\For{$k = 1, 2, \ldots, N$}
    \State $\beta_k \gets \min\!\left(\dfrac{k-1}{k+2},\; \beta_{\max}\right)$ \Comment{Adaptive momentum schedule}
    \State $\tilde{x}_k \gets x_k + \beta_k\, z_k$ \Comment{Lookahead (extrapolation) point}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets \beta_k\, z_k - \alpha\, \nabla f(\tilde{x}_k)$ \Comment{Update velocity at lookahead}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k + z_{k+1}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The momentum schedule $\beta_k = (k-1)/(k+2)$ increases gradually from $0$ (steepest descent at $k=1$) towards $\beta_{\max}$. The theoretical choice $\beta_k = (t_k - 1)/t_{k+1}$ with $t_{k+1}^2 - t_{k+1} \leq t_k^2$ (e.g., $t_k = (k+1)/2$) achieves the $O(1/k^2)$ rate. The $\beta_{\max}$ cap prevents over-acceleration on non-convex objectives.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Adam Optimiser}
\label{alg:adam}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, learning rate $\alpha$, moments $\beta_1, \beta_2 \in (0,1)$, $\varepsilon > 0$, iterations $N$
\State $m_0 \gets \mathbf{0},\; v_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$t = 1, 2, \ldots, N$}
    \State $g_t \gets \nabla f(x_t)$
    \State $m_t \gets \beta_1 m_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_1) g_t$ \Comment{First moment (mean) estimate}
    \State $v_t \gets \beta_2 v_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_2) g_t \odot g_t$ \Comment{Second moment (variance) estimate}
    \State $\hat{m}_t \gets m_t / (1 - \beta_1^t)$ \Comment{Bias correction for initialisation at zero}
    \State $\hat{v}_t \gets v_t / (1 - \beta_2^t)$
    \State $x_{t+1} \gets x_t - \alpha\, \hat{m}_t \oslash (\sqrt{\hat{v}_t} + \varepsilon)$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The bias correction terms $1/(1-\beta_i^t)$ are crucial in early iterations: without them, $m_1 = (1-\beta_1)g_1$ would be severely downscaled relative to the true gradient, slowing initial convergence. The effective per-coordinate step is $\alpha\hat{m}_{t,i}/(\sqrt{\hat{v}_{t,i}}+\varepsilon) \approx \alpha \cdot \text{sign}(g_{t,i})$ near convergence when gradients are consistent, giving Adam an effective step size bounded by $\alpha(1+\varepsilon)/\varepsilon \approx \alpha/\varepsilon$ per coordinate.

\subsection{Mini-Batch Stochastic Gradient Descent}
\label{subsec:sgd}

Mini-batch SGD on Benchmark A uses learning rate $\alpha = 0.06$, batch sizes $b \in \{5, 40\}$, 50 epochs, with data randomly permuted at each epoch.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Mini-Batch SGD}
\label{alg:sgd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Data $(X, y)$ with $n$ samples, initial $\theta_0$, learning rate $\alpha$, batch size $b$, epochs $E$
\For{$e = 1, 2, \ldots, E$}
    \State Randomly permute indices $\{1, \ldots, n\}$
    \For{each mini-batch $\mathcal{B} \subset \{1,\ldots,n\}$ of size $b$}
        \State $\hat{g} \gets \frac{1}{b} X_{\mathcal{B}}^T(X_{\mathcal{B}}\theta - y_{\mathcal{B}})$ \Comment{Stochastic gradient estimate}
        \State $\theta \gets \theta - \alpha\, \hat{g}$
    \EndFor
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The stochastic gradient $\hat{g}$ is an unbiased estimate of the true gradient $\nabla J(\theta)$ since $\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}] = \nabla J(\theta)$. Its variance $\mathrm{Var}(\hat{g}) \approx \sigma_g^2/b$ decreases with batch size, creating the fundamental bias-variance trade-off: small $b$ yields frequent updates with high variance, large $b$ yields fewer updates with low variance.

\subsection{Local Approximations of $g(x) = x^4$}

Taylor's theorem states that for a smooth function $g$:
\begin{align}
\text{First-order:}\quad &g(x) \approx g(x_0) + g'(x_0)(x - x_0) \\
\text{Second-order:}\quad &g(x) \approx g(x_0) + g'(x_0)(x - x_0) + \tfrac{1}{2}g''(x_0)(x - x_0)^2.
\end{align}
For $g(x) = x^4$ at $x_0 = 0.25$: $g(0.25) = 0.25^4 = 3.906 \times 10^{-3}$, $g'(0.25) = 4 \times 0.25^3 = 0.0625$, $g''(0.25) = 12 \times 0.25^2 = 0.75$.

\textbf{First-order approximation:}
\begin{equation}
g(x) \approx 3.906\times 10^{-3} + 0.0625(x - 0.25).
\end{equation}

\textbf{Second-order approximation:}
\begin{equation}
g(x) \approx 3.906\times 10^{-3} + 0.0625(x - 0.25) + 0.375(x - 0.25)^2.
\end{equation}

The Newton update minimises the second-order approximation: setting the derivative to zero,
\begin{equation}
g'(x_0) + g''(x_0)(x - x_0) = 0 \implies x_{\text{Newton}} = x_0 - \frac{g'(x_0)}{g''(x_0)} = 0.25 - \frac{0.0625}{0.75} = 0.25 - 0.0833 = 0.1667.
\end{equation}
This Newton step moves towards the true minimum at $x^\star = 0$ and constitutes the core of Newton's method.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/q3_approximation.pdf}
\caption{Q3: Local approximations of $g(x) = x^4$ at $x_0 = 0.25$. The first-order (tangent line) captures only the local slope and deviates rapidly from the true function, providing no curvature information. The second-order approximation (upward parabola) captures both slope and curvature, fitting well in a neighbourhood of $x_0$. Newton's update corresponds to stepping to the minimum of this parabola at $x = 0.1667$, which is a better approximation of the true minimum $x^\star = 0$ than any fixed-step gradient descent from $x_0 = 0.25$.}

\subsection{Newton's Method}

In $d$ dimensions, Newton's method replaces the gradient step $x_{k+1} = x_k - \alpha \nabla f(x_k)$ with the curvature-corrected step $x_{k+1} = x_k - \alpha H_k^{-1}\nabla f(x_k)$, where $H_k = \nabla^2 f(x_k)$ is the Hessian. This makes Newton's method invariant to affine transformations of the input space — a property gradient descent lacks.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Newton's Method with Damping and Hessian Regularisation}
\label{alg:newton}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, damping $\lambda \geq 0$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $H_k \gets \nabla^2 f(x_k) + \lambda I$ \Comment{Tikhonov regularisation for numerical stability}
    \State $p_k \gets H_k^{-1} g_k$ \Comment{Newton direction (descent direction since $H_k \succ 0$)}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, p_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The regularisation $\lambda I$ (with $\lambda = 10^{-8}$) ensures $H_k$ is positive definite even when the Hessian has near-zero eigenvalues. The damping parameter $\alpha < 1$ is a line search approximation: the pure Newton step ($\alpha = 1$) can diverge on strongly non-quadratic functions because the second-order model ceases to be accurate far from $x_k$. Damping restricts the step to a region where the model remains reliable.

\textbf{Convergence theory:} For a twice continuously differentiable function with Lipschitz-continuous Hessian, Newton's method in the \emph{quadratic convergence regime} (sufficiently close to a non-degenerate local minimum) satisfies:
\begin{equation}
\|x_{k+1} - x^\star\| \leq C \|x_k - x^\star\|^2
\end{equation}
for some constant $C > 0$. This \emph{quadratic} convergence means the number of correct decimal digits doubles every iteration, compared to \emph{linear} convergence $(x_k - x^\star) \leq (1-\mu/L)^k(x_0 - x^\star)$ for gradient descent.

\subsection{Why Newton's Method Works: Condition Number Reduction}

Newton's method effectively solves a \emph{preconditioned} gradient descent problem. The update $x_{k+1} = x_k - H_k^{-1}g_k$ is equivalent to gradient descent on the function $\tilde{f}(z) = f(H_k^{1/2}z)$ (after the substitution $x = H_k^{-1/2}z$), which has an identity Hessian and hence condition number $\kappa = 1$. This is why Newton's method is indifferent to anisotropy and achieves rapid convergence on ill-conditioned problems --- it automatically applies a curvature-adaptive preconditioner at each step.

\subsection{Part A: Finite Difference and Nesterov Random Search (Benchmark B)}

\subsubsection{Finite Difference Gradient Approximation}

The forward finite-difference approximation replaces each partial derivative with:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x) \approx \frac{f(x + \delta e_i) - f(x)}{\delta},
\label{eq:fd}
\end{equation}
where $e_i$ is the $i$-th standard basis vector. By Taylor's theorem, the truncation error is $O(\delta)$:
\begin{equation}
\frac{f(x + \delta e_i) - f(x)}{\delta} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x) + \frac{\delta}{2}\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i^2}(x) + O(\delta^2).
\end{equation}
Additionally, floating-point arithmetic introduces rounding error of order $O(\varepsilon_{\text{mach}}/\delta)$. The total error is thus $O(\delta) + O(\varepsilon_{\text{mach}}/\delta)$, minimised at $\delta^\star \sim \sqrt{\varepsilon_{\text{mach}}} \approx 10^{-8}$. The experiment tests $\delta = 0.05$ (small, good approximation) and $\delta = 0.8$ (large, poor approximation where truncation error dominates). Each FD gradient evaluation requires $d + 1 = 3$ function evaluations for $d = 2$ dimensions.

\subsubsection{Nesterov Random Search}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Nesterov Zeroth-Order Random Search}
\label{alg:nrs}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, perturbation $\delta$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State Sample $u_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$; normalise $u_k \gets u_k / \|u_k\|$ \Comment{Uniform direction on unit sphere}
    \State $\hat{g}_k \gets \dfrac{f(x_k + \delta u_k) - f(x_k)}{\delta}\, u_k$ \Comment{Stochastic directional derivative}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \hat{g}_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The estimator $\hat{g}_k$ approximates the gradient via a single random direction. It satisfies $\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_k] \approx \nabla f_\delta(x_k)$ where $f_\delta(x) = \mathbb{E}_{u\sim\text{Uniform}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}[f(x+\delta u)]$ is a smoothed version of $f$. The key advantage is dimension-independence: exactly \emph{two} function evaluations per iteration regardless of $d$, versus $d+1$ for FD. The cost is higher variance in the gradient estimate, since only one direction is probed per step. The convergence rate of random search is $O(d/k)$ for smooth convex objectives — a $d$-fold slowdown versus exact GD's $O(1/k)$.

\subsection{Part B: Derivative-Free Methods on Benchmark C (Rosenbrock)}

\subsubsection{Nelder--Mead Simplex Method}

The Nelder--Mead algorithm maintains a simplex of $n+1 = 3$ vertices in $\mathbb{R}^2$ and iteratively deforms it through four geometric operations:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Reflection} ($\alpha_r = 1$): Move the worst vertex $x_n$ through the centroid $\bar{x}$ of the remaining vertices: $x_r = \bar{x} + \alpha_r(\bar{x} - x_n)$.
\item \textbf{Expansion} ($\gamma = 2$): If reflection improved, try $x_e = \bar{x} + \gamma(\bar{x} - x_n)$ to exploit the improvement further.
\item \textbf{Contraction} ($\rho = 0.5$): If reflection worsened, contract either outside ($x_c = \bar{x} + \rho(x_r - \bar{x})$) or inside ($x_c = \bar{x} + \rho(x_n - \bar{x})$).
\item \textbf{Shrink} ($\sigma = 0.5$): If contraction failed, contract all non-best vertices towards the best: $x_i \gets x_1 + \sigma(x_i - x_1)$.
\end{enumerate}
No gradient information is required; the algorithm adapts solely through function value comparisons. Initial simplex step size: 0.35; iterations: 160.

\subsection{Problem Formulation and KKT Conditions}

The constrained problem is:
\begin{equation}
\min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^2}\; f(x) = (x_1 - 1)^2 + 5(x_2 - 2)^2 + \sin(x_1) \quad \text{subject to}\quad x_1 \geq 0.5.
\end{equation}
This is a semi-infinite constraint $g(x) = 0.5 - x_1 \leq 0$. The KKT (Karush--Kuhn--Tucker) conditions for a local minimum $x^\star$ state that there exists a multiplier $\mu^\star \geq 0$ such that:
\begin{align}
\nabla f(x^\star) + \mu^\star \nabla g(x^\star) &= 0 \quad \text{(stationarity)}, \label{eq:kkt1}\\
g(x^\star) &\leq 0 \quad \text{(primal feasibility)}, \label{eq:kkt2}\\
\mu^\star &\geq 0 \quad \text{(dual feasibility)}, \label{eq:kkt3}\\
\mu^\star g(x^\star) &= 0 \quad \text{(complementary slackness)}. \label{eq:kkt4}
\end{align}
Here $\nabla g(x) = [-1, 0]^T$. Since the unconstrained minimum of $f$ is $x^\star_{\text{unc}} \approx (0.582, 2.0)$ with $x_1 = 0.582 > 0.5$, the constraint is inactive at the unconstrained minimum. Therefore the constrained optimum is the same: $x^\star_{\text{con}} = (0.582, 2.0)$, $\mu^\star = 0$ (constraint is inactive, so complementary slackness is satisfied trivially). Starting from the infeasible $x_0 = (0.2, 4.0)$ (which violates $x_1 \geq 0.5$), both methods must first achieve feasibility before optimising.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Projected Gradient Descent}
\label{alg:pgd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, projection $\Pi_{\mathcal{F}}$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \nabla f(x_k)$ \Comment{Unconstrained gradient step}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets \Pi_{\mathcal{F}}(z_{k+1}) = \bigl[\max(0.5, z_1),\; z_2\bigr]^T$ \Comment{Project: $x_1 \leftarrow \max(0.5, x_1)$}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The projection $\Pi_{\mathcal{F}}(z) = \arg\min_{x:\, x_1 \geq 0.5} \|x - z\|^2$ has the closed form $\Pi_{\mathcal{F}}(z) = [\max(0.5, z_1), z_2]^T$ for the half-space $x_1 \geq 0.5$. Projected GD guarantees $x_k \in \mathcal{F}$ for all $k \geq 1$; feasibility is a hard constraint, enforced exactly at every step. For an $L$-smooth convex $f$ over a closed convex set, projected GD with $\alpha = 1/L$ satisfies $f(x_k) - f^\star \leq \|x_0 - x^\star\|^2/(2\alpha k)$.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Penalty Method (Exact Penalty / $L_1$ Penalty)}
\label{alg:penalty}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, penalty weight $\lambda > 0$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State Penalised objective: $F_{\lambda}(x) = f(x) + \lambda\, \max(0,\; 0.5 - x_1)$
    \State Penalised gradient: $\nabla F_\lambda(x) = \nabla f(x) - \lambda\, \mathbf{1}[x_1 < 0.5]\, e_1$
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \nabla F_\lambda(x_k)$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The penalty $\lambda\max(0, g(x))$ is an exact $L_1$ penalty: for sufficiently large $\lambda > \mu^\star$ (the true KKT multiplier), the unconstrained minimiser of $F_\lambda$ coincides with the constrained minimiser $x^\star$. Since $\mu^\star = 0$ here (inactive constraint), any $\lambda > 0$ theoretically recovers the correct solution. In practice, larger $\lambda$ enforces feasibility more aggressively but may require smaller step sizes for stability.

\textbf{Settings:} Projected GD ($\alpha = 0.08$), Penalty with $\lambda \in \{0.15, 1.8, 4.5\}$ (step sizes $\alpha = 0.05$ for $\lambda \leq 1.8$; $\alpha = 0.03$ for $\lambda = 4.5$), Unconstrained GD ($\alpha = 0.07$). All run for 100 iterations.

\subsection{Comparative Discussion}

\textbf{Projected GD} enforces feasibility exactly at every iteration through the projection oracle. This is the fundamental advantage: no matter how large the gradient step, the projection always returns to the feasible set. The cost is requiring an efficient projection oracle; for the half-space $x_1 \geq 0.5$, this is trivial (a single $\max$ operation), but for general convex constraints (e.g., semidefinite cones), projection can be as expensive as the optimisation step itself.

\textbf{Penalty methods} trade exact feasibility for simplicity: only a modified gradient is needed, requiring no projection capability. The weight $\lambda$ controls the trade-off between feasibility enforcement and solution quality. The relationship between $\lambda$ and step size stability is fundamental: larger $\lambda$ increases the Lipschitz constant of $\nabla F_\lambda$ (since the penalty term adds a discontinuous gradient at $x_1 = 0.5$), necessitating smaller $\alpha$ for stability. This explains the progression $\alpha = 0.05 \to 0.03$ as $\lambda = 1.8 \to 4.5$.

In theory, augmented Lagrangian methods (ADMM, method of multipliers) overcome the need for large $\lambda$ by updating the multiplier estimate at each outer iteration, achieving feasibility with bounded $\lambda$. For this experiment, simple penalty methods with $\lambda = 4.5$ suffice since the constraint is inactive at the true optimum.

\subsection{Feasible Set and Linear Programme}

Feasible set: $\mathcal{X} = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^2 : 0.5 \leq x_1 \leq 5,\; -5 \leq x_2 \leq 10\}$ (a bounded box polytope with 4 vertices: $(0.5, -5)$, $(5, -5)$, $(5, 10)$, $(0.5, 10)$).

\textbf{Linear Programme:} For $f(x) = a^T x = x_1 + 2x_2$ with $a = [1, 2]^T$, the minimum over $\mathcal{X}$ is obtained independently per coordinate:
\begin{equation}
x_i^\star = \begin{cases} l_i & \text{if } a_i > 0 \\ u_i & \text{if } a_i < 0 \end{cases} \implies x^\star = (0.5,\; -5), \quad f^\star = 0.5 + 2(-5) = -9.5.
\end{equation}
This illustrates the \emph{fundamental theorem of linear programming}: the optimum of a linear objective over a bounded polytope is always attained at a vertex. Since $\mathcal{X}$ has 4 vertices, an exhaustive vertex enumeration would solve the LP in 4 function evaluations — exponentially fewer than any continuous-space search in higher dimensions.

\subsection{Frank--Wolfe (Conditional Gradient) Algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Frank--Wolfe (Conditional Gradient)}
\label{alg:fw}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0 \in \mathcal{X}$, mixing parameter $\beta \in (0,1)$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_k \gets \arg\min_{x \in \mathcal{X}}\; \nabla f(x_k)^T x$ \Comment{Linear minimisation oracle (LMO)}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets \beta\, x_k + (1-\beta)\, z_k$ \Comment{Fixed-step convex combination; $x_{k+1} \in \mathcal{X}$}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\textbf{Key properties:}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Projection-free:} The iterate $x_{k+1} = \beta x_k + (1-\beta)z_k$ is a convex combination of $x_k \in \mathcal{X}$ and $z_k \in \mathcal{X}$ (vertices), hence $x_{k+1} \in \mathcal{X}$ by convexity of $\mathcal{X}$. No projection oracle is needed.
\item \textbf{LP oracle:} The subproblem $\min_{x \in \mathcal{X}} \nabla f(x_k)^T x$ is a linear programme over $\mathcal{X}$. For the box $\mathcal{X}$, this decomposes per coordinate:
\begin{equation}
(z_k)_i = \begin{cases} l_i & \text{if } (\nabla f(x_k))_i > 0 \\ u_i & \text{if } (\nabla f(x_k))_i \leq 0 \end{cases},
\end{equation}
requiring only $O(d)$ operations --- much cheaper than projection for complex polytopes (e.g., nuclear norm ball, flow polytopes).
\item \textbf{Convergence rate:} For $L$-smooth convex objectives with a fixed step-size schedule $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$, Frank--Wolfe achieves:
\begin{equation}
f(x_k) - f^\star \leq \frac{2LC^2}{k+2}, \quad C = \max_{x,y \in \mathcal{X}} \|x - y\|,
\end{equation}
giving an $O(1/k)$ convergence rate — slower than projected GD's $O(1/k)$ but with each iteration costing only an LP solve rather than a projection. The fixed $\beta$ used here (rather than decreasing $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$) introduces a non-vanishing oscillation term.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Frank--Wolfe gap:} The quantity $h_k = \nabla f(x_k)^T(x_k - z_k) \geq 0$ (the Frank--Wolfe duality gap) provides an upper bound on $f(x_k) - f^\star$ at zero computational cost. When $h_k = 0$, the KKT conditions for the constrained problem are satisfied.

\subsection{Interior vs Boundary Behaviour}

\textbf{Interior optimum:} Frank--Wolfe converges but exhibits a characteristic ``zig-zag'' pattern. \textbf{Boundary optimum:} Frank--Wolfe is well-suited to boundary optima because the LP oracle naturally selects vertices near the constrained optimum, and the iterate gradually approaches the boundary without requiring a projection step. The algorithm demonstrates that even for boundary-constrained problems, the projection-free property provides a significant structural advantage. The residual gap ($f = 0.364$ vs $f^\star = 0.25$) after 140 iterations with fixed $\beta = 0.93$ would be eliminated by using the standard decreasing schedule $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$, which ensures vanishing step size and exact convergence.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

This report systematically evaluated six families of optimisation algorithms across three benchmark problems of increasing difficulty. The key findings, organised by method family, are as follows.

\textbf{Adaptive step-size methods (Q1):} The Polyak step size is the most theoretically elegant adaptive method, achieving the best convergence on Rosenbrock ($f = 0.009$) when the optimal value is known. Its sensitivity to $f^\star$ misspecification is a fundamental limitation: on noisy problems where $f^\star$ is unknown, divergence is catastrophic. Adagrad and Heavy Ball provide robust alternatives, matching or exceeding the GD baseline on all benchmarks while requiring no knowledge of $f^\star$. RMSprop's non-monotone step makes it more suitable for non-stationary problems but underperforms here due to conservative base rates.

\textbf{Momentum methods (Q2):} Nesterov acceleration achieves the optimal $O(1/k^2)$ rate for convex problems, providing a 7-fold improvement over GD on Rosenbrock in the same number of iterations. Adam combines momentum and adaptive scaling, but its benefit is less pronounced on small-dimensional deterministic problems than in high-dimensional stochastic settings where its coordinate-wise adaptation and variance reduction shine. Mini-batch SGD demonstrates the fundamental batch-size trade-off: smaller batches converge faster in epochs but with more variance near the minimum.

\textbf{Newton's method (Q3):} The most impactful finding is Newton's one-step exact solution of the quadratic regression problem. Its quadratic convergence rate, effectively eliminating the condition number by pre-multiplying with $H^{-1}$, makes it the method of choice when Hessians are available and $d$ is moderate. On Rosenbrock, damped Newton achieves a 5-fold objective improvement over GD in $1/4$ the iterations, despite not entering the quadratic convergence regime in 20 steps. The cost scales as $O(d^3)$ per iteration (linear solve), which becomes prohibitive for large-scale problems.

\textbf{Derivative-free methods (Q4):} Finite differences with small $\delta$ closely replicate exact GD at $d+1$ function evaluations per step, making it the preferred derivative-free method when $d$ is small. Nesterov random search scales better with $d$ (2 evaluations per step) but converges $d$-fold slower in iterations. Nelder--Mead provides derivative-free trajectory optimisation for small $d$, adapting its simplex geometry to the landscape without requiring any smoothness assumptions. Grid search guarantees global coverage but is exponentially expensive in $d$.

\textbf{Constrained optimisation (Q5):} Projected GD provides the gold standard for constrained optimisation with efficient projection oracles: feasibility is guaranteed from the first iteration. Penalty methods offer a simpler alternative at the cost of approximate feasibility, requiring careful tuning of $\lambda$ relative to the step size. Both methods converge to the same constrained optimum here since the constraint is inactive at the KKT point, but for problems where the constraint is active (boundary optima), the distinction matters more fundamentally.

\textbf{Frank--Wolfe algorithm (Q6):} The projection-free property makes Frank--Wolfe uniquely valuable for constraint sets where projection is expensive but LP-subproblem solutions are cheap (e.g., flow polytopes, nuclear norm balls). Its $O(1/k)$ convergence with fixed $\beta$ is competitive with projected GD's $O(1/k)$ per LP oracle call. The vertex-oscillation phenomenon near interior optima is an inherent characteristic of the algorithm, reduced by smaller step sizes ($\beta$ closer to 1) at the cost of slower initial progress.

\textbf{Overall benchmark comparison:} Rosenbrock provides the most discriminating test: condition number $\kappa \approx 2000$ separates methods fundamentally. The ranking on Rosenbrock (Polyak $>$ Nesterov $>$ Heavy Ball $>$ Adam $>$ Adagrad $>$ GD $\approx$ RMSprop) reflects the degree to which each method exploits curvature information or accumulates momentum in a consistent direction. Second-order methods (Newton) and zero-order methods with adequate iterations (Nelder--Mead) can outperform all first-order methods given sufficient computation, but at higher per-iteration cost.

def gradient_descent(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        x = x - alpha * grad_fn(x)
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Polyak Step Size}]
def polyak_step(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, f_star, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        alpha_k = (loss_fn(x) - f_star) / (np.dot(g, g) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(alpha_k)
        x = x - alpha_k * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

def adagrad(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha0, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    G = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        G += g**2
        eff_alpha = alpha0 / (np.sqrt(G) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(np.mean(eff_alpha))
        x = x - eff_alpha * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={RMSprop}]
def rmsprop(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha0, beta, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    v = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        v = beta * v + (1 - beta) * g**2
        eff_alpha = alpha0 / (np.sqrt(v) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(np.mean(eff_alpha))
        x = x - eff_alpha * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Heavy Ball / Polyak Momentum}]
def heavy_ball(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    z = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        z = beta * z + alpha * g
        x = x - z
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Nesterov Accelerated Gradient}]
def nesterov_momentum(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta_max, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    z = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for k in range(1, n_iters + 1):
        beta_k = min((k - 1) / (k + 2), beta_max)
        lookahead = x + beta_k * z
        g = grad_fn(lookahead)
        z = beta_k * z - alpha * g
        x = x + z
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Adam Optimiser}]
def adam_optimiser(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta1, beta2, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    m_vec, v_vec = np.zeros_like(x), np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for t in range(1, n_iters + 1):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        m_vec = beta1 * m_vec + (1 - beta1) * g
        v_vec = beta2 * v_vec + (1 - beta2) * g**2
        m_hat = m_vec / (1 - beta1**t)
        v_hat = v_vec / (1 - beta2**t)
        x = x - alpha * m_hat / (np.sqrt(v_hat) + eps)
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Newton's Method with Hessian Regularisation}]
def newtons_method(grad_fn, hess_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, n_iters, damping=1e-8):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, update_norms = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        H = hess_fn(x) if callable(hess_fn) else hess_fn
        H_reg = H + damping * np.eye(len(x))
        try:
            p = np.linalg.solve(H_reg, g)
        except np.linalg.LinAlgError:
            p = g
        update_norms.append(np.linalg.norm(alpha * p))
        x = x - alpha * p
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(update_norms)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Frank--Wolfe (Conditional Gradient)}]
def frank_wolfe(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, bounds, beta, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, z_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        z = np.array([bounds[i][0] if g[i] > 0 else bounds[i][1]
                       for i in range(len(g))])
        z_hist.append(z.copy())
        x = beta * x + (1 - beta) * z
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(z_hist)
\end{lstlisting}
