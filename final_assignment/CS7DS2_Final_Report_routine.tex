\begin{abstract}
This report presents a comprehensive comparative study of six families of optimisation algorithms applied to three canonical benchmark problems: a linear regression quadratic loss (Benchmark A, $\kappa \approx 1.12$), a toy neural-network loss with sinusoidal perturbation (Benchmark B, $\kappa \approx 6.9$), and the Rosenbrock function (Benchmark C, $\kappa \approx 2508$). The methods investigated span: \textbf{adaptive step-size} methods (Polyak, Adagrad, RMSprop, Heavy Ball); \textbf{accelerated and stochastic} methods (Nesterov acceleration, Adam, mini-batch SGD); \textbf{second-order} methods (Newton with Hessian damping/regularisation); \textbf{derivative-free} approaches (finite differences, Nesterov random search, Nelder--Mead simplex, grid search); \textbf{constrained optimisation} via projected gradient descent and quadratic penalty; and the \textbf{Frank--Wolfe} (conditional gradient) algorithm for linearly-constrained problems.

Key findings: (i) the Polyak step diverges when the true minimum $f^\star$ is misspecified: $\alpha_k \to (f^\star_{\rm true} - \tilde{f}^\star)/\varepsilon \gg 1$ as $\|\nabla f\| \to 0$, causing oscillations $9.9\times$ above the noise floor on Benchmark A; (ii) on Rosenbrock ($\kappa\approx 2508$), damped Newton (20 iters) achieves $f=0.686$ versus GD's $f=3.732$ (80 iters), a $5.4\times$ improvement, while its quadratic convergence rate is confirmed by a slope-2 phase portrait; (iii) Nesterov ($f=0.479$) outperforms Adam ($f=1.578$) on Rosenbrock because Adam's diagonal Hessian approximation cannot capture the dominant off-diagonal curvature $H_{12} = -400x_1 \approx -400$; (iv) on Benchmark B, GD requires $\approx 32$ iterations vs.\ Nesterov's $\approx 12$ for $10^{-4}$ accuracy, matching theoretical rates $\rho_{\rm GD}\approx 0.747$ and $\rho_{\rm Nes}\approx 0.449$; (v) projected gradient descent enforces feasibility from iteration 1 as a hard constraint, while penalty methods require $\lambda \geq 4.5$ for near-zero violation within 100 iterations; (vi) the Frank--Wolfe gap $g_k = \nabla f(x_k)^T(x_k - z_k)$ provides a certified suboptimality bound at no extra cost, a unique feature absent from projected gradient methods.

Theoretical contributions include: convergence proof sketches for Newton (quadratic), Nesterov ($O(1/k^2)$), and Frank--Wolfe ($O(1/k)$); step-size sensitivity analysis demonstrating that Rosenbrock's stability boundary $\alpha < 2/L = 0.002$ is five orders of magnitude tighter than Benchmark A's; and finite-difference truncation--round-off trade-off analysis identifying the optimal perturbation $\delta^\star \sim \sqrt{\varepsilon_{\rm machine}} \approx 10^{-8}$. All methods are evaluated on convergence speed, trajectory behaviour, stability, and computational cost, with practical recommendations for each scenario.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================

Optimisation lies at the heart of modern machine learning: training a model reduces to minimising a loss function, and the choice of optimiser fundamentally determines both convergence speed and solution quality. This assignment investigates six families of methods across three benchmark problems of increasing difficulty, from the perfectly-conditioned quadratic (Benchmark A, $\kappa \approx 1$) to the highly ill-conditioned Rosenbrock banana valley (Benchmark C, $\kappa \approx 2508$).

The algorithms studied progress from the simplest (constant-step gradient descent) to increasingly sophisticated strategies that exploit curvature information (Newton), reduce gradient variance (mini-batch SGD), enforce feasibility (projected gradient), or require no derivatives at all (Nelder--Mead). Understanding \emph{why} one method outperforms another on a given problem class---not merely \emph{that} it does---is the central goal of this study.

Throughout, two theoretical quantities inform the analysis. For a twice-differentiable function $f:\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ with $\mu$-strongly-convex and $L$-smooth Hessian (i.e.\ $\mu I \preceq \nabla^2 f(x) \preceq LI$), the \textbf{condition number} $\kappa = L/\mu$ governs first-order convergence rates: gradient descent converges as $\|x_k - x^\star\|^2 \leq \left(\frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1}\right)^{2k}\|x_0 - x^\star\|^2$, while Nesterov acceleration improves this to $\left(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa}+1}\right)^{2k}$. The three benchmarks span a wide range of condition numbers:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Benchmark} & $\kappa$ & $\rho_{\rm GD}=\frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1}$ & $\rho_{\rm Nes}=\frac{\sqrt\kappa-1}{\sqrt\kappa+1}$ & GD iters/$10^{-4}$ & Nes iters/$10^{-4}$ \\
\midrule
A (Quadratic) & 1.12 & 0.056 & 0.057 & 3 & 3 \\
B (Toy NN) & 6.90 & 0.747 & 0.449 & 32 & 12 \\
C (Rosenbrock) & 2508 & 0.9992 & 0.961 & 36{,}842 & 233 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

On Benchmark A where $\kappa \approx 1.12$, all first-order methods converge rapidly; on Rosenbrock where $\kappa \approx 2508$, GD would require over 36{,}000 iterations to reduce error by $10^{-4}$, whereas Nesterov needs only 233. Figure~\ref{fig:convergence_rate_comparison} visualises these theoretical predictions as a function of $\kappa$.

A key conceptual extension: the \textbf{Polyak--Łojasiewicz (PL) condition} $\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla f(x)\|^2 \geq \mu(f(x) - f^\star)$ is a weaker requirement than strong convexity that still enables linear convergence of gradient descent. Under the PL condition, GD satisfies $f(x_k) - f^\star \leq (1 - \mu/L)^k(f(x_0) - f^\star)$ without requiring the function to be globally convex or have a unique minimiser. Rosenbrock satisfies the PL condition \emph{locally} near the minimum, explaining why gradient methods eventually converge there despite the non-convexity along the valley approach.

\subsection{Benchmark A: Linear Regression Quadratic Loss}

A synthetic dataset with $m = 1000$ samples and $n=2$ parameters is generated:
\begin{equation}
  \theta^{\star} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
  x^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_2), \quad
  y^{(i)} = {\theta^{\star}}^{\!T} x^{(i)} + \varepsilon^{(i)}, \quad
  \varepsilon^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1).
\end{equation}
The loss function is the mean squared error (MSE):
\begin{equation}
  J(\theta) = \frac{1}{2m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \left(\theta^T x^{(i)} - y^{(i)}\right)^2
            = \frac{1}{2m}\|X\theta - y\|^2.
\end{equation}
The gradient and Hessian are:
\begin{equation}
  \nabla J(\theta) = \frac{1}{m}X^T(X\theta - y), \qquad
  H = \nabla^2 J(\theta) = \frac{1}{m}X^TX \quad \text{(constant)}.
\end{equation}
Because $H$ is constant and positive semi-definite, this is a \emph{convex quadratic}. The Lipschitz constant is $L = \lambda_{\max}(H) \approx 1.033$ and the strong-convexity constant is $\mu = \lambda_{\min}(H) \approx 0.925$, giving $\kappa = L/\mu \approx 1.12$ --- a well-conditioned problem. Due to observation noise $\varepsilon^{(i)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$, the irreducible minimum is $J^{\star} \approx 0.4826$ (the OLS noise floor), not zero. Starting point: $\theta_0 = [0, 0]^T$.

\subsection{Benchmark B: Toy Neural Network Loss}
\begin{equation}
  f(x_1, x_2) = (x_1 - 1)^2 + 5(x_2 - 2)^2 + \sin(x_1).
  \label{eq:benchmark_B}
\end{equation}
The gradient is $\nabla f = [2(x_1 - 1) + \cos(x_1),\ 10(x_2 - 2)]^T$ and the Hessian is:
\begin{equation}
  H(x) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 - \sin(x_1) & 0 \\ 0 & 10 \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The eigenvalues of $H$ vary with position: the $(1,1)$ entry $2-\sin(x_1)$ ranges in $[1, 3]$ while the $(2,2)$ entry is the fixed constant $10$. The condition number varies between approximately $10/3 \approx 3.3$ and $10/1 = 10$, creating \emph{moderate anisotropy}. The minimum is obtained by solving $2(x_1^\star - 1) + \cos(x_1^\star) = 0$ (numerically) and $x_2^\star = 2$ (algebraically), yielding $x^\star \approx (0.5824, 2.0)$ with $f^\star \approx 0.7244$. At the minimum, $H(x^\star) = \text{diag}(1.45, 10)$ and $\kappa \approx 6.9$. Starting point: $x_0 = [-1, 4]^T$.

\subsection{Benchmark C: Rosenbrock Function}
The Rosenbrock function \cite{rosenbrock1960} is the canonical optimisation stress test:
\begin{equation}
  f(x_1, x_2) = (1 - x_1)^2 + 100(x_2 - x_1^2)^2.
  \label{eq:rosenbrock}
\end{equation}
The gradient is:
\begin{equation}
  \nabla f = \begin{bmatrix}
    -2(1 - x_1) - 400 x_1(x_2 - x_1^2) \\
    200(x_2 - x_1^2)
  \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
and the Hessian is:
\begin{equation}
  H(x) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 + 1200x_1^2 - 400x_2 & -400x_1 \\ -400x_1 & 200 \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
At the global minimum $(1,1)$ ($f^\star = 0$), the Hessian eigenvalues are approximately $0.40$ and $1002$, giving $\kappa \approx 2508$. This extreme ill-conditioning, combined with a narrow curved valley along $x_2 = x_1^2$, makes Rosenbrock the canonical stress-test for optimisation algorithms. Starting point: $x_0 = [-1, 1]^T$.

\begin{remark}
The Rosenbrock condition number $\kappa \approx 2508$ means the GD convergence factor is $\rho_{\rm GD} = (\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1) \approx 0.9992$, so each iteration reduces the error by only $0.08\%$. Reaching $10^{-4}$ accuracy from $\|x_0 - x^\star\| = 2\sqrt{2}$ would require over 36{,}000 iterations --- far beyond the 80--200 budgeted.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Algorithm Formulations}

Four adaptive methods are compared against a constant step-size gradient descent (GD) baseline across all three benchmarks for 120 iterations. The baseline GD update is:
\begin{equation}
  x_{k+1} = x_k - \alpha \nabla f(x_k).
\end{equation}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Polyak Step Size}
\label{alg:polyak}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, optimal value $f^{\star}$, tolerance $\varepsilon$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $\alpha_k \gets \dfrac{f(x_k) - f^{\star}}{\|g_k\|^2 + \varepsilon}$
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha_k\, g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The Polyak step size is theoretically motivated by the bound $f(x_{k+1}) \leq f^\star + \frac{\|g_k\|^2}{4\alpha_k}$ for any descent step; choosing $\alpha_k = (f(x_k) - f^\star)/\|g_k\|^2$ minimises the right-hand side, making $f(x_{k+1}) \leq f^\star + (f(x_k) - f^\star)/4$, which is optimal for the Polyak--\L ojasiewicz class of functions.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Adagrad}
\label{alg:adagrad}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, base learning rate $\alpha_0$, tolerance $\varepsilon$, iterations $N$
\State $G_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $G_{k+1} \gets G_k + g_k \odot g_k$ \Comment{Per-coordinate accumulation of squared gradients}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \dfrac{\alpha_0}{\sqrt{G_{k+1}} + \varepsilon} \odot g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Adagrad \cite{duchi2011} adapts the learning rate \emph{per coordinate}: coordinates with historically large gradients receive smaller updates. The effective learning rate $\alpha_k^{(i)} = \alpha_0/\sqrt{G_k^{(i)} + \varepsilon}$ is \emph{monotonically decreasing}, which guarantees progress on convex problems but can prematurely freeze learning on non-stationary objectives.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{RMSprop}
\label{alg:rmsprop}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, learning rate $\alpha_0$, decay $\beta$, tolerance $\varepsilon$, iterations $N$
\State $v_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $v_{k+1} \gets \beta\, v_k + (1 - \beta)\, g_k \odot g_k$ \Comment{Exponential moving average of squared gradients}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \dfrac{\alpha_0}{\sqrt{v_{k+1}} + \varepsilon} \odot g_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

RMSprop differs from Adagrad by replacing the full sum $G_k$ with an exponential moving average $v_k$. This prevents the effective learning rate from decaying to zero, making it suitable for non-stationary objectives. With $\beta = 0.9$, recent gradients receive weight $(1-0.9) = 0.1$ per step, giving an effective window of approximately $1/(1-\beta) = 10$ steps.

\textbf{Adagrad as approximate coordinate Newton.} The Adagrad update $x \gets x - \frac{\alpha_0}{\sqrt{G} + \varepsilon} \odot g$ can be interpreted as a Newton step with a diagonal Hessian approximation $\hat{H} = \text{diag}(\sqrt{G}/\alpha_0)$. The accumulated sum $G^{(i)} = \sum_{j=0}^k (g_j^{(i)})^2 \approx k \cdot \mathbb{E}[(g^{(i)})^2] \approx k \cdot H_{ii}$ (by the Fisher information identity for quadratics), so $\sqrt{G^{(i)}} \approx \sqrt{k \cdot H_{ii}}$ and the effective step size $\alpha_0/\sqrt{G^{(i)}} \approx \alpha_0/(\sqrt{k} \cdot \sqrt{H_{ii}})$. This approximates coordinate-wise Newton scaling but with a $1/\sqrt{k}$ decay --- which is the source of both Adagrad's fast early convergence (correct curvature scaling) and its plateau (the $1/\sqrt{k}$ decay dominates near the optimum). RMSprop replaces $\sqrt{k}$ with $1/(1-\beta)$ (the effective window length), keeping the curvature scaling without the decay.

\textbf{Adam = RMSprop + Nesterov momentum (approximately).} Adam's update $\alpha\, m_t/\sqrt{v_t}$ can be decomposed as: (i) gradient normalisation by $\sqrt{v_t}$ (same as RMSprop), and (ii) momentum accumulation in $m_t$ (same as Heavy Ball). When $\beta_2 = 0$ (no second-moment averaging), Adam reduces to Heavy Ball. When $\beta_1 = 0$ (no first moment), Adam reduces to RMSprop. At the typical values $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$, Adam combines both in a unified framework that is robust to sparse gradients (via $\sqrt{v_t}$ normalisation) and noisy directions (via $m_t$ averaging).

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Polyak Momentum / Heavy Ball}
\label{alg:heavyball}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, momentum $\beta$, iterations $N$
\State $z_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets \beta\, z_k + \alpha\, \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - z_{k+1}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Heavy Ball builds a velocity vector $z_k$ that accumulates past gradients. For a strongly convex quadratic with condition number $\kappa = L/\mu$, the optimal heavy-ball parameters are:
\begin{equation}
  \alpha^\star = \frac{4}{(\sqrt{L}+\sqrt{\mu})^2}, \qquad
  \beta^\star = \left(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa}+1}\right)^2,
\end{equation}
yielding the optimal first-order convergence rate $\rho^\star = \left(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa}+1}\right)^k$. For Benchmark A ($\kappa \approx 1.12$), the optimal $\beta^\star \approx 0.003$ is tiny, so momentum provides little benefit --- consistent with the observation that GD and Heavy Ball converge at similar speeds on Benchmark A. For Benchmark B ($\kappa \approx 6.9$), $\beta^\star \approx 0.42$ provides significant acceleration. The experimentally tuned values ($\beta = 0.88$--$0.90$) are higher than the optimal quadratic values, which works because the non-quadratic structure of Benchmarks B and C allows heavier momentum without instability.

\subsection{The Polyak Divergence Mechanism}

\begin{proposition}[Polyak divergence under misspecification]
Let $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}$ be $L$-smooth and let the Polyak step use $\tilde{f}^\star < f^\star_{\rm true}$. Then for any iterate $x_k$ with $f(x_k) \to f^\star_{\rm true}$ and $\|\nabla f(x_k)\| \to 0$:
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_k = \frac{f(x_k) - \tilde{f}^\star}{\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2 + \varepsilon}
           \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{f^\star_{\rm true} - \tilde{f}^\star}{\varepsilon} \;\gg\; 1,
\end{equation}
so the step size grows unboundedly (as $\varepsilon \to 0$), causing oscillations or divergence.
\end{proposition}

\textbf{Key finding:} The Polyak step size is highly sensitive to the accuracy of $f^{\star}$. On Benchmark A, $f^\star_{\rm true} \approx 0.4826$ but $\tilde{f}^\star = 0$ is specified. As $\theta \to \theta_{\rm OLS}$, the numerator $f(\theta) - 0 \approx 0.48$ remains large while $\|\nabla f\|^2 \to 0$, so $\alpha_k$ grows unboundedly --- causing oscillations that land at $f \approx 4.77$ after 120 iterations ($9.9\times$ the noise floor). On Benchmark B, the Polyak step shows oscillation but is more contained due to the bounded gradients (bounded $\sin$ term), settling at $f = 0.98$ ($35\%$ above the true minimum). On Benchmark C where $f^\star = 0$ is the true minimum, Polyak achieves $f = 0.024$ --- the best among all Q1 methods.

\subsection{Comparative Analysis}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Convergence speed:} When $f^{\star}$ is known exactly, Polyak converges fastest (Rosenbrock: $f = 0.024$). Otherwise, Heavy Ball and Adagrad match or exceed the GD baseline. Ranking on ill-conditioned Rosenbrock: Polyak ($f^\star$ known) $>$ Heavy Ball $>$ Adagrad $>$ GD $\approx$ RMSprop.
\item \textbf{Per-coordinate adaptivity:} Adagrad and RMSprop adapt independently per coordinate. The key difference: Adagrad's effective rate $\alpha_0/\sqrt{G_k}$ is monotonically decreasing (suitable for convex objectives), while RMSprop's exponential forgetting factor allows the rate to recover, making it better for non-stationary or deep learning settings.
\item \textbf{Stability:} Constant-step GD is the most predictable but restricted to $\alpha < 2/L$. Polyak is unstable when $f^\star$ is misspecified. Heavy Ball requires careful tuning of $\beta$ (its optimal value depends on $\kappa$). Adagrad is uniformly stable but may prematurely plateau.
\item \textbf{Practical guidance:} Exact $f^\star$ is almost never available in practice, limiting Polyak to research settings. RMSprop and Adam (Section~\ref{sec:q2}) dominate deep learning due to their robustness. For well-conditioned convex problems, GD or Adagrad suffice; for ill-conditioned problems, Nesterov or Adam is preferred.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Heavy Ball oscillation on non-quadratic functions.} The optimal Heavy Ball parameters $(\alpha^\star, \beta^\star)$ are derived for quadratic objectives; on non-quadratic functions (Benchmarks B and C), the constant momentum can overshoot the changing curvature landscape and create oscillations. The experimentally tuned $\beta = 0.88$--$0.90$ exceeds the theoretically optimal $\beta^\star \approx 0.003$ (Benchmark A) and $0.42$ (Benchmark B), yet works empirically because the non-quadratic Rosenbrock landscape has a different effective curvature regime at each point, and a larger $\beta$ effectively implements low-pass filtering of the gradient history, which is beneficial for navigating the narrow valley. This illustrates the practical limitation of quadratic analysis: optimal parameters for quadratics can be far from optimal for non-convex problems.

\textbf{Theoretical interpretation.} For $f$-smooth quadratics, the per-iteration contraction ratio satisfies:
\begin{equation}
  \|x_{k+1} - x^\star\|^2 \leq \max\!\left(|1 - \alpha\mu|^2,\, |1 - \alpha L|^2\right)\|x_k - x^\star\|^2.
\end{equation}
The right-hand side is minimised at $\alpha^\star = 2/(L+\mu)$, yielding contraction $\left(\frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1}\right)^2$. For $\alpha > 2/L$, the factor $|1-\alpha L| > 1$, triggering divergence. The sensitivity of this optimal $\alpha^\star$ to $\kappa$ explains why Rosenbrock requires a step size five orders of magnitude smaller than Benchmark A.

\subsection{Algorithm Formulations}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Nesterov Momentum / Accelerated Gradient}
\label{alg:nesterov}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, max momentum $\beta_{\max}$, iterations $N$
\State $z_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$k = 1, 2, \ldots, N$}
    \State $\beta_k \gets \min\!\left(\frac{k-1}{k+2},\ \beta_{\max}\right)$ \Comment{Adaptive momentum coefficient}
    \State $\tilde{x}_k \gets x_k + \beta_k\, z_k$ \Comment{Lookahead point}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets \beta_k\, z_k - \alpha\, \nabla f(\tilde{x}_k)$
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k + z_{k+1}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The critical distinction from Heavy Ball (Algorithm~\ref{alg:heavyball}) is the \emph{lookahead}: the gradient is evaluated at the extrapolated point $\tilde{x}_k = x_k + \beta_k z_k$ rather than at $x_k$. This allows Nesterov momentum to ``look ahead'' and correct its trajectory before overshooting. The adaptive schedule $\beta_k = (k-1)/(k+2)$ is the canonical choice that achieves the optimal $O(1/k^2)$ convergence rate on convex functions, compared to $O(1/k)$ for standard GD.

\textbf{Why $\beta_k = (k-1)/(k+2)$?} This sequence arises from the \emph{estimate sequence} proof technique introduced by Nesterov. Define $t_k$ via $t_1 = 1$ and $t_{k+1} = \frac{1+\sqrt{1+4t_k^2}}{2}$. Then $\beta_k = (t_k - 1)/t_{k+1}$ and $t_k \approx (k+1)/2$ for large $k$, giving $\beta_k \approx (k-1)/(k+2)$. The convergence proof maintains an auxiliary \emph{estimate sequence} $\{\phi_k\}$ such that $f(x_k) - f^\star \leq \min_x \phi_k(x)/(1/t_k^2)$; the momentum schedule ensures this sequence is simultaneously a lower bound on $f$ and converges rapidly. Intuitively, the increasing $\beta_k$ acts as a \emph{warm-up}: at iteration 1, $\beta_1 = 0$ (pure gradient step with no momentum), and by iteration 10, $\beta_{10} = 9/12 = 0.75$ (strong momentum). This prevents the instability of Heavy Ball, which uses a constant $\beta$ from the start.

\begin{theorem}[Nesterov convergence, convex case \cite{nesterov1983}]
Let $f$ be convex and $L$-smooth. Nesterov's accelerated gradient method with $\alpha = 1/L$ and $\beta_k = (k-1)/(k+2)$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}
  f(x_k) - f^\star \leq \frac{2L\|x_0 - x^\star\|^2}{(k+1)^2},
\end{equation}
versus $O(1/k)$ for gradient descent, an improvement by a factor of $O(k)$.
\end{theorem}

\textbf{Is $O(1/k^2)$ optimal?} Yes. Nesterov's lower bound (1983) shows that any first-order method using only gradients $\{\nabla f(x_0), \ldots, \nabla f(x_{k-1})\}$ satisfies $f(x_k) - f^\star \geq \frac{3L\|x_0 - x^\star\|^2}{32(k+1)^2}$ in the worst case, matching Nesterov's upper bound up to a constant factor. This means no first-order method can asymptotically outperform Nesterov acceleration on the class of smooth convex functions --- it is \emph{optimal} in an information-theoretic sense.

\textbf{Nesterov for strongly convex functions.} The $O(1/k^2)$ result applies to convex (not strongly convex) functions. When $f$ is $\mu$-strongly convex, the optimal first-order rate is $O(\rho^k)$ with $\rho = (\sqrt{\kappa}-1)/(\sqrt{\kappa}+1)$, achieved by Nesterov acceleration with a \emph{fixed} momentum $\beta = (\sqrt{\kappa}-1)/(\sqrt{\kappa}+1)$ (the same formula as Heavy Ball's optimal $\beta^\star$). The practical implementation clips $\beta_k \leq \beta_{\max}$, which empirically interpolates between the two regimes.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Adam Optimiser}
\label{alg:adam}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, learning rate $\alpha$, moments $\beta_1, \beta_2$, tolerance $\varepsilon$, iterations $N$
\State $m_0 \gets \mathbf{0},\ v_0 \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$t = 1, 2, \ldots, N$}
    \State $g_t \gets \nabla f(x_t)$
    \State $m_t \gets \beta_1\, m_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_1)\, g_t$ \Comment{First-moment (mean) estimate}
    \State $v_t \gets \beta_2\, v_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_2)\, g_t \odot g_t$ \Comment{Second-moment (variance) estimate}
    \State $\hat{m}_t \gets m_t\, /\, (1 - \beta_1^t)$ \Comment{Bias correction for first moment}
    \State $\hat{v}_t \gets v_t\, /\, (1 - \beta_2^t)$ \Comment{Bias correction for second moment}
    \State $x_{t+1} \gets x_t - \alpha\, \hat{m}_t\, /\, (\sqrt{\hat{v}_t} + \varepsilon)$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Adam \cite{kingma2015} combines RMSprop's \cite{hinton2012} per-coordinate adaptive scaling (via $v_t$) with momentum (via $m_t$), inheriting advantages of both. The bias corrections prevent early iterates from being biased towards zero (since $m_0 = v_0 = 0$, without correction $m_1 = (1-\beta_1)g_1$ is far from the true first moment). The effective update is $\alpha \cdot \hat{m}_t/\sqrt{\hat{v}_t} \approx \alpha \cdot \text{sign}(g_t)$ elementwise at the start, providing normalised steps.

\subsection{Mini-Batch Stochastic Gradient Descent}

Mini-batch SGD on Benchmark A uses learning rate $\alpha = 0.06$, batch sizes $b \in \{5, 40\}$, and 50 epochs, with data shuffled at every epoch.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Mini-Batch SGD}
\label{alg:sgd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Data $(X, y)$, initial $\theta_0$, learning rate $\alpha$, batch size $b$, epochs $E$
\For{$e = 1, 2, \ldots, E$}
    \State Randomly permute data indices
    \For{each mini-batch $\mathcal{B}$ of size $b$}
        \State $g \gets \frac{1}{b} X_{\mathcal{B}}^T(X_{\mathcal{B}}\theta - y_{\mathcal{B}})$ \Comment{Stochastic gradient estimate}
        \State $\theta \gets \theta - \alpha\, g$
    \EndFor
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Each mini-batch gradient $\hat{g}_b$ is an unbiased estimator of the full gradient: $\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_b] = \nabla J(\theta)$, but with variance:
\begin{equation}
  \text{Var}[\hat{g}_b] = \frac{1}{b}\text{Var}\!\left[\nabla_\theta \ell(\theta; x^{(i)}, y^{(i)})\right] \approx \frac{\sigma^2_{\rm grad}}{b}.
\end{equation}
Smaller batches reduce computation per epoch but increase gradient variance, leading to a \emph{noise floor}: SGD with constant step $\alpha$ converges to a neighbourhood of $\theta^\star$ of radius $O(\alpha\sigma^2_{\rm grad}/b)$, not to the exact minimum. To achieve exact convergence, the learning rate must decay: the standard schedule $\alpha_k = \alpha_0/(1 + \gamma k)$ satisfies $\sum_k \alpha_k = \infty$ (ensures escape from any basin) and $\sum_k \alpha_k^2 < \infty$ (ensures the noise term vanishes), achieving $\mathbb{E}[f(\theta_k) - f^\star] = O(1/k)$.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/q2_sgd_batch.pdf}
\caption{Q2: Mini-batch SGD with different batch sizes. With $b = 5$, each epoch performs $m/5 = 200$ parameter updates, providing faster per-epoch convergence but higher gradient variance. With $b = 40$, each epoch has $m/40 = 25$ updates, resulting in smoother but slower per-epoch progress. The smaller batch reaches lower loss faster in early epochs due to more frequent updates, but exhibits larger oscillations near the minimum because each mini-batch gradient deviates more from the true gradient.}

On Rosenbrock, the Hessian is highly non-diagonal (the off-diagonal term $H_{12} = -400x_1 \approx -400$ at the optimum), so a diagonal preconditioner cannot capture the dominant curvature direction. Nesterov, by contrast, accumulates momentum in the correlated direction along the valley floor, which is precisely the direction that needs acceleration.

\subsection{Variance Reduction: Beyond Standard SGD}

Standard mini-batch SGD with constant step size cannot converge to the exact minimum; the gradient variance $\sigma^2_{\rm grad}/b$ creates an irreducible noise floor even as $k \to \infty$. Two landmark variance-reduction methods achieve exact convergence with linear rates, matching full-batch gradient descent in asymptotic rate while retaining SGD's per-iteration cost:

\textbf{SAG (Stochastic Average Gradient) \cite{roux2012}.} Stores the most recent gradient for each data point $i$. At iteration $k$, updates one randomly chosen gradient $g_i^{(k)} \gets \nabla \ell_i(x_k)$ and updates using the average:
\begin{equation}
  x_{k+1} = x_k - \frac{\alpha}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n g_i^{(k)}.
\end{equation}
SAG achieves a linear convergence rate $O(\rho^k)$ with $\rho = 1 - \min(1/(8n), \mu/(4L)) < 1$ for $\mu$-strongly convex, $L$-smooth objectives. The memory cost is $O(nd)$ (one gradient vector per data point).

\textbf{SVRG (Stochastic Variance Reduced Gradient) \cite{johnson2013}.} Avoids the memory cost by periodically computing a full-batch gradient snapshot $\tilde{\mu} = \nabla f(\tilde{x})$ at a reference point $\tilde{x}$. The variance-reduced stochastic gradient is:
\begin{equation}
  v_k = \nabla \ell_{i_k}(x_k) - \nabla \ell_{i_k}(\tilde{x}) + \tilde{\mu}.
\end{equation}
This is an unbiased estimate of $\nabla f(x_k)$ with variance $O(\|x_k - \tilde{x}\|^2)$ that vanishes as $x_k \to \tilde{x}$. SVRG achieves the same linear rate as SAG with only $O(d)$ memory, at the cost of one full pass every $m$ steps to recompute $\tilde{\mu}$. The optimal inner loop length is $m = O(n)$, giving an effective per-iteration cost of $2 \times$ standard SGD.

For Benchmark A (linear regression, $n = 1000$), SVRG would achieve the exact minimum $J^\star = 0.4826$ rather than fluctuating around it; the noise floor seen in Figure~\ref{fig:q2_sgd_batch} would disappear entirely with SVRG.

\begin{remark}[Adam vs SVRG]
Adam is popular in deep learning despite its non-convergence guarantees (\cite{reddi2018} showed Adam can diverge without amsgrad correction) because the adaptive scaling is beneficial for non-convex, non-smooth objectives where the gradient magnitudes vary dramatically across layers. For convex objectives like Benchmark A, SVRG is theoretically superior.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Local Approximations of \texorpdfstring{$g(x) = x^4$}{g(x) = x\^{}4}}

\textbf{Taylor expansions} around $x_0 = 0.25$ with $g'(x) = 4x^3$ and $g''(x) = 12x^2$:

\textbf{First-order (tangent line):}
\begin{equation}
  T_1(x) = g(x_0) + g'(x_0)(x - x_0) = 0.0039 + 0.0625(x - 0.25).
\end{equation}

\textbf{Second-order (local parabola):}
\begin{equation}
  T_2(x) = g(x_0) + g'(x_0)(x - x_0) + \tfrac{1}{2}g''(x_0)(x - x_0)^2 = 0.0039 + 0.0625(x - 0.25) + 0.375(x - 0.25)^2.
\end{equation}

The \textbf{Newton step} minimises $T_2$: setting $T_2'(x) = 0$ gives:
\begin{equation}
  x^\star_{T_2} = x_0 - \frac{g'(x_0)}{g''(x_0)} = 0.25 - \frac{0.0625}{0.75} \approx 0.167,
\end{equation}
which is a better estimate of the true minimiser $x^\star = 0$ than the gradient step $x_0 - \alpha g'(x_0)$ with any fixed $\alpha$. For $g(x) = x^4$, Newton's method has superlinear convergence: starting from $x_0 = 0.25$, the sequence is $0.25 \to 0.167 \to 0.083 \to 0.028 \to \cdots$.

\textbf{Error analysis for $g(x) = x^4$.} The true minimiser of $T_2$ is $x_N = x_0/3$ (since $g''(x_0) = 12x_0^2$ and $g'(x_0) = 4x_0^3$, so $x_N = x_0 - g'/g'' = x_0 - x_0/3 = 2x_0/3$). Thus each Newton step reduces $x_k$ by a factor of $2/3$, giving linear convergence $x_k = (2/3)^k x_0$. This is \emph{linear} rather than quadratic because $g(x) = x^4$ has a degenerate minimiser at $x=0$ where $g''(0) = 0$ (the Hessian vanishes at the minimum), violating the strong convexity assumption $\mu > 0$ required for quadratic convergence. In general, Newton's method achieves quadratic convergence only when the Hessian is positive definite at the minimiser.

\textbf{Third-order approximation.} A third-order Taylor expansion $T_3(x) = T_2(x) + \frac{1}{6}g'''(x_0)(x-x_0)^3$ with $g'''(x) = 24x$ gives $T_3(x) = 0.0039 + 0.0625(x-0.25) + 0.375(x-0.25)^2 + (24\times 0.25/6)(x-0.25)^3$. The cubic term better captures the asymmetry of $x^4$ near $x_0 = 0.25$ (the function increases more steeply to the right than to the left). Minimising $T_3$ gives $x_{T_3} \approx 0.113$, closer to the true minimum than the Newton step $x_N = 0.167$, at the cost of solving a cubic equation per iteration.

\subsection{Newton's Method with Hessian Damping}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Newton's Method with Hessian Regularisation}
\label{alg:newton}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, damping $\lambda$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $g_k \gets \nabla f(x_k)$
    \State $H_k \gets \nabla^2 f(x_k) + \lambda I$ \Comment{Tikhonov-regularised Hessian}
    \State $p_k \gets H_k^{-1}\, g_k$ \Comment{Newton direction}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, p_k$ \Comment{Damped Newton step}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The Hessian damping $\lambda I$ (Tikhonov regularisation) serves two purposes: (i) it ensures $H_k + \lambda I \succ 0$ even when $H_k$ is singular or indefinite (as near saddle points), and (ii) it controls the step size: the damped direction $p_k = (H_k + \lambda I)^{-1}g_k$ interpolates between the Newton direction ($\lambda \to 0$) and the gradient direction ($\lambda \to \infty$). With $\lambda = 10^{-8}$, regularisation has negligible effect unless $H_k$ is near-singular.

\textbf{Settings:} GD uses 80 iterations; Newton uses only 20 iterations with $\lambda = 10^{-8}$.

\subsection{Theoretical Convergence Rates}

\begin{theorem}[Quadratic convergence of Newton's method]
Let $f$ be twice continuously differentiable with $L$-Lipschitz Hessian and $\mu$-strongly-convex near $x^\star$. Then Newton's method with $\alpha = 1$ satisfies, for $\|x_k - x^\star\|$ sufficiently small:
\begin{equation}
  \|x_{k+1} - x^\star\| \leq \frac{L}{2\mu}\|x_k - x^\star\|^2.
\end{equation}
This \emph{quadratic convergence} means each iteration approximately doubles the number of correct decimal digits, compared to the \emph{linear convergence} $\|x_{k+1} - x^\star\| \leq \frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1}\|x_k - x^\star\|$ of gradient descent.
\end{theorem}

\textbf{Proof sketch.} The Newton step $x_{k+1} = x_k - [H(x_k)]^{-1}\nabla f(x_k)$ satisfies:
\begin{align}
  x_{k+1} - x^\star &= x_k - x^\star - [H(x_k)]^{-1}\nabla f(x_k) \nonumber\\
  &= [H(x_k)]^{-1}\bigl(H(x_k)(x_k - x^\star) - \nabla f(x_k)\bigr).
\end{align}
By the mean-value theorem, $\nabla f(x_k) = H(x^\star)(x_k - x^\star) + \int_0^1 [H(x^\star + t(x_k-x^\star)) - H(x^\star)](x_k-x^\star)\, dt$. The integral term is bounded by $\frac{L}{2}\|x_k - x^\star\|^2$ (Lipschitz Hessian). Combined with $\|[H(x_k)]^{-1}\| \leq 1/\mu$ (strong convexity), this yields the quadratic bound. $\square$

\textbf{Practical implication:} For Benchmark C (Rosenbrock), the constant $L/(2\mu) \approx 1002/(2 \times 0.4) \approx 1253$. This means the local convergence phase only starts when $\|x_k - x^\star\| \lesssim \mu/L = 0.0004$ --- near the true minimum $(1,1)$. The 20-iteration budget is too short for full local convergence, so the damped Newton step ($\alpha = 0.22$) is essential to navigate the highly curved valley before entering the local convergence regime.

\textbf{Three phases of Newton convergence.} Practical Newton methods exhibit three distinct phases:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Global (damping) phase:} Far from $x^\star$, the Hessian may be indefinite or poorly conditioned, and full Newton steps would diverge. The damped step $\alpha < 1$ ensures descent: $f(x_{k+1}) < f(x_k)$ by Armijo-type line search. In this phase, convergence is roughly linear.
\item \textbf{Local quadratic phase:} Once $\|x_k - x^\star\|$ is small enough (below the threshold $2\mu^2/L\|\nabla^2 f\|$), the quadratic bound kicks in and convergence becomes super-linear. In practice this transition is visible as a sudden acceleration in the objective curve.
\item \textbf{High-precision phase:} Once $\|x_k - x^\star\| \approx \varepsilon_{\rm mach}^{1/2}$, floating-point errors in the Hessian solve $H^{-1}g$ prevent further progress. This numerical floor is approximately $\varepsilon_{\rm mach} \cdot \kappa(H)$.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Trust-region vs line-search Newton.} The damped Newton method implemented here uses a fixed step size $\alpha = 0.22$. An alternative is the \emph{trust-region Newton method}, which constrains the step to a ball $\|p_k\| \leq \Delta_k$ and adjusts the radius $\Delta_k$ based on the quality of the quadratic model. Trust-region methods are globally convergent (not just locally) and avoid the need to tune $\alpha$, at the cost of solving a constrained quadratic subproblem per iteration. For the Rosenbrock function, a trust-region method would adaptively increase $\Delta_k$ once the method reaches the local convergence regime, achieving faster final convergence than the fixed-$\alpha$ approach used here.

The observed update magnitude plot (Figure~\ref{fig:q3_update}) confirms this: Newton's update magnitude decreases super-linearly (approximately parabolic on the log scale), while GD's decreases linearly.

\subsection{Part A: Finite Difference Approximation and Nesterov Random Search (Benchmark B)}

\subsubsection{Finite Difference Gradient Approximation}

The forward finite-difference (FD) approximation replaces each partial derivative with:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x) \approx \frac{f(x + \delta e_i) - f(x)}{\delta} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x) + \frac{\delta}{2}\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i^2}(x) + O(\delta^2),
\end{equation}
where $e_i$ is the $i$-th standard basis vector. The approximation error has two components:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Truncation error:} $O(\delta)$ from Taylor expansion --- larger $\delta$ means larger gradient error.
\item \textbf{Round-off error:} $O(\varepsilon_{\rm machine}/\delta)$ from floating-point cancellation --- smaller $\delta$ amplifies this.
\end{itemize}
The optimal $\delta$ minimises the total error: $\delta^\star \sim \sqrt{\varepsilon_{\rm machine}} \approx 10^{-8}$ for IEEE double precision. The assignment tests $\delta = 0.05$ (near-optimal for this problem) and $\delta = 0.8$ (large truncation error). Each FD gradient evaluation requires $n+1 = 3$ function evaluations (one for $f(x)$, one per coordinate).

\subsubsection{Nesterov Random Search}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Nesterov Random Search (Zeroth-Order Method)}
\label{alg:nrs}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, perturbation $\delta$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State Sample $u_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_n)$, normalise: $u_k \gets u_k / \|u_k\|$
    \State $\hat{g}_k \gets \dfrac{f(x_k + \delta u_k) - f(x_k)}{\delta}\, u_k$ \Comment{Stochastic directional-derivative estimate}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \hat{g}_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

This zeroth-order method estimates the directional derivative along a single random direction $u_k$, requiring only \emph{two} function evaluations per iteration regardless of dimension $n$.

\textbf{Gradient estimator analysis.} The estimator is:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{g}_k = \frac{f(x_k + \delta u_k) - f(x_k)}{\delta}\, u_k.
\end{equation}
For any unit vector $u$ and $L$-smooth $f$:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{f(x+\delta u)-f(x)}{\delta} = \nabla f(x)^T u + \frac{\delta}{2} u^T \nabla^2 f(\xi) u + O(\delta^2)
\end{equation}
Taking expectation over $u \sim \text{Uniform}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})$ (unit sphere):
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}_u[\hat{g}_k] = \mathbb{E}[(\nabla f(x)^T u) u] = \frac{1}{n}\nabla f(x)
\end{equation}
since $\mathbb{E}[u u^T] = \frac{1}{n}I$ for uniform random unit vectors. The estimator $\hat{g}_k$ is therefore a \emph{scaled} unbiased estimator of $\nabla f$; to get an unbiased estimator of $\nabla f$, one would use $n \hat{g}_k$. However, scaling by $n$ also scales the variance: $\text{Var}[n\hat{g}_k] = n^2 \text{Var}[\hat{g}_k] = O(n \|\nabla f\|^2)$ --- this \emph{dimension-dependent variance} is the fundamental limitation of random search in high dimensions. Despite this, using only $2$ function evaluations per step (vs.\ $d+1$ for FD), random search is competitive for very high-dimensional problems where even computing FD is expensive.

For our $n=2$ benchmarks, the variance penalty is small; the dominant limitation is the noisy gradient direction rather than the step size choice.

\subsubsection{Results and Analysis}

At the starting point $x_0 = [-1, 4]^T$, the exact gradient is $\nabla f(x_0) \approx [-3.46,\ 20.0]^T$ with $\|\nabla f(x_0)\| \approx 20.3$. The finite-difference gradient errors are:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Good FD ($\delta = 0.05$): approximate gradient $\approx [-3.39,\ 20.25]^T$, absolute error $= 0.26$ (relative $1.3\%$).
\item Poor FD ($\delta = 0.8$): approximate gradient $\approx [-2.40,\ 24.0]^T$, absolute error $= 4.14$ (relative $20.4\%$).
\end{itemize}
The dominant error for $\delta = 0.8$ is in the $x_1$ direction, where the nonlinear term $\cos(x_1)$ changes by $\cos(-1) - \cos(-0.2) \approx 0.54 - 0.98 = -0.44$ over the interval $[-1, -0.2]$, producing a finite-difference error of order $\delta \cdot |g''(x_0)| \approx 0.8 \times 5.9 \approx 4.7$, consistent with the observed error.

Figure~\ref{fig:fd_error_analysis} shows the FD gradient error as a function of $\delta$ (left) and the resulting convergence on Benchmark B (right). The optimal $\delta$ for forward FD balances truncation error $O(\delta)$ against round-off error $O(\varepsilon_{\rm mach}/\delta)$, giving $\delta^\star \approx \sqrt{\varepsilon_{\rm mach}} \approx 10^{-8}$. Central FD achieves $O(\delta^2)$ truncation error with the same round-off floor, placing its optimal at $\delta^\star \approx \varepsilon_{\rm mach}^{1/3} \approx 10^{-5}$.

\subsection{Part B: Derivative-Free Methods on Rosenbrock (Benchmark C)}

\subsubsection{Nelder--Mead Simplex Method}

The Nelder--Mead algorithm maintains a simplex of $n+1 = 3$ vertices in $\mathbb{R}^2$ and iteratively transforms it through four operations. Let $x_1, x_2, x_3$ be the simplex vertices ordered $f(x_1) \leq f(x_2) \leq f(x_3)$ and $c = (x_1 + x_2)/2$ their centroid (excluding the worst):
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,label=\arabic*.]
\item \textbf{Reflection:} $x_r = c + \alpha_r(c - x_3)$, $\alpha_r = 1$. Accept if $f(x_1) \leq f(x_r) < f(x_2)$.
\item \textbf{Expansion:} If $f(x_r) < f(x_1)$, try $x_e = c + \gamma(c - x_3)$, $\gamma = 2$.
\item \textbf{Contraction:} If $f(x_r) \geq f(x_2)$, try $x_c = c + \rho(x_3 - c)$, $\rho = 0.5$.
\item \textbf{Shrink:} If contraction fails, set $x_i \leftarrow x_1 + \sigma(x_i - x_1)$, $\sigma = 0.5$, for all $i > 1$.
\end{enumerate}
No gradient information is required. The Nelder--Mead method \cite{nelder1965} does not have a convergence guarantee for dimensions $d \geq 2$ (it can stagnate on non-convex problems), but works well in practice for smooth functions in low dimensions. Settings: initial simplex step $= 0.35$, 160 iterations.

\begin{remark}[Nelder-Mead on ill-conditioned problems]
On the Rosenbrock function, the simplex must align itself with the narrow banana-shaped valley. Initially the simplex is too large and straddles the valley walls; after several shrink operations it contracts to a small, valley-aligned shape. This alignment phase dominates the early iterations, explaining why the best-so-far curve is flat initially before showing rapid improvement.
\end{remark}

\textbf{Key trade-off:} Grid search is the only method that provides a \emph{global} guarantee (up to grid resolution) but at exponential cost in $d$. Gradient-based methods scale well with dimension but cannot escape local minima. Nelder--Mead is versatile for low-dimensional ($d \leq 10$) non-smooth problems. Nesterov random search is the only truly dimension-free method (2 FE/iter), making it suitable for extremely high-dimensional black-box optimisation.

\textbf{Dimension curse for grid search.} For Benchmark B ($d = 2$), a $55 \times 55$ grid gives 3025 evaluations and resolution $\Delta x \approx 0.074$, which is affordable. In $d$ dimensions with $N$ points per axis, the total evaluation count is $N^d$: for $d = 100$ (a typical neural network layer), even $N = 2$ would require $2^{100} \approx 10^{30}$ evaluations --- beyond any computational budget. This exponential scaling is known as the \emph{curse of dimensionality} and motivates the entire field of continuous optimisation.

\textbf{Finite differences vs.\ automatic differentiation.} In modern deep learning, gradients are computed by \emph{reverse-mode automatic differentiation} (backpropagation), which computes the exact gradient in a single forward-backward pass at cost $O(d)$ --- independent of the number of parameters. This is strictly superior to forward finite differences ($O(d)$ passes) for large $d$, which is why backpropagation displaced FD gradient estimation in neural network training by the mid-1980s.

\subsection{Problem Setup}

Minimise Benchmark B (Equation~\eqref{eq:benchmark_B}) subject to the half-space constraint $x_1 \geq 0.5$, starting from the infeasible point $x_0 = (0.2, 4.0)$.

The constrained optimum is at $x^\star_c = (0.582, 2.0)$ with $f(x^\star_c) \approx 0.7244$, which happens to coincide with the unconstrained optimum since $x^\star_{unc} = (0.582, 2.0)$ satisfies $x_1^\star > 0.5$ --- the constraint is \emph{inactive} at the true minimum. The difficulty here lies in the infeasible starting point and the convergence path through the infeasible region.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Projected Gradient Descent (PGD)}
\label{alg:pgd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, projection $\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \nabla f(x_k)$ \Comment{Unconstrained gradient step}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets \Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(z_{k+1})$ \Comment{Project back: $(x_1, x_2) \mapsto (\max(0.5, x_1), x_2)$}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

For the half-space $\mathcal{X} = \{x : x_1 \geq 0.5\}$, the projection is $\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(z)_1 = \max(0.5, z_1)$, $\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(z)_2 = z_2$ --- a simple clipping operation.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Penalty Method}
\label{alg:penalty}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0$, step size $\alpha$, penalty weight $\lambda$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $F_{\lambda}(x) \gets f(x) + \lambda \max(0,\, 0.5 - x_1)$ \Comment{Penalised objective}
    \State $\nabla F_\lambda(x) \gets \nabla f(x) + \lambda \cdot (-e_1) \cdot \mathbf{1}[x_1 < 0.5]$ \Comment{Subgradient of penalty}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets x_k - \alpha\, \nabla F_{\lambda}(x_k)$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\textbf{Settings:} PGD ($\alpha = 0.08$); penalty with $\lambda \in \{0.15, 1.8, 4.5\}$ (step sizes $\alpha = 0.05$ for $\lambda \leq 1.8$; $\alpha = 0.03$ for $\lambda = 4.5$); unconstrained GD ($\alpha = 0.07$). All run for 100 iterations.

\subsection{Results}

\begin{figure}[H]

\subsection{Comparative Discussion}

\textbf{Projected Gradient Descent} enforces feasibility as a hard constraint at every iteration via the projection step. The fundamental advantage is \emph{guaranteed feasibility}: $x_k \in \mathcal{X}$ for all $k \geq 1$, even from an infeasible starting point. For the half-space $x_1 \geq 0.5$, projection reduces to a $\max$ operation with cost $O(1)$. For complex convex sets (e.g.\ the spectral norm ball, the Birkhoff polytope), projection can be expensive or require iterative procedures.

\begin{theorem}[Projected GD convergence]
Let $f$ be $\mu$-strongly convex and $L$-smooth, and $\mathcal{X}$ be a closed convex set. Projected GD with step size $\alpha \in (0, 2/L)$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}
  \|x_{k+1} - x^\star\|^2 \leq (1 - 2\mu\alpha + \mu\alpha L)\|x_k - x^\star\|^2 \cdot \|x_k - x^\star\|^2 \leq \rho^k \|x_0 - x^\star\|^2,
\end{equation}
with $\rho = 1 - \mu\alpha(2 - \alpha L) < 1$ for $\alpha \leq 1/L$. The optimal step size $\alpha^\star = 1/L$ gives $\rho^\star = 1 - \mu/L = 1 - 1/\kappa$, same as unconstrained GD.
\end{theorem}

\textbf{Key observation:} Projection does not worsen the convergence rate relative to unconstrained GD. The \emph{non-expansiveness} of projection ($\|\Pi_\mathcal{X}(u) - \Pi_\mathcal{X}(v)\| \leq \|u-v\|$) ensures the projection step cannot increase the distance to $x^\star$ (since $x^\star \in \mathcal{X}$). This is why PGD converges at the same rate as unconstrained GD despite the additional constraint.

\textbf{Penalty methods} are simpler to implement (no projection oracle needed) but enforce feasibility only approximately. The penalised objective is $F_\lambda(x) = f(x) + \lambda c(x)$ where $c(x) = \max(0, 0.5 - x_1)$. As $\lambda \to \infty$, the minimiser of $F_\lambda$ converges to the constrained minimiser of $f$, but the Lipschitz constant of $\nabla F_\lambda$ grows with $\lambda$, requiring smaller step sizes for stability (here, $\alpha = 0.03$ for $\lambda = 4.5$ vs.\ $\alpha = 0.05$ for $\lambda = 1.8$).

\textbf{Augmented Lagrangian} (not implemented here) improves upon the basic penalty method by adding a dual variable $\mu$ updated via $\mu_{k+1} = \mu_k + \lambda c(x_k)$, enabling convergence to exact feasibility at finite $\lambda$ without the ill-conditioning of large penalty coefficients.

\textbf{KKT conditions:} At the constrained minimum $x^\star_c$, the KKT conditions require $\nabla f(x^\star_c) + \mu^\star \nabla c(x^\star_c) = 0$ with $\mu^\star \geq 0$ (dual variable) and $\mu^\star c(x^\star_c) = 0$ (complementary slackness). Since the constraint is inactive ($x_1^\star = 0.582 > 0.5$), complementary slackness gives $\mu^\star = 0$, and the unconstrained stationarity condition $\nabla f(x^\star_c) = 0$ holds.

\textbf{Penalty method conditioning analysis.} The condition number of the penalised Hessian $\nabla^2 F_\lambda$ grows with $\lambda$. For the quadratic penalty $\frac{\lambda}{2}\max(0, 0.5-x_1)^2$, when $x_1 < 0.5$ the penalised Hessian in the $x_1$ direction is $H_{11}^{\rm eff} = H_{11}^f + \lambda$. The condition number of $\nabla^2 F_\lambda$ at the constrained minimum is approximately:
\begin{equation}
  \kappa(F_\lambda) \approx \frac{\max(\lambda_{\max}^f, \lambda)}{{\lambda_{\min}^f}} = \frac{\max(10, \lambda)}{1.45}.
\end{equation}
For $\lambda = 4.5$: $\kappa \approx 10/1.45 \approx 6.9$ (unchanged from unconstrained). For $\lambda = 100$: $\kappa \approx 100/1.45 \approx 69$ --- the problem becomes nearly $10\times$ harder. This quantifies the penalty method's ill-conditioning trade-off: tighter constraint enforcement ($\lambda \uparrow$) comes at the cost of harder optimisation.

\textbf{When to use which method:}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Projected GD}: use when projection is cheap ($O(1)$--$O(d\log d)$): box, simplex, Euclidean ball constraints.
\item \textbf{Penalty method}: use when no projection oracle exists and constraint violation is tolerable during optimisation; increase $\lambda$ gradually.
\item \textbf{Augmented Lagrangian (ADMM)}: use when both exact feasibility and moderate $\kappa$ are required; the workhorse of practical constrained solvers (IPOPT, OSQP, SCS).
\item \textbf{Interior-point methods}: use for problems where the constraint set has a known self-concordant barrier (conic LP, SDP, second-order cone programs). Not studied in this assignment.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Feasible Set and Linear Programme}

The feasible set is the axis-aligned box: $\mathcal{X} = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^2 : 0.5 \leq x_1 \leq 5,\ -5 \leq x_2 \leq 10\}$.

\textbf{Linear Programme:} Minimise $f(x) = a^Tx = x_1 + 2x_2$ (with $a = [1, 2]^T$) over $\mathcal{X}$.

Since both components of $a$ are positive, the gradient $\nabla f = a$ points in the direction of increasing $f$, so the minimum is at the lower bounds in each coordinate:
\begin{equation}

\subsection{Frank--Wolfe Algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Frank--Wolfe (Conditional Gradient)}
\label{alg:fw}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial point $x_0 \in \mathcal{X}$, mixing parameter $\beta$, iterations $N$
\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$}
    \State $z_k \gets \arg\min_{z \in \mathcal{X}}\, \nabla f(x_k)^T z$ \Comment{Linear minimisation oracle (LMO)}
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets \beta\, x_k + (1 - \beta)\, z_k$ \Comment{Convex combination, $x_{k+1} \in \mathcal{X}$}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

For box constraints, the LP subproblem decomposes per coordinate in $O(d)$:
\begin{equation}
  (z_k)_i = \begin{cases} l_i & \text{if } (\nabla f(x_k))_i > 0 \\ u_i & \text{if } (\nabla f(x_k))_i \leq 0 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $[l_i, u_i]$ are the box bounds. The FW iterate is always a convex combination of feasible points (since $x_0 \in \mathcal{X}$ and $z_k \in \mathcal{X}$), so \textbf{no projection is needed}: $x_{k+1} \in \mathcal{X}$ by construction.

\begin{theorem}[Frank--Wolfe convergence rate \cite{jaggi2013}]
Let $f$ be convex and $L$-smooth over $\mathcal{X}$ with diameter $D = \max_{x,y \in \mathcal{X}}\|x-y\|$. Frank--Wolfe with step size $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}
  f(x_k) - f^\star \leq \frac{2LD^2}{k+2} = O(1/k).
\end{equation}
This $O(1/k)$ rate is \emph{slower} than projected GD's $O(\rho^k)$ linear rate, but requires only a linear minimisation oracle rather than a projection oracle.
\end{theorem}

\textbf{Note on the fixed-$\beta$ schedule:} This implementation uses a fixed $\beta$ (equivalently, a fixed step size $\gamma = 1 - \beta$ towards $z_k$) rather than the standard $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$ schedule. With fixed $\gamma$, the iterates satisfy $x_{k+1} = (1-\gamma)^{k+1}x_0 + \gamma\sum_{j=0}^{k}(1-\gamma)^{k-j}z_j$, a geometric moving average of the LP solutions. Figure~\ref{fig:q6_beta_comparison} shows that the choice of $\beta$ has a non-trivial effect: small $\beta$ (large step towards $z_k$) converges faster initially but oscillates more near the optimum, while large $\beta$ (small steps) converges more smoothly to a better final value.

\subsection{Interior vs.\ Boundary Behaviour}

\textbf{Interior optimum:} Frank--Wolfe exhibits the characteristic oscillatory ``zig-zag'' pattern. Each LMO selects a vertex of $\mathcal{X}$ determined by the gradient direction, which changes as $x_k$ moves. The convex combination gradually averages these LP solutions, but the oscillating $z_k$ introduces noise. The theoretical $O(1/k)$ rate (with decaying $\gamma_k$) is demonstrated qualitatively. Smaller $\beta$ (larger steps towards $z_k$) is faster initially but creates more oscillation near the optimum.

\textbf{Boundary optimum:} Frank--Wolfe is naturally suited to boundary optima because $z_k$ consistently selects the vertex closest to the constrained optimum, pulling the iterate towards the boundary. The method converges without explicit projection. However, the fixed $\beta$ schedule (as opposed to the optimal $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$) limits the asymptotic convergence rate: with $\gamma_k = 2/(k+2)$, the $O(1/k)$ rate would be achieved, and the boundary case could more efficiently approach $f^\star = 0.25$.

\textbf{Frank--Wolfe gap as stopping criterion.} At each iteration, the \emph{Frank--Wolfe gap} is defined as:
\begin{equation}
  g_k = \nabla f(x_k)^T(x_k - z_k) = \nabla f(x_k)^T x_k - \min_{z \in \mathcal{X}} \nabla f(x_k)^T z \geq 0.
\end{equation}
The gap satisfies $g_k \geq f(x_k) - f^\star$ (a certified lower bound on the suboptimality), making it a reliable stopping criterion: when $g_k \leq \varepsilon$, we are guaranteed $f(x_k) - f^\star \leq \varepsilon$. This is a unique feature of the FW algorithm with no analogue in projected GD.

\textbf{Proof that $g_k \geq f(x_k) - f^\star$.} By convexity of $f$:
\begin{equation}
  f(x^\star) \geq f(x_k) + \nabla f(x_k)^T(x^\star - x_k) \implies f(x_k) - f^\star \leq \nabla f(x_k)^T(x_k - x^\star).
\end{equation}
Since $x^\star \in \mathcal{X}$, the LP solution $z_k = \arg\min_{z \in \mathcal{X}}\nabla f(x_k)^T z$ satisfies $\nabla f(x_k)^T z_k \leq \nabla f(x_k)^T x^\star$, so $\nabla f(x_k)^T(x_k - x^\star) \leq \nabla f(x_k)^T(x_k - z_k) = g_k$. $\square$

Figure~\ref{fig:fw_gap} illustrates this: the gap $g_k$ (solid) consistently upper-bounds the suboptimality $f(x_k) - f^\star$ (dotted) at every iteration, providing a certified progress certificate.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fw_gap_analysis.pdf}
\caption{Q6: Frank--Wolfe certified gap analysis. Left: suboptimality $f(x_k) - f^\star$ for both $\beta$ values. Right: FW gap $g_k$ (solid) vs.\ suboptimality (dotted). The gap consistently upper-bounds the suboptimality at every iteration, confirming the theoretical guarantee $g_k \geq f(x_k) - f^\star$. This certificate is available ``for free'' at each FW iteration (the LMO already computes $z_k$), making it a practical stopping rule: terminate when $g_k < \varepsilon$.}
\label{fig:fw_gap}
\end{figure}

\textbf{FW vs.\ Projected GD:} The key advantage of FW is the \emph{linear oracle}: for complex sets (nuclear norm ball, flow polytope, spectrahedra), a linear minimisation is often tractable while projection is not. The disadvantage is the slower $O(1/k)$ convergence rate vs.\ projected GD's linear $O(\rho^k)$ rate. For the simple box constraint studied here, projected GD would converge significantly faster; however, for constrained matrix problems (e.g.\ low-rank factorisation), Frank--Wolfe is often the only practical choice.

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]

\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Constrained Methods (Question 5)}

Projected GD is unequivocally superior when a cheap projection oracle exists: it guarantees $x_k \in \mathcal{X}$ from step 1 and converges at the same asymptotic rate as unconstrained GD. The penalty method's main advantage is simplicity (no projection oracle required). However, the penalty approach has two practical limitations: (i) large $\lambda$ introduces ill-conditioning that forces smaller step sizes; (ii) for inequality constraints with multiple active boundaries, the penalty gradient is non-smooth at the boundary.

The \textbf{augmented Lagrangian} method overcomes both issues by updating a dual variable $\mu_k$:
\begin{equation}
  L_\lambda(x, \mu) = f(x) + \mu \max(0, 0.5 - x_1) + \frac{\lambda}{2}\max(0, 0.5-x_1)^2, \quad
  \mu_{k+1} = \mu_k + \lambda \max(0, 0.5 - x_1^{(k)}),
\end{equation}
which achieves exact feasibility at \emph{finite} $\lambda$ without the ill-conditioning of large pure-penalty approaches. This is the basis of modern constrained solvers (IPOPT, SNOPT).

\subsection{Frank--Wolfe (Question 6)}

Frank--Wolfe offers a principled approach to constrained optimisation for structured constraint sets. Its $O(1/k)$ convergence rate is weaker than projected GD's linear $O(\rho^k)$ rate, but has two key advantages: (i) the \textbf{linear oracle} (LMO) is often tractable where projection is not --- for the nuclear norm ball, the LMO is a rank-1 SVD while projection is a full SVD; (ii) the algorithm is \textbf{projection-free} and produces sparse iterates as convex combinations of atoms, which is valuable for matrix factorisation and optimal transport.

The key limitation observed in the experiments is the $O(1/k)$ rate causes slow convergence near the optimum. The \textbf{away-step Frank--Wolfe} variant addresses this by also allowing steps \emph{away} from the worst active atom, achieving a linear rate for strongly convex objectives. The \textbf{pairwise Frank--Wolfe} variant further improves by making both towards- and away-steps in a single iteration.

\subsection{Quasi-Newton Methods: Bridging First- and Second-Order}

A natural gap in the methods studied is the class of \emph{quasi-Newton} methods, which achieve super-linear convergence without computing full Hessians. The BFGS update (Broyden--Fletcher--Goldfarb--Shanno, 1970) builds an approximation $B_k \approx H_k = \nabla^2 f(x_k)$ using only gradient differences:
\begin{equation}
  s_k = x_{k+1} - x_k, \quad y_k = \nabla f(x_{k+1}) - \nabla f(x_k), \quad
  B_{k+1} = B_k - \frac{B_k s_k s_k^T B_k}{s_k^T B_k s_k} + \frac{y_k y_k^T}{y_k^T s_k}.
\end{equation}
The update satisfies the \emph{secant equation} $B_{k+1}s_k = y_k$ (the Hessian approximation is consistent with the observed gradient change) and preserves positive definiteness when the curvature condition $s_k^T y_k > 0$ holds (guaranteed by Wolfe-condition line search). BFGS achieves a \emph{super-linear} convergence rate: $\|x_k - x^\star\| \to 0$ faster than any geometric sequence, but not as fast as Newton's quadratic rate.

\textbf{Performance prediction for our benchmarks.} For Benchmark C (Rosenbrock, $\kappa \approx 2508$, $n = 2$):
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item The full-memory BFGS stores a $2 \times 2$ Hessian approximation (only 3 scalars for a symmetric matrix) --- negligible memory overhead.
\item After $\sim 5$ iterations, $B_k$ converges to the true Hessian at $x^\star$, enabling Newton-like steps.
\item Expected final objective: $f \approx 10^{-6}$ after 50 iterations (compared to Newton's $f \approx 4.6 \times 10^{-4}$, limited by damping), as BFGS adapts its Hessian approximation to the local curvature more gradually and does not require explicit damping.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{L-BFGS} (limited-memory BFGS) stores only the last $m$ pairs $(s_k, y_k)$, computing the inverse Hessian-vector product implicitly in $O(mn)$ time. With $m \approx 10$, L-BFGS is the de facto standard for unconstrained optimisation in dimensions $d \geq 1000$ and is used internally by PyTorch's \texttt{torch.optim.LBFGS}.

\subsection{The Optimisation--Generalisation Tradeoff}

This study has focused exclusively on optimisation performance: minimising the training objective. In machine learning contexts, a subtlety arises: \emph{better optimisation does not always mean better generalisation}. Several observations from the experiments are relevant:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{SGD's noise as regularisation.} Mini-batch SGD with large batches tends to find sharper minima (higher loss curvature, poor generalisation) than small batches \cite{keskar2017}. The gradient noise implicitly biases SGD towards flat minima with better generalisation. This is why production ML training rarely uses full-batch gradient descent or Newton despite their faster convergence.

\item \textbf{Adam's implicit regularisation.} Adam's normalisation by $\sqrt{v_t}$ performs effective gradient clipping, preventing large gradient updates in noisy directions. This is beneficial for deep networks with vanishing/exploding gradients, independently of optimisation speed.

\item \textbf{Newton and overfitting.} Newton's one-step convergence on Benchmark A gives the exact OLS solution $\hat\theta = (X^TX)^{-1}X^Ty$. In high dimensions ($d > n$), $X^TX$ is singular and the exact solution is the minimum-norm solution, which may overfit. Regularised Newton (ridge regression: $H_{\rm reg} = X^TX + \lambda I$) bridges Newton's speed with regularisation.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Gradient Descent and Polyak Step Size}]
def gradient_descent(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        x = x - alpha * grad_fn(x)
        x_hist.append(x.copy())
        f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)

def polyak_step(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, f_star, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        alpha_k = (loss_fn(x) - f_star) / (np.dot(g, g) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(alpha_k)
        x = x - alpha_k * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy()); f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Adagrad and RMSprop}]
def adagrad(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha0, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float); G = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x); G += g**2
        eff_alpha = alpha0 / (np.sqrt(G) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(np.mean(eff_alpha))
        x = x - eff_alpha * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy()); f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)

def rmsprop(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha0, beta, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float); v = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist, alpha_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        v = beta * v + (1 - beta) * g**2
        eff_alpha = alpha0 / (np.sqrt(v) + eps)
        alpha_hist.append(np.mean(eff_alpha))
        x = x - eff_alpha * g
        x_hist.append(x.copy()); f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(alpha_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Heavy Ball, Nesterov Momentum, and Adam}]
def heavy_ball(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float); z = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x); z = beta * z + alpha * g; x = x - z
        x_hist.append(x.copy()); f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)

def nesterov_momentum(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta_max, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float); z = np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for k in range(1, n_iters + 1):
        beta_k = min((k - 1) / (k + 2), beta_max)
        lookahead = x + beta_k * z
        g = grad_fn(lookahead)
        z = beta_k * z - alpha * g; x = x + z
        x_hist.append(x.copy()); f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)

def adam_optimiser(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, beta1, beta2, eps, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    m_vec, v_vec = np.zeros_like(x), np.zeros_like(x)
    x_hist, f_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)]
    for t in range(1, n_iters + 1):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        m_vec = beta1 * m_vec + (1 - beta1) * g
        v_vec = beta2 * v_vec + (1 - beta2) * g**2
        m_hat = m_vec / (1 - beta1**t); v_hat = v_vec / (1 - beta2**t)
        x = x - alpha * m_hat / (np.sqrt(v_hat) + eps)
        x_hist.append(x.copy()); f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Newton's Method and Frank--Wolfe}]
def newtons_method(grad_fn, hess_fn, loss_fn, x0, alpha, n_iters, damping=1e-8):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, update_norms = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        H = hess_fn(x) if callable(hess_fn) else hess_fn
        H_reg = H + damping * np.eye(len(x))
        try: p = np.linalg.solve(H_reg, g)
        except np.linalg.LinAlgError: p = g
        update_norms.append(np.linalg.norm(alpha * p))
        x = x - alpha * p
        x_hist.append(x.copy()); f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(update_norms)

def frank_wolfe(grad_fn, loss_fn, x0, bounds, beta, n_iters):
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    x_hist, f_hist, z_hist = [x.copy()], [loss_fn(x)], []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(x)
        z = np.array([bounds[i][0] if g[i] > 0 else bounds[i][1]
                       for i in range(len(g))])
        z_hist.append(z.copy())
        x = beta * x + (1 - beta) * z
        x_hist.append(x.copy()); f_hist.append(loss_fn(x))
    return np.array(x_hist), np.array(f_hist), np.array(z_hist)
\end{lstlisting}
